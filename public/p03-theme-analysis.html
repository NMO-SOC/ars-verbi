Insecurity and Dissatisfaction

Jackson’s protagonists are frequently discontented. The woman in ‘The Daemon Lover’ suffers from deep-seated dissatisfaction, anxiety about her appearance and ageing, and insecurity about her desirability; she hopes marriage will let her reinvent herself. Miss Clarence in ‘The Villager’ disguises her disappointment that she never became a dancer. Elizabeth Style pins her hopes on Jim Harris. Clara Spencer escapes her grey life entirely, at the cost of her identity. Jackson suggests that these characters’ unhappiness is shaped both by society and by themselves. Social expectations about marriage, success and femininity set the standards they fail to meet, but their own fantasies of another life deepen their dissatisfaction. As one useful interpretation puts it, the protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’ intensifies her own unhappiness through her thoughts, choices and actions, while the happiness of others accentuates her dissatisfaction.

Social Judgement and Exclusion

Jackson’s communities decide who belongs. People who challenge social expectations are judged, excluded or punished: Mrs MacLane for employing Mr Jones, Mrs Walpole for her uncontrolled dog, David for failing to be the right kind of man. Insiders police the boundaries out of fear of becoming outsiders themselves: Mrs Winning abandons her friend because she is anxious about losing belonging and respectability. Useful vocabulary: conformity, gatekeepers, ostracise, marginalise, alienated, exclusionary, respectability, social status.

Unexpected Events Expose Our Struggles

A stranger arrives, a fiancé disappears, a dog kills chickens, a man on a train tells a story. In each case, the disruption exposes problems that already existed beneath the surface: Miss Clarence’s disappointment, David’s passivity, Mrs Walpole’s fear of her neighbours, the mother’s inability to control what her son learns. James Harris, who appears in story after story, embodies this idea: he enters characters’ lives unexpectedly and reveals the gap between how they see themselves and how others see them. Useful vocabulary: catalyst, disruption, destabilise, unravel, expose, vulnerability.

The Violence and Cruelty Beneath a Civilised Society

Physical violence features in only a few stories (‘The Witch’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘The Lottery’), so think of this idea as including cruelty, insensitivity, intolerance and lack of compassion. Jackson’s violence is often:

  • Arbitrary – based on chance rather than any fair reason, like the lottery’s random selection;

  • Ritualised – performed according to tradition, like the annual drawing;

  • Normalised – treated as ordinary even when it is harmful, like the neighbours’ advice in ‘The Renegade’;

  • Entrenched – so established that it is difficult to change, like the racism of ‘Flower Garden’.

Jackson repeatedly shows that the most disturbing cruelty comes from ordinary, respectable people: friendly neighbours, polite mothers, children. Children both reveal the hidden prejudices of adults and occasionally resist them, highlighting the flaws in adult values.

Conformity and Tradition

Many stories show a chain of social pressure: society establishes rules about how people should behave; adults teach children what is normal; fear of punishment pressures people to conform; people come to accept the rules without questioning them; and harmful attitudes are passed from one generation to the next. In ‘The Lottery’, even little Davy Hutchinson is given pebbles.

Key Vocabulary for the Four Ideas

Jackson’s characters are a strange and varied lot, but her stories return again and again to a few central ideas. In the longer stories most of these ideas are present; in the shorter ones it is easier to see just one or two. Reading with these ideas in mind will help you read with purpose.

Social Judgement and Exclusion

Like people in the real world, Jackson’s characters feel judged by those around them, and they judge others. Many judgements rest on societal expectations about the appropriate way to behave, some tied to the era (women as good housekeepers, men as strong providers). Most of Jackson’s protagonists break with what is ‘accepted’ in some way, and feel uneasy about not fitting in and anxious about their choices. Many feel strongly that they should go along with the unwritten rules of dominant values.

  • Social judgement: cultural, community, public, accepted, unwritten, expected, dominant, mainstream, collective; expectations, standards, conventions, behaviours, rules, pressures, values, attitudes, roles.

  • Exclusion: alienation, isolation, marginalisation, ostracism, loneliness, separation, detachment, disconnection, lack of belonging.

  • Anxiety: tension, uncertainty, dread, unease, insecurity, emotional turbulence, nervousness, agitation, concern.

Insecurity and Dissatisfaction

Many protagonists feel insecure about their life choices or identity. Even characters who seem certain daydream about alternative lives or feel unhappy about some aspect of their lives. They often express insecurity by imagining different lifestyles, and they regularly look out of windows or through doorways while doing so. Another sign of self-doubt is choosing clothes and feeling that none of their outfits, belongings or homes are right.

  • Dissatisfaction: discontent, frustration, restlessness, resentment, longing for more, lack of fulfilment, feeling trapped, disappointment, quiet despair.

  • Insecurity: uncertainty, self-doubt, self-consciousness, lack of confidence, fear of judgement, feelings of inadequacy, social awkwardness, nervousness, hesitation.

  • Struggling with identity: disconnection with self, role confusion, internal conflict, feelings of otherness, lack of self-assurance, uncertain of purpose, feelings of personal failure, yearning for validation.

Unexpected Events Expose Our Struggles

In every story there is an unexpected event, or the arrival of a stranger, that exposes the protagonist’s inner struggles. Sometimes these events seem positive and make characters feel their lives will change for the better. At other times strange people and events make characters feel more vulnerable and unsure of themselves. Occasionally they help characters see that their own lives are worth keeping. Jackson’s larger point is that surprises make us look at our own lives in a new light.

  • Unexpected events: an unforeseen development, a sudden shift in perspective, a disruption to normality, an unpredictable turn of events, something at odds with convention, an event which is psychologically unsettling, an unprecedented change.

  • Unexpected encounters: a crossing of paths, a fleeting exchange, an accidental meeting, a destabilising social encounter, a moment of unexpected human connection, a symbolic encounter with otherness, being drawn into a stranger’s orbit.

The Violence Beneath a Civilised Society

Beneath the thin veneer of polite society lie violent thoughts and, often, violent actions. Sometimes violence is treated as an ordinary, expected part of life, such as putting down a dog that kills chickens. At other times it is disturbing and frightening, like a stranger telling a child a fantasy about killing his sister. Even stories without physical violence have characters expressing violent emotions or bullying others. Jackson is not condoning violence; she exposes it as a fundamental part of being human.

  • Violent thoughts and actions: aggressive impulses, destructive behaviour, hostile actions, emotional volatility, eruptions of anger, acts of hostility, simmering resentment, sinister thoughts, impulsive acts of emotional aggression, hostility driven by insecurity, a surprising capacity for brutality.

  • Beneath a civilised veneer: under the surface of respectability, behind a facade of civility, beneath an appearance of order, concealed by social refinement, hidden beneath cultivated manners, under a mask of normality, concealed by respectability, under the guise of civility, beneath the rituals of polite society, hidden behind outward conformity, obscured by the cultivated image of control.
