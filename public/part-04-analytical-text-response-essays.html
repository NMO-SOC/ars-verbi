Part 04

Analytical Text Response Essays

Planning

Deconstructing the Prompt

When first planning your essay, you must directly respond to the prompt that is presented to you, and consider the key authorial messages that could help you respond with insight. This involves three important steps:

  • Find the key concepts. This will most likely correlate with the key concepts you have studied throughout the unit, you should also make sure to identify synonyms of these concepts too!

  • Find any instructional verbs. These are words that instruct you to answer the prompt in a certain manner, in English the main verbs to look out for is discuss, do you agree, to what extent or how.

  • Identify any expectations/limitations. These ore words that limit what you con include in your essays and set on expectation on what you should be discussing.

Example prompt: ‘The dream sequences in Rainbow’s End are the characters’ only escape from the depressing reality of life.’ Do you agree? This is a very straightforward prompt—you need to demonstrate a multiple-sided response to what Harrison perceives as the role of dreams and aspirations in her characters’ lives. There are no limits to what should be discussed here, but you are expected to focus your discussion on the ideas of dreams, escape, and reality as they relate to the values, identities, and choices of Nan Dear, Gladys, and Dolly. Consider the historical context of 1950s assimilationist Australia, racism, class, and the characters’ evolving sense of agency throughout the play.

Contention

Now that you understand what the prompt is asking, we can begin to think about what our interpretation of Harrison’s message is. Let’s consider the first prompt again: ‘The dream sequences in Rainbow’s End are the characters’ only escape from the depressing reality of life.’ Do you agree? We can approach this in multiple ways. Here are three simplistic interpretations (agree, disagree, somewhere in between):

  • Harrison suggests that dreams offer the characters comfort, but they cannot change the reality of their circumstances.

  • Harrison implies that dreams are the characters’ only refuge from a society that denies them dignity and opportunity.

  • Harrison demonstrates that it is action, not dreaming, that ultimately allows the characters to escape their circumstances.

However, these interpretations are quite general and lack specificity. They don’t tell the reader exactly what you are arguing. Let’s improve them with more precise language and deeper knowledge of the text:

  • Harrison portrays dreams as both sustaining and limiting, suggesting that Gladys’s fantasies of meeting the Queen preserve her dignity while simultaneously revealing how deeply colonial ideals of acceptance have been internalised.

  • Harrison implies that, for a family denied housing, education, and respect, the imagination becomes a vital site of survival and self-worth in a society that offers no material escape.

  • Harrison critiques passive dreaming by charting Gladys’s transformation from daydreamer to activist, suggesting that genuine escape from injustice comes not from fantasy but from finding one’s voice and demanding change.

These improved interpretations use specific language, engage with the complexity of the characters’ motivations, and demonstrate a sophisticated understanding of the play’s ideas.

Extending Sentences - Connectives

To create a ‘sophisticated’ idea you need to avoid single ideas, all of the contentions above are singular and lack connections to other ideas. We can connect them together using three simple connectives (and their advanced synonyms!)

Useful Conjunctions and Their Purposes
Conjunction Purpose Synonyms
Because This conjunction explains why the information you have stated is true, demonstrating greater understanding of the author’s purpose. As, as a result of, considering, being, for, through, seeing as.
But This conjunction adds contradictory information, allowing for greater debate. However, nevertheless, yet, despite, even so, nonetheless, except.
So This conjunction allows for inferencing the cause and effect of the statement, which demonstrates greater thinking and ideas about the text. Therefore, consequently, accordingly, leading to.

Specificity and Relevancy

Examiners punish vague or general statements, especially those that lack clear links to the topic or the author’s message. For example: By emphasising the importance of dreams in the play, Harrison shows that they matter to Gladys. This is not technically incorrect—but it is far too general and superficial. It doesn’t demonstrate insightful analysis, nor does it show a clear understanding of Harrison’s authorial intent. This kind of vague sentence would not receive full marks. Here’s an improved version: By emphasising Gladys’s fantasies of royal recognition, Harrison portrays how dreams function not as an escape from reality, but as a fragile shield of dignity for those denied respect and opportunity by an assimilationist society. This is better. It is clear, specific, well-structured, and shows awareness of both the theme (dreams vs reality), character motivation, and authorial message. It also directly links to the contention: that dreams sustain the characters, even in circumstances that offer no material escape. The issue you will face is that there is only so much you can discuss in your paragraph from here, as you have only examined one idea—dreams sustain dignity. To fix this, lets’ go back to an old friend:

Because, But, So - Make a Complex Sentence!

Too often, students fail to complete long paragraphs because they are stuck repeating themselves or have run out of things to say about their topic they introduced. Therefore, if we have two ideas in our topic sentence, we can ‘hinge’ these sentences together which demonstrate complex understanding, writing and structure - all of them are key parts of the rubric you are judged on! Here’s an example:

By emphasising Gladys’s daydreams of royal recognition, Harrison conveys how, for a family denied dignity and opportunity, the imagination becomes a vital refuge from an unjust reality—ultimately suggesting that dreams alone cannot deliver change, and that true escape demands the courage to speak and act.

Now, you have two linked ideas:

  • Dreams preserve dignity and hope for the characters in an unjust society.

  • Dreaming alone, without action, cannot deliver genuine change.

This provides a clear structure for an entire paragraph and sets you up to use a hinge sentence later to develop further layers—e.g., how this ‘dreaming” can also reveal internalised colonial ideals or delay confrontation with injustice. By planning your paragraph in this way, you’ll ensure:

  • Specific and sophisticated analysis

  • Strong authorial intent

  • Complex understanding of the characters’ motivations and Harrison’s critique of assimilationist policies in 1950s Australia

The Dynamics of a Text

The study design asks you to analyse the dynamics of a text. These are lenses for knowing your text so well that any topic can be answered. They do not sit in separate boxes: a character’s motivation usually connects to a tension, a setting or a point of view. Make one page of notes for each lens, with evidence.

1. Character Motivations

Ask why each character does what they do. Is the drive emotional (fear, anger, joy), moral (duty, loyalty, compassion), self-image (wanting to seem good or honourable), or cultural, religious and social (beliefs strong enough to compel action)? Note where motivation changes: a character driven by money early may be driven by conscience later.

One quotation can support several readings. When Max says of Norma, “I made her a star. I directed all of her early films. I was her first husband”, his loyalty could be read as responsibility, as hope of returning to directing, as lingering love, or as the identity the role gives him. Choose the reading the topic needs, and use the words of the quote as your evidence for it.

For Rainbow’s End, try the same exercise with Gladys at the Shire meeting, Nan Dear’s refusal to leave the humpy, or Errol’s persistence despite his father.

2. Tensions in Relationships

Tension is the friction between two characters: betrayal, misunderstanding, conflicting desires, secrecy, trauma, gender expectations, social order. Look at the language a character uses about the other. Line up the key relationships and note what rubs them against each other and whether it is resolved.

3. The Function of Settings

Naming the setting in your introduction earns little. Explaining what a setting does earns a great deal. Ask: of all places, why this one? Does it mirror a character’s mood, or contrast with it to raise tension? Does it confine characters or free them? Does it give someone authority or take it away? Useful phrasing: “the setting functions as a physical manifestation of”, “the confined setting reinforces”, “the setting intensifies the conflict between”.

For Sunset Boulevard, the mansion functions as a museum of Norma’s past and a cage for Joe. For Rainbow’s End, the humpy functions as self-determination and the “featureless” Rumbalara houses as assimilation made concrete.

4. The Complexities of the Plot

Map the plot on one large page. Is it linear or non-linear? Are there flashbacks, flash-forwards, frame narratives, dream sequences, circular endings? Then ask why the author chose this sequence. A structural choice (Joe narrating from the pool; Gladys’s dreams cutting into the action) is evidence you can use.

5. The Role of Point of View

Whose voice do we hear? Whose perspective are we denied? Is the narrator reliable, and how do we know? Do characters see the same person differently? Does the audience know things characters do not (dramatic irony)? Useful phrasing: “by aligning the audience with”, “the limited perspective heightens”, “from Nan Dear’s point of view, however”.

Beyond the Dynamics

The study design also names: ideas, concerns and conflicts (not “themes”: turn a theme into the concerns and conflicts it raises); structures and language features (symbolism, dramatic irony, parallel plots, repetition, stage directions, film techniques), always explained for how and why; and the historical, social and cultural values of the world of the text, used as evidence in body paragraphs rather than only as a line in the introduction.

Connect the Lenses

Once each lens has its own page, link them. A mind map or colour coding works well: mark the quotes that serve more than one lens (a line that reveals a motivation and uses a key language feature), because those are the most flexible pieces of evidence on exam day. Some lenses will matter more for your text than others; that is expected.

Five Ways to Substantiate

No assessor counts types of evidence. But a response that draws on only quotations shows a narrower range than one that also uses characters, structure, plot, setting, language features and context. Quality beats quantity: each piece of evidence must be explained, not just included.

Analytical Text Response Structure

Topics

There are three types of questions you may receive on a SAC or exam. These are the ‘Discuss’, ‘To what extent’ and ‘Do you agree?’ statements. These are all tackled in a slightly varying manner.

  • Discuss : This is probably the most common type of prompt that you will encounter. When dealing with such prompts, it is important that you discuss as many aspects of it as possible. One way to improve your discussion here is to include a paragraph that rebuts the prompt, like what you do in your persuasive essays. However, since it is a discuss prompt, you do not need to make the two sides of your argument extremely distinct.

  • To what extent: These topics require you to make a decision. Never say ‘it is to some extent’ in your contention. You really need to assign a portion of something. The best way to do this is to start with subordinating conjunctions like ‘whilst” or ‘although”. To What Extent questions rarely want you to agree or disagree - they want you to explore a shade of grey.

  • How: The ‘How’ topics usually focus on the construction of a text - your evidence should contain metalanguage like ‘characterisation’ for instance.

Introduction

The introduction is the first piece of your writing that will be examined. For this reason, it should be clear, direct and show your understanding of the topic. The introduction is there to show your examiner how you wish to respond to the set topic. If your topic contains a quote, ensure that you mention it somewhere in your introduction to show you are addressing the topic.

Body Paragraphs

The body paragraphs form majority of your essay. The body paragraphs are the main reasons that the contention your provided is true. Your body paragraphs need to be able to demonstrate your understanding of the text and the fluency of your language. You should ensure that every sentence (and thus every word) brings something towards your argument.

Structure

Below is the structure for a body paragraph using ‘Th.E. S.A.M.L.’ Method. This method of writing body paragraphs was developed in order to maximise possible marks. Only the ‘TH’ and ‘L’ elements of this structure need to be in the order given.

Th
Thematic-based topic sentence

Your topic sentence needs to be based upon a theme in the text, not a character.

✗ ‘Norma Desmond’s obsession with herself ultimately leads to her downfall.’
✓ ‘Obsession with self is what ultimately leads to the downfall of characters.’
E
Evidence

Quotes and scenes from the text that support the theme.

S
Symbols

Symbols that relate to this theme — including historical and social context, and setting.

A
Authorial intention

What was the author intending?

M
Metalanguage

Discuss the metalanguage the author uses around this theme.

L
Linking sentence

Link back to your contention.

Only the Th and L elements need to appear in this order — the middle elements can be woven through the paragraph.

Basic Example ‘Th.E S.A.M’

‘Obsession with one’s self is what ultimately leads to the downfall of characters. This can be seen with Norma exclaiming ‘who else?’ when Joe questions her about her script. The script itself is also a representation of Norma’s obsession as she has written several hundred pages where she stars in every scene. Wilder uses this to demonstrate that Norma is unable to move on from anything besides herself. The slow camera pan whenever the script is shown foreshadows Norma’s impending doom which links is inherently linked to her self-absorption. In this way, obsession with self ultimately leads to the downfall of several characters in Sunset Boulevard.’

Hinge Sentences

In your topic sentence, you can introduce two related ideas. This creates room for a more complex, insightful paragraph. But remember: when you shift focus from the first idea to the second, you must signal this change to the reader clearly. This is where the hinge sentence comes in. A hinge sentence acts like a bridge—it acknowledges what you’ve already explored, then transitions to the second idea you introduced in your topic sentence. Here’s how that works:

By emphasising Gladys’s daydreams of royal recognition, Harrison conveys how, for a family denied dignity and opportunity, the imagination becomes a vital refuge from an unjust reality—ultimately suggesting that dreams alone cannot deliver change, and that true escape demands the courage to speak and act.

Evidence + Analysis of First Idea

In the dream sequences where Gladys imagines being received by the Queen, Harrison frames fantasy not as idle escapism but as a shield of dignity for a woman excluded from the “decent” society she longs to join. Yet Nan Dear’s blunt dismissal—“And what bloody good would that do? Daydreams!” (p. 154)—exposes the limits of this refuge, highlighting how dreaming leaves the family’s material circumstances untouched.

Hinge Sentence (transitioning to Second idea)

However, while Gladys’s dreams preserve her sense of self-worth, Harrison also reveals that fantasy alone cannot deliver her family from injustice.

Analysis of Second Idea

Gladys’s transformation culminates in her address to the Rodney Shire Council, where she demands “proper schooling … The white people too — they need to be educated about us, and our ways” (p. 197). Harrison complicates the notion of escape by showing that it is voice and action, not fantasy, that begin to change the family’s circumstances, suggesting that dreams are only powerful when they inspire agency. By using a hinge sentence, you show your reader that you’re not just stacking ideas but developing an argument, deepening your interpretation, and showing the author’s nuanced message.

Conclusion

If you think of a conclusion as less of summarising everything, and more of combining all of these conflicting ideas and messages into one overall conclusive point. We can do this by using the following structure.

Structure

The structure for a conclusion can follow the same structure as an introduction. However, instead of introducing the text, you are concluding your discussion. Here is an alternative method: Topic:

‘The dream sequences in Rainbow’s End are the characters’ only escape from the depressing reality of life.’ Do you agree?

Conclusion Structure for Analytical Essay Writing
Purpose in Conclusion Example Sentence
Overview of how the whole text demonstrates the key concepts in the essay topic Fundamentally, Rainbow’s End explores the tension between dreams and reality, particularly how fantasy can offer dignity, but not deliverance, to those denied opportunity by an unjust society.
Summary of the complications of this topic Throughout the play, Harrison complicates the notion of escape by showing that while Gladys’s daydreams preserve her sense of self-worth, they leave her family’s circumstances unchanged, challenging the romanticisation of hope without action.
Focus on the key authorial message in conclusive language Therefore, Harrison critiques a society that forces its most marginalised to retreat into fantasy, asserting that genuine change lies not in dreaming of acceptance but in finding the voice to demand it, as Gladys does before the Shire Council.
