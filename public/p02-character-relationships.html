Mrs Appleyard and Sara Waybourne

The film’s most painful relationship is one of power. Mrs Appleyard is upright, ornately dressed and usually framed above other characters or behind her desk; Sara is small, plain, stooped and often seated or diminished in the frame. Sara loves poetry and feeling; Mrs Appleyard values rules and fees. As the college comes under pressure, Mrs Appleyard turns her frustration on Sara, the one person with no family or money to protect her.

Key Quotes

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘There are several sets of tuition fees impossibly overdue... Sara Waybourne, for instance.’

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘We shall have to make other arrangements.’

  • Mrs Appleyard: Appleyard College is not a ‘charitable institution’.

Key Ideas

  • Class and social hierarchy

  • Authority and cruelty

  • Reason versus emotion

  • Victim or agent of her own fate?

Sara Waybourne: Victim of Fate or Author of Her Own Demise?

One of the most significant minor characters, Sara is Miranda’s devoted admirer. We first see her watching Miranda wash her face and presenting her with a Valentine’s card. Her outsider status is established early: she is prevented from going to the picnic and must stay behind to learn poetry, but she thinks her own poetry is better than the classic and refuses to learn it. Mrs Appleyard nags her about her posture throughout, a good symbol of how Sara doesn’t, or won’t, fit in. Late in the film we learn that her guardian has not been paying her fees. Her story is ‘doomed’, as Irma predicts. Sara is contradictory. She stands up to Mrs Appleyard, and it is easy to cheer for her. But in Edwardian times orphans were near the bottom of the class ladder, and a girl like Sara could easily have ended up closer to Minnie the maid’s position, like her brother Albert. She also has a comfortable life for an orphan, lounging about a privileged boarding school writing poetry. Yet she is bullied by Mrs Appleyard, and Miss Lumley ties her to a wall to ‘improve’ her posture.

One side of Sara’s story On the other hand
Miranda: ‘Someday, Sara, you shall have to come home with me to the station... meet my sweet, funny family for yourself.’ Sara says Miranda had many ‘secrets... she knew she wouldn’t come back’.
Miranda: ‘You must learn to love someone else, apart from me.’ Sara shakes her head at this advice, but she has a brother she apparently doesn’t keep in touch with.
Miss Lumley reminds Sara that she is ‘not to go on the picnic’. But Miss Lumley is sympathetic, because she is not allowed to go on the picnic either.
Mrs Appleyard: ‘There are several sets of tuition fees impossibly overdue... Sara Waybourne, for instance.’ Mrs Appleyard: ‘This is not a charitable institution.’
Sara shakes her head when Mrs Appleyard says, ‘We shall have to make other arrangements.’ Sara grew up in an orphanage with her brother Albert, so she must have some understanding of her position in society.
Sara tells Minnie that she had her head shaved in the orphanage for telling the matron she wanted to be a ‘circus rider’. Sara bit the matron’s arm until it bled.
Miss Lumley ties Sara to the gymnasium wall to ‘cure her terrible stooping’. Mademoiselle has the courage to stand up to Mrs Appleyard, but Miss Lumley is too afraid (until she finally resigns).
Just before Sara dies, Weir focuses on her photograph of Miranda, suggesting that Sara is all alone. Albert dreams of his ‘kid sister’ on the night of her death.
The head gardener finds Sara’s body in the greenhouse. Mrs Appleyard claims that Sara’s guardian took her away.

Question to answer: Is Sara a victim of fate, or does she bring about her own demise?

Control and the Natural World: a Spectrum

Sara’s surname is symbolic: ‘Waybourne’ sounds like ‘wayward’, or contrary. Sara does not fit the rigid hierarchy of Appleyard College and is the complete antithesis of Mrs Appleyard. Try placing the characters (Sara, Mrs Appleyard, Miranda, Miss McCraw, Michael, Albert, Mademoiselle, Miss Lumley, Irma) on a spectrum from control (logic and hierarchy) to untamed forces (nature and mystery), and label each with a phrase:

  • Towards control: systematic regulation, a desire for logic and reason, the established conventions of social order, the pursuit of understanding and certainty, the acceptance of social conventions, submission to established systems, the security of rationality and an ordered world, a belief in the authority of social institutions, an attempt to create meaning through systems, the triumph of structure over the natural world, a fear of the unknown.

  • Towards untamed forces: the wildness of nature, untamed forces, elemental forces, the enigma of life, challenges to established order, the unpredictability of the natural world, primal forces, the fragility of human attempts to impose order, the uncertainty of nature, the raw energy of the natural world, the mystery of nature.

Miranda and Sara

Sara adores Miranda, who is kind to her and seems to offer belonging. Miranda’s gentle advice that Sara ‘must learn to love someone else’ reads, in hindsight, like a farewell. With Miranda gone, Sara is completely alone; just before Sara’s death, Weir focuses on her photograph of Miranda.

Key Quotes

  • Miranda: ‘Someday, Sara, you shall have to come home with me to the station... meet my sweet, funny family for yourself.’

  • Miranda: ‘You must learn to love someone else, apart from me.’

  • Sara: Miranda had many ‘secrets... she knew she wouldn’t come back.’

Key Ideas

  • Love and isolation

  • Foreshadowing

  • The cost of the disappearance for those left behind

Michael and Albert

Michael is a privileged young Englishman; Albert is the Fitzhuberts’ groom, raised in an orphanage. At the picnic Albert makes crude remarks about the girls while Michael watches them in silence. The search draws them together: Albert rescues Michael from the Rock, and their conversations grow into a genuine friendship that ignores the social hierarchy around them. In their final scene Albert tells Michael about his dream of his ‘kid sister’, a connection to Sara that neither fully understands.

Key Quotes

  • Albert: If ‘the bloody cop and the bloody... tracker and the bloody dog can’t find ‘em, well, no one bloody can.’

  • Michael: The girls ‘may be out there dying of thirst.’

  • Michael: ‘I wake up every night in a cold sweat. Just wondering if they’re still alive.’

Key Ideas

  • Friendship across class

  • Acceptance (Albert) versus the search for understanding (Michael)

  • Instinct and intuition

An Evolving Relationship

Through Michael and Albert’s friendship, Weir suggests that the natural world transcends the rigid class divisions of society. Their shared experiences in nature transform both men, while also leaving them disoriented and stripped of certainty. Theirs is the only relationship that genuinely evolves through the film: as they become immersed in the mystery, their friendship mirrors nature’s ability to transcend the social divisions that once separated them.

Beginning Middle End
Although Albert belongs to a different class, he is friendly and open towards Michael at the picnic, offering to share a drink. Michael returns the friendliness. Albert comments candidly on the girls’ appearance as they cross the stream. When Michael calls him ‘crude’, Albert replies, ‘I say the crude things. You just think ‘em.’ Albert believes the girls must be dead, but agrees to help Michael search. He accepts that Michael wants to stay on the Rock overnight and lies to Michael’s uncle for him. Albert finds Michael the next day, recognises the importance of the torn fabric in his hand, then searches the Rock and finds Irma. In Michael’s final scene he shares his dreams with Albert: he can’t help thinking about the Rock; ‘It comes back at night in dreams.’ Albert responds with his own ‘funny dream’, in which he sees his sister Sara, accompanied by the powerful smell of pansies.

Sentence starters: Rather than reinforcing social hierarchy, Michael and Albert’s relationship...; Unlike the rigid social world they inhabit...; Against the backdrop of a transcendent natural world...; In contrast to the strict social hierarchies of Edwardian society...; Eschewing the social mores of the time... + becomes more..., resists..., goes beyond..., evolves from... to..., surpasses..., overcomes..., redefines... Comparative statements to agree, disagree or partly agree with:

  • Throughout the film, Albert is more open to new experiences than Michael.

  • Albert’s connection to nature is instinctive, whereas Michael’s must be learned.

  • Michael learns more from Albert than Albert learns from Michael.

  • Michael’s search for the girls is ultimately a search for himself, while Albert remains secure in his identity.

  • Albert embodies acceptance, while Michael embodies the search for understanding.

Dreams

At the end of the film both men have dreams. Michael’s is about the Rock; Albert’s is about his sister Sara, which seems random, because for most of the film we did not know they were related. Now, just after Sara has died, Albert dreams about her. Possible readings: for Weir, dreams are symbolic of the mysterious powers of nature; because dreams are disconnected from the rational mind, they symbolise a natural world beyond our control; people and nature are connected in ways that transcend logical thought, and dreams symbolise these connections.

Albert and Sara

Albert and Sara are brother and sister, separated in the orphanage. The film never brings them together: Albert does not know his sister is at the college a few miles away, and Sara does not keep in touch with him. On the night of Sara’s death, Albert dreams that she visits him to say goodbye.

Key Ideas

  • Class and lost opportunity

  • Mystery and the supernatural

  • Dramatic irony

Mrs Appleyard and Miss McCraw

Mrs Appleyard relies on Miss McCraw as the embodiment of reason and order. That the most rational person in the film is ‘spirited away’ shakes Mrs Appleyard’s belief that the world can be controlled.

Key Quotes

  • Mrs Appleyard: She had come to ‘depend so much on Greta McCraw’ and her ‘masculine intellect’.

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘How could she allow herself to be spirited away?’

Key Ideas

  • The limits of reason

  • Dependence and control

Mademoiselle and Miss Lumley

The two junior teachers are visual opposites: Mademoiselle wears lace, pearls and softly waving hair, while Miss Lumley wears plaid, spectacles and muddy colours. Mademoiselle goes to the picnic; Miss Lumley, like Sara, is left behind. Mademoiselle has the courage to stand up to Mrs Appleyard and stays to the end; Miss Lumley is too afraid until she finally resigns.

Key Quotes

  • Miss Lumley: Irma’s discovery is ‘wonderful news’.

  • Mademoiselle: Irma is ‘prettier than ever’.

Key Ideas

  • Appearance versus reality

  • Fear of authority

  • Curiosity and the need for answers

Appearances Are Deceiving

In many ways the two women are presented as opposites, and it is easy to typecast them: Mademoiselle is glamorous, French and sympathetic to the girls; Miss Lumley defers to Mrs Appleyard, is unfashionable, wears spectacles and is a little afraid of the girls. But Weir makes both more complicated than they appear. Costume: Mademoiselle wears lace blouses, frills and bows, pearls, gently waving hair, lace gloves and flowers and ribbons on her hats; Miss Lumley wears plaid, simple printed blouses, spectacles, muddy colours and mousy hair. Vocabulary: elegant, graceful, chic, romantic, charming, tastefully adorned, embellished, innocent (Mademoiselle); dowdy, prim, sensible, muted, utilitarian, respectable (Miss Lumley). Phrases for analysis: emotionally perceptive, harmonious with nature, in touch with innocence and girlhood, connected with the natural world, graceful and nurturing, dreamlike and ethereal; well organised and disciplined, strict and conservative, accepting of her role in the social hierarchy, emotionally restrained, respectful of societal conventions, rule-bound and dutiful.

Miss Lumley Mademoiselle
Beginning Is aligned with Sara, because neither is allowed to go to the picnic. Goes to the picnic. Compares Miranda to a work of art.
Middle Tells Mrs Appleyard that it is ‘wonderful news’ that Irma is found; is clearly frightened when chastised; believes the other girls will be found. What motivates them? Statements to weigh up: Miss Lumley is motivated by a desire to obey her headmistress and do a good job; Mademoiselle wants to be liked and admired by the students; while Mademoiselle appears to want the best for the students, she is ultimately driven by her own interests; Miss Lumley is driven by fear of Mrs Appleyard’s authority, although she can understand that some of her behaviour is wrong; Miss Lumley would like to appear rational and dutiful but is actually driven by emotions and fear. Topic sentence to develop: Throughout the film, Weir demonstrates that people often act according to their own emotional needs, even when they appear to be acting rationally or professionally.

Irma and the Other Girls

When Irma returns, the girls who once envied and admired her surround her in the gymnasium demanding ‘Tell us, Irma!’. Her vivid red dress sets her apart from their white uniforms. The scene turns into a frightening display of mob behaviour, showing how quickly civilised behaviour collapses when people are desperate for answers.

Key Ideas

  • Mass hysteria and mob mentality

  • The desire for certainty

  • The collapse of civility
