Mrs Appleyard and Sara Waybourne

The film’s most painful relationship is one of power. Mrs Appleyard is upright, ornately dressed and usually framed above other characters or behind her desk; Sara is small, plain, stooped and often seated or diminished in the frame. Sara loves poetry and feeling; Mrs Appleyard values rules and fees. As the college comes under pressure, Mrs Appleyard turns her frustration on Sara, the one person with no family or money to protect her.

Key Quotes

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘There are several sets of tuition fees impossibly overdue... Sara Waybourne, for instance.’

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘We shall have to make other arrangements.’

  • Mrs Appleyard: Appleyard College is not a ‘charitable institution’.

Key Ideas

  • Class and social hierarchy

  • Authority and cruelty

  • Reason versus emotion

  • Victim or agent of her own fate?

Sara Waybourne: Victim of Fate or Author of Her Own Demise?

Sara is only a minor character, but she matters enormously. She adores Miranda: early on we see her watching Miranda wash and giving her a Valentine’s card. From the start she is set apart. She is kept back from the picnic to learn a poem, which she refuses to do because she thinks her own poetry is better. Mrs Appleyard keeps correcting her posture, an apt image of a girl who cannot, or will not, be made to fit. Later we learn that her guardian has stopped paying her fees. Irma calls her ‘doomed’, and the film bears this out. Sara is hard to pin down. Her defiance of Mrs Appleyard makes her easy to sympathise with, yet the film also shows how precarious her position is. As an orphan in 1900 she sits near the bottom of the social order: her brother Albert works as a groom, and without a guardian’s money she could just as easily be working beside Minnie in the kitchen. For now she lives among the daughters of wealthy families, reading and writing poems, but that comfort depends entirely on fees that are not being paid. Meanwhile she is singled out by Mrs Appleyard and strapped to the gymnasium wall by Miss Lumley.

One side of Sara’s story On the other hand
Miranda: ‘Someday, Sara, you shall have to come home with me to the station... meet my sweet, funny family for yourself.’ Sara says Miranda had many ‘secrets... she knew she wouldn’t come back’.
Miranda: ‘You must learn to love someone else, apart from me.’ Sara shakes her head at this advice, but she has a brother she apparently doesn’t keep in touch with.
Miss Lumley reminds Sara that she is ‘not to go on the picnic’. But Miss Lumley is sympathetic, because she is not allowed to go on the picnic either.
Mrs Appleyard: ‘There are several sets of tuition fees impossibly overdue... Sara Waybourne, for instance.’ Mrs Appleyard: ‘This is not a charitable institution.’
Sara shakes her head when Mrs Appleyard says, ‘We shall have to make other arrangements.’ Sara was raised in an orphanage alongside Albert, so she would know exactly what being sent back would mean.
Sara tells Minnie she had her head shaved at the orphanage for telling the matron she wanted to be a ‘circus rider’. She also bit the matron’s arm hard enough to draw blood.
Miss Lumley straps Sara to the gymnasium wall to ‘cure her terrible stooping’. Mademoiselle is willing to confront Mrs Appleyard; Miss Lumley stays silent until she resigns.
Just before Sara dies, Weir focuses on her photograph of Miranda, suggesting that Sara is all alone. Albert dreams of his ‘kid sister’ on the night of her death.
The head gardener finds Sara’s body in the greenhouse. Mrs Appleyard claims that Sara’s guardian took her away.

Question to answer: Does Sara’s fate come from outside her, or does she help bring it about?

Control and the Natural World: a Spectrum

Sara’s surname is symbolic: ‘Waybourne’ sounds like ‘wayward’, or contrary. Sara does not fit the rigid hierarchy of Appleyard College and is the complete antithesis of Mrs Appleyard. Try placing the characters (Sara, Mrs Appleyard, Miranda, Miss McCraw, Michael, Albert, Mademoiselle, Miss Lumley, Irma) on a spectrum from control (logic and hierarchy) to untamed forces (nature and mystery), and label each with a phrase:

  • Towards control: faith in rules, reliance on logic, deference to authority, a need for explanation, comfort in routine, respect for rank, the urge to measure and record, anxiety about the unknown, belief that discipline makes people safe, loyalty to the institution.

  • Towards untamed forces: the pull of the bush, instinct over reason, openness to mystery, indifference to rank, a dreamlike or spiritual sense of the world, acceptance of the unexplained, attraction to the wild, freedom from convention, trust in intuition.

Miranda and Sara

Sara adores Miranda, who is kind to her and seems to offer belonging. Miranda’s gentle advice that Sara ‘must learn to love someone else’ reads, in hindsight, like a farewell. With Miranda gone, Sara is completely alone; just before Sara’s death, Weir focuses on her photograph of Miranda.

Key Quotes

  • Miranda: ‘Someday, Sara, you shall have to come home with me to the station... meet my sweet, funny family for yourself.’

  • Miranda: ‘You must learn to love someone else, apart from me.’

  • Sara: Miranda had many ‘secrets... she knew she wouldn’t come back.’

Key Ideas

  • Love and isolation

  • Foreshadowing

  • The cost of the disappearance for those left behind

Michael and Albert

Michael is a privileged young Englishman; Albert is the Fitzhuberts’ groom, raised in an orphanage. At the picnic Albert makes crude remarks about the girls while Michael watches them in silence. The search draws them together: Albert rescues Michael from the Rock, and their conversations grow into a genuine friendship that ignores the social hierarchy around them. In their final scene Albert tells Michael about his dream of his ‘kid sister’, a connection to Sara that neither fully understands.

Key Quotes

  • Albert: If ‘the bloody cop and the bloody... tracker and the bloody dog can’t find ‘em, well, no one bloody can.’

  • Michael: The girls ‘may be out there dying of thirst.’

  • Michael: ‘I wake up every night in a cold sweat. Just wondering if they’re still alive.’

Key Ideas

  • Friendship across class

  • Acceptance (Albert) versus the search for understanding (Michael)

  • Instinct and intuition

An Evolving Relationship

Michael and Albert begin the film on opposite sides of a strict class line: the nephew of a colonel and his uncle’s groom. The search for the girls pulls them into the same experience, and by the end they talk as equals. Weir uses their friendship to suggest that the Rock does not recognise rank. It also leaves both men unsettled: Michael is haunted, and Albert’s confident common sense gives way to a dream he cannot explain. Of all the relationships in the film, theirs is the one that changes most.

Beginning Middle End
At the picnic Albert treats Michael as a mate rather than a master, passing him the bottle, and Michael responds in kind. As the girls cross the creek, Albert comments freely on their looks; when Michael calls him ‘crude’, he answers, ‘I say the crude things. You just think ‘em.’ Albert is sure the girls are dead, but he helps Michael search anyway. When Michael decides to stay on the Rock overnight, Albert covers for him with his uncle. The next day Albert finds Michael, realises the scrap of lace in his hand matters, goes back up and finds Irma. In their last scene together, Michael admits he cannot stop thinking about the Rock: ‘It comes back at night in dreams.’ Albert answers with a ‘funny dream’ of his own, in which his sister Sara appears, with a strong scent of pansies.

Sentence starters: Although they come from opposite ends of the social ladder, Michael and Albert...; As the search draws them together...; Weir uses the friendship between the nephew and the groom to...; Set against the class-bound world of the Fitzhuberts... + grows into..., crosses..., outlasts..., unsettles..., moves from... to..., challenges... Comparative statements to agree, disagree or partly agree with:

  • Albert adapts to the disappearance more easily than Michael does.

  • Albert’s practical knowledge of the bush matters more in the search than Michael’s determination.

  • The friendship changes Michael far more than it changes Albert.

  • For Michael the search becomes personal in a way it never does for Albert.

  • Albert is content to leave the mystery unsolved; Michael cannot let it go.

Dreams

Late in the film each man describes a dream. Michael’s returns him to the Rock. Albert’s brings him his sister Sara, which catches the audience off guard, because the film has only just revealed that they are brother and sister, and because Sara has just died. Weir offers no explanation. You could read the dreams as signs of a connection between people and places that logic cannot reach; as evidence that the Rock’s influence has spread beyond the picnic ground; or as Weir’s way of showing that the most important truths in the film arrive through feeling rather than reason.

Albert and Sara

Albert and Sara are brother and sister, separated in the orphanage. The film never brings them together: Albert does not know his sister is at the college a few miles away, and Sara does not keep in touch with him. On the night of Sara’s death, Albert dreams that she visits him to say goodbye.

Key Ideas

  • Class and lost opportunity

  • Mystery and the supernatural

  • Dramatic irony

Mrs Appleyard and Miss McCraw

Mrs Appleyard relies on Miss McCraw as the embodiment of reason and order. That the most rational person in the film is ‘spirited away’ shakes Mrs Appleyard’s belief that the world can be controlled.

Key Quotes

  • Mrs Appleyard: She had come to ‘depend so much on Greta McCraw’ and her ‘masculine intellect’.

  • Mrs Appleyard: ‘How could she allow herself to be spirited away?’

Key Ideas

  • The limits of reason

  • Dependence and control

Mademoiselle and Miss Lumley

Weir dresses the two junior mistresses as opposites: Mademoiselle in lace and pearls with soft, loose hair, Miss Lumley in tartan and spectacles in dull colours. Mademoiselle goes on the picnic; Miss Lumley stays behind, like Sara. Mademoiselle stands up to Mrs Appleyard and remains at the college to the end, whereas Miss Lumley stays silent until she finally resigns.

Key Quotes

  • Miss Lumley: Irma’s discovery is ‘wonderful news’.

  • Mademoiselle: Irma is ‘prettier than ever’.

Key Ideas

  • Appearance versus reality

  • Fear of authority

  • Curiosity and the need for answers

Appearances Are Deceiving

In many ways the two women are presented as opposites, and it is easy to typecast them: Mademoiselle is glamorous, French and sympathetic to the girls; Miss Lumley defers to Mrs Appleyard, is unfashionable, wears spectacles and is a little afraid of the girls. But Weir makes both more complicated than they appear. Costume: Mademoiselle is dressed in lace, pearls, soft ribbons and flowered hats, with her hair loosely waved; Miss Lumley wears tartan, plain blouses and spectacles, in drab colours. Vocabulary: refined, delicate, romantic, feminine, ornamented, warm (Mademoiselle); plain, severe, practical, subdued, dutiful, colourless (Miss Lumley). Phrases for analysis: emotionally attuned to the girls, softened by beauty and art, sympathetic to youth and feeling; bound by duty, anxious to please authority, restrained and correct, fearful of disorder.

Miss Lumley Mademoiselle
Beginning Is aligned with Sara, because neither is allowed to go to the picnic. Goes to the picnic. Compares Miranda to a work of art.
Middle Calls Irma’s return ‘wonderful news’ and is sharply rebuked; flinches under Mrs Appleyard’s anger; keeps hoping the others will be found. Questions Edith about what she saw, both in bed and back at the Rock; hands the police a piece of Miranda’s clothing for the dogs; is crushed when only Irma comes back; embraces Irma as ‘prettier than ever’, then quizzes her about her statement to the police.
End Hides behind the piano when the girls turn on Irma; insists that strapping Sara up was ‘for her own good’; resigns. Brings Irma back to class and leaves her there; loses her temper with Edith and slaps her; is still at the college at the end, keeping Mrs Appleyard company at dinner.

What motivates them? Statements to weigh up: Miss Lumley mostly wants to keep her position and avoid her headmistress’s anger; Mademoiselle cares deeply about the girls; Mademoiselle’s warmth is partly about being admired; Miss Lumley knows that some of what she is told to do is wrong but is too frightened to refuse; both women claim to act from duty when they are acting from feeling. Topic sentence to develop: Weir shows that behind the professional manner of the college’s teachers lie fear, loyalty and longing, and these, not reason, decide how they act.

Irma and the Other Girls

When Irma returns, the girls who once envied and admired her surround her in the gymnasium demanding ‘Tell us, Irma!’. Her vivid red dress sets her apart from their white uniforms. The scene turns into a frightening display of mob behaviour, showing how quickly civilised behaviour collapses when people are desperate for answers.

Key Ideas

  • Mass hysteria and mob mentality

  • The desire for certainty

  • The collapse of civility
