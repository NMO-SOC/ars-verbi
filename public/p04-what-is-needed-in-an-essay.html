What is Needed in an Essay

On this page
  • Introduction
  • Body Paragraphs
  • Conclusion

An essay is a structured piece of writing that presents an argument in response to a topic. In Section A, every essay has the same shape: an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. Each part has a set job, and the introduction and conclusion follow the same structure.

Introduction

The introduction tells the marker which text you are writing about, what you think about the topic, and how your essay will prove it. Write it in three moves, in this order.

1
Text, author and form

Name the text, its author (or director) and its form: novel, film, play, short story collection.

2
Contention

Your answer to the topic, in one clear sentence.

3
Three arguments

Outline the argument of each body paragraph, in the order they will appear.

Topic: ‘The desire for social acceptance shapes the behaviour of many of Jackson’s characters.’ Do you agree?

Model introduction

1In her 1949 short story collection The Lottery and Other Stories, Shirley Jackson exposes the quiet pressures that sit beneath ordinary American life. 2Jackson suggests that the desire for social acceptance shapes much of her characters’ behaviour, although it is often the fear of exclusion, rather than a wish to belong, that drives them. 3Her characters conform to avoid the judgement of their communities, construct false versions of themselves in order to appear successful, and, at their worst, take part in cruelty so that they are not the ones left outside.

Body Paragraphs

An essay normally has three body paragraphs, one for each argument named in your introduction and in the same order. Build each one with Th.E.S.A.M.L.

Th
Thematic-based topic sentence

Your topic sentence needs to be based upon a theme in the text, not a character.

✗ ‘Tessie Hutchinson is stoned to death by the villagers.’
✓ ‘Jackson suggests that blind loyalty to tradition allows ordinary people to commit extraordinary cruelty.’
E
Evidence

Quotes and scenes from the text that support the theme.

S
Symbols

Symbols that relate to this theme — including historical and social context, and setting.

A
Authorial intention

What was the author intending?

M
Metalanguage

Discuss the metalanguage the author uses around this theme.

L
Linking sentence

Link back to your contention.

Only the Th and L elements need to appear in this order — the middle elements can be woven through the paragraph.

Conclusion

The conclusion has the same structure as the introduction. Return to the text, author and form, restate your contention, and sum up your three arguments, this time as conclusions you have proven rather than ideas you are about to explore. Use fresh wording and do not add new evidence.

1
Text, author and form

Name the text, its author (or director) and its form: novel, film, play, short story collection.

2
Contention

Restate your contention, in fresh words.

3
Three arguments

Sum up what each body paragraph proved, in the same order.

Model conclusion

1Across The Lottery and Other Stories, Shirley Jackson presents a world in which belonging is never guaranteed. 2The need to be accepted is one of the most powerful forces in her characters’ lives, and fear of exclusion, more than a wish to belong, is what drives them. 3They conform to escape judgement, invent more impressive versions of themselves, and join in cruelty rather than become its target, revealing how fragile, and how dangerous, the desire for acceptance can be.

Moving Up the Mark Bands

Use this as a checklist on your draft. Each band move is a few things you can actually change. The wording of the bands comes from the Section A descriptors on the Exam Assessment Criteria page.
Before you start, check every body paragraph has:

  • a topic sentence that states a theme, not a character;

  • at least one quotation from the text;

  • one technique (narration, setting, sound, a repeated image) and what it does;

  • a last sentence that links back to your contention.

From 6 to 7

  1. Use the text in every body paragraph. Count the quotations in each paragraph. If one has none, find a line that shows the idea you are making.

  2. Name one technique in each paragraph and say what it does. Replace “Jackson uses a calm voice” with the effect: “the calm voice makes the violence sound ordinary”.

  3. Keep the argument going to the end of the paragraph. If the last sentence only retells the story, rewrite it to say what the story shows.

From 7 to 8

  1. Explain how the technique produces the idea. Use this order: technique, then effect on the reader, then the idea about the topic.

  2. Link every paragraph back to the contention in its last sentence. Your essay should read as one argument, not three separate points.

  3. Cut repetition. Read the essay aloud. If two sentences say the same thing, keep the more precise one.

Worked example, using the Lottery low sample, body paragraph 2.

  • Before (6): “Tessie is the only one who complains. She says ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’. The other villagers tell her to be a good sport. This shows people are afraid of being different.”

  • After (7–8): “Tessie is the only one who complains, and her ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’ is answered not with an argument but with ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’. That reply turns a question of fairness into a question of manners, which is how the village keeps dissent quiet. The pressure is social, not violent, and that is why conformity lasts.”

From 8 to 9 or 10

  1. Show one complication. Find a piece of evidence that works against your view. Answer it in one sentence, then say what your view still is or where it needs limiting.

  2. Use precise verbs for what the text does. Replace “shows” and “reveals” with the action itself: “silences”, “exposes”, “normalises”. Check each verb against the text.

  3. Check every claim. Each sentence should point to something a marker can find in the text. If a sentence is a general comment about life, cut it or tie it to the text.

Practice

Take the Picnic low sample and apply the 6-to-7 checklist to one paragraph. Then take the Picnic medium sample and apply the 8-to-9 checklist to the same paragraph. The difference between the two is the band move.
