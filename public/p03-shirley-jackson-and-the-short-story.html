Shirley Jackson and the Short Story

On this page
  • Why the Short Story?
  • Reading the Collection as a Whole
  • Analysis

Shirley Jackson (1916–1965)

Shirley Jackson was born in San Francisco and spent most of her adult life in North Bennington, Vermont, where her husband, the critic Stanley Edgar Hyman, taught at Bennington College. She raised four children while writing novels, short stories and humorous accounts of family life. As an outsider in a small New England town, she experienced the kind of suspicion and gossip her stories depict. She is best known today for ‘The Lottery’ and for her novels The Haunting of Hill House (1959) and We Have Always Lived in the Castle (1962), and is regarded as a major writer of psychological suspense and the uncanny.

Why the Short Story?

A short story has little room for explanation. Jackson uses this to her advantage. She often:

  • opens with a description of the protagonist’s ordinary routine or a pleasant setting;

  • introduces an unexpected event, stranger or disruption;

  • lets tension build through small, everyday details and dialogue;

  • ends abruptly, often with the protagonist excluded, defeated or trapped, and without explaining what it all means.

The reader is left to fill the silence, and that is where much of the unease comes from.

Reading the Collection as a Whole

Although each story stands alone, the collection is designed to be read together. A figure named James Harris (or Jim, or Jamie) appears in many stories in different guises: a missing fiancé, a writer, a dinner guest, a neighbour, a stranger in a blue suit. The book is divided into parts introduced by epigraphs from Joseph Glanvill’s seventeenth-century treatise on witchcraft, and ends with an epilogue: verses from the old ballad ‘James Harris, The Daemon Lover’, in which a mysterious lover carries a woman away to her doom.

Analysis

These links suggest that the strangeness in each story is part of a larger pattern. James Harris works almost like a demon or tempter who drifts through ordinary lives, exposing the dissatisfaction and fear already present beneath the surface. In essays, connecting stories through these recurring figures and motifs shows the examiners that you understand the collection, not just individual stories.
