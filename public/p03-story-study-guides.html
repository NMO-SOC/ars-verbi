Use these guides while you read each story. Each one lists the ideas to underline and the symbols to circle, questions to think about as you read, and close analysis of key moments.

‘The Daemon Lover’

The unnamed protagonist, thirty-four years old, is getting ready for her wedding day. Jamie, her fiancé, never arrives, and the story follows her as she goes looking for him, meeting strangers who are busy with their own lives and mostly indifferent to hers. The title can be read two ways. Jamie behaves like a ‘demon lover’ from the old ballad, a man who lures a woman away and vanishes. But a ‘daemon’ is also a guiding spirit, and Jackson leaves open the possibility that what drives the woman to that final door is her own longing rather than anything Jamie does. Annotate:

  • Unexpected events that expose our vulnerabilities – things that are damaged, shabby or out of place behind a tidy front.

  • Dissatisfaction with our identities or lives – her careful, tidy apartment set against the Roysters’ lively home; what she chooses to wear.

  • Feeling isolated or excluded from wider society – doors and opportunities being opened or locked.

Questions to consider:

  1. As she waits, she finds she is ‘not satisfied with her clothes, her face, her apartment.’ What is she hoping marriage will give her?

  2. At the Roysters’, the couple are eating, their apartment is ‘full of music’ and their suitcases are out. How does this compare to her apartment, and what impression does it give of other people’s lives?

  3. The story ends with her knocking repeatedly on a door. What might the closed door symbolise?

Food and emptiness. Jackson keeps noting that the protagonist does not eat: ‘all this coffee... no real breakfast’; ‘There was nothing to eat in the apartment’; ‘In the drugstore she found that there was nothing she wanted to eat.’ Possible readings: the detail keeps the setting realistic; her empty cupboard mirrors an empty life; she is starved of more than food; she is so fixed on the wedding that she forgets to look after herself. The second reading connects most clearly with the rest of the story. Dressing for the wedding. She agonises over what to wear:

  • ‘she wanted to be soft, feminine’ – she wants to look like the bride she has imagined.

  • ‘it was very early in the year for a print dress’ – it is important to her that she observes the social mores of wider society.

  • the blue dress is sensible but has nothing of a wedding about it – she wants to be correct, but she also wants to feel like a bride.

  • putting on her make-up ‘was another delicate balance’ – she wants to look lovely without seeming to be pretending.

  • the print dress looked made for ‘someone who would run freely, dance, swing it with her hips’ – she cannot picture herself as that carefree woman.

Together these details show a woman pulled between what she thinks is proper and what she actually wants. For example: Jackson reveals the protagonist’s longing to look the part of a bride through her wish to be ‘soft, feminine’. Word bank: reveals, exposes, captures, dramatises, conveys + the gap between what she wants and what is expected of her; a self shaped by other people’s approval; the fear of being seen as foolish or unwanted; the loneliness of a woman without a place in the city; the fragility of her hopes; the cost of waiting for someone else to give life meaning.

‘Like Mother Used to Make’

The title phrase calls up recipes handed down from mother to daughter, a world of women’s cooking. It never appears in the story itself, but it fits David Turner, who keeps a spotless home and has baked a cherry pie for dinner with his neighbour Marcia. When Mr Harris, a stranger, arrives looking for Marcia, he assumes the apartment and the cooking are hers. Rather than correct him, David lets the assumption stand, and the evening slips out of his control. Annotate:

  • Conformity through judgement and social expectations of gender – cooking, cleaning and tidiness, against mess, disorder and damaged things.

  • Human isolation or insecurity – keys, locks and doors; who is noticed and who is overlooked.

  • The disruption other people bring – unexpected events; being unsure how to respond.

Questions to consider:

  1. How is David described at the beginning of the story, and what does it suggest about his personality?

  2. When David begins tidying up, how does Marcia react? What does each reaction suggest about their attitudes to convention?

  3. David ends the story alone in an apartment that is not his own. What does this suggest about the cost of living against expectation?

David and Marcia.

  • David: ‘David Turner... did everything in small, quick movements’; ‘The neat small table and four careful chairs’; ‘It pleased him to have only one key to his home’; it gave him ‘a pleasant feeling’, the only way into his ‘warm fine home’; ‘He had chosen a sedate, pretty pattern’; ‘He had always been partial to yellows and browns.’

  • Marcia: ‘Marcia’s home was bare and at random’; it was ‘too cluttered’; ‘Marcia’s bed was unmade’; ‘The window had been open all day and papers had blown wildly around the floor’; ‘Marcia arrived with a shout and fresh air and disorder’; ‘She was a tall handsome girl with a loud voice’; ‘Marcia was always hungry.’

Statements to weigh: David takes more pleasure in housekeeping than Marcia does; Jackson uses Marcia’s mess to criticise women who reject domestic life; David’s order is a sign of strength, not weakness; Marcia’s carelessness gives her a freedom David lacks; David’s neatness is a way of keeping control in a world where he has very little. By the end, David has been shut out of his own home and his own pie has been credited to Marcia. Sentence starters: In the final scene, David is displaced by...; Jackson suggests that David gives way because...; Marcia, by contrast,...; David’s retreat into Marcia’s apartment shows...

‘The Villager’

Miss Hilda Clarence, thirty-five, seems settled, until an errand makes her think about the life she might have had. She came to Greenwich Village, the bohemian heart of New York, because it was where young artists went, and she still thinks of herself as a ‘villager’. In reality she works as a secretary and dresses in ‘smart clothes’; the dancing she came for never happened. Looking around an apartment whose owners are moving out, she builds a picture of their lives and slips into it, and Jackson shows how someone can be both content and quietly disappointed at once. Annotate:

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction about identity, or satisfaction and confidence about self – opening and closing doors; furniture and household objects; clothing.

  • Social judgement and exclusion – who does the housework; whose career counts.

Questions to consider:

  1. Miss Clarence came to Greenwich Village because ‘everyone who wanted to study dancing or sculpture’ came there. What does this suggest about how individual she is?

  2. At the start she is ‘Miss Clarence’; at the end she gives her name as ‘Hilda Clarence’. What has changed in how she sees herself?

  3. Many of Jackson’s stories close on a single small action. What does the last thing Miss Clarence does tell us?

Judgements and gender. Miss Clarence is the only woman we actually meet; Artie Roberts (on the phone) and Harris are men. Walking through the apartment, she judges the absent couple: ‘she doesn’t even make coffee in the morning’; ‘Arthur was the artist, then, and Nancy...’. Artie mentions that he is off to Paris while his wife goes back to her family in Chicago. Readings: women are expected to give up their ambitions; men move more freely between cities and careers; women are expected to swallow disappointment quietly; the men barely notice what the women give up; a woman’s place is assumed to be the home. Miss Clarence and Harris.

  • Miss Clarence: ‘she had come to New York... because she wanted to be a dancer’; ‘The first thing Miss Clarence did was go to the window... But she could see only dirty roofs’; ‘She opened the cupboard’; ‘could Nancy, she wondered affectionately, be a dancer?’; ‘She had already decided that she could not use any of the furniture.’

  • Harris: ‘I hope to write... Going to get a job and write nights’; ‘This place is certainly nicer than mine’; ‘He wandered over and opened the bathroom door; when he glanced in Miss Clarence winced’; he ‘opened the kitchen door’; ‘What I need is a wife’; ‘I’m sorry about the furniture... Wish I could have found something.’

Both are would-be artists living lives they did not plan, and both size up the apartment, but Harris still talks about his future, while Miss Clarence can only borrow someone else’s. Practice topic: Jackson’s characters are rarely content with what they have. Discuss. Starters: Across the collection, Jackson shows...; Jackson’s characters repeatedly...; Although Miss Clarence appears..., she...; Where Harris..., Miss Clarence...; Jackson sets... against... to show...; On the surface..., but underneath...

‘The Witch’

Johnny, a small boy, is on a train with his mother and baby sister. His mother keeps the baby quiet with toast and gives Johnny only half her attention, teaching both children, without seeming to try, to sit still and behave. Then an elderly stranger sits down and tells Johnny a gruesome story about killing his own little sister. Johnny is thrilled; his mother is appalled. When the man leaves, Johnny decides he must have been a ‘witch’, someone who does not follow the rules everyone else lives by. Annotate:

  • Violence lies beneath the veneer of civilised behaviour – excitement about violence.

  • Social pressure and parenting create conformity – the train window and the world outside it; toast and lollipops as rewards; the strap holding the baby in her seat.

Questions to consider:

  1. Strangers on the train tell the boy he is ‘a fine big fellow’, and ‘These comments annoyed the little boy’. Why might he be irritated?

  2. The boy tells his mother he has seen a witch out of the window. She replies, ‘Fine.’ How does this show adults modelling social norms for children?

  3. When the stranger asks his age and name, the boy says ‘Twenty-six. Eight hunnerd and forty eighty’ and ‘Mr. Jesus’. What do these answers show about how much of adult convention he has absorbed?

When the man has gone, the mother tells Johnny that he ‘was just teasing’, but Johnny prefers his own explanation: the man was ‘a witch’. Readings to weigh: to a child, an adult who breaks the rules can only be a creature from a story; the mother is defending the rules of how adults should behave; everyone finds a way to explain away what disturbs them; children take strangeness in their stride more easily than adults; the mother simply wants to protect her son. How children are taught to behave: the baby is ‘strapped securely to the seat’ (physical restraint); she is ‘very quiet and busy with her rattle and her toast’ (kept occupied and silent); ‘the little boy had a seat all to himself’ (independence, but also separation); when he comforts his sister, ‘the little boy received a lollipop’ (good behaviour is paid for). Practice topic: How does Jackson challenge the roles expected of women and men in her stories? Vocabulary: trained to behave, kept in line, shaped by what others expect, bound by manners, rewarded for compliance, eager to please; breaking the rules of polite society, stepping outside their role, refusing what is expected, defying convention, unsettling the people around them.

‘The Renegade’

A renegade is someone who breaks ranks, and this story has two of them. One is Mrs Walpole, a city woman now living in the country, who never feels she belongs and is sure her neighbours run their homes better than she does. The other is her dog, Lady, who has started killing the neighbours’ chickens. Every piece of advice the town offers about Lady becomes a measure of how uncertain Mrs Walpole is, and how sure of themselves everyone else seems. Annotate:

  • Social judgement and pressure – chores and routines; keeping to time.

  • Violent attitudes and behaviours – violence answered with more violence; the children’s cheerful interest in cruelty.

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction – measuring herself against her neighbours; seeing herself in the dog.

Questions to consider:

  1. The story begins with a morning that has already gone wrong. How does this rushed start affect Mrs Walpole?

  2. How does Mrs Walpole try to fit in with the town, or win its approval?

  3. At the end she feels ‘the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat.’ Whose are the ‘harsh hands’?

Mrs Walpole measures herself against the ‘country folk’ and feels she does not belong, but that is her perception, not necessarily the town’s. Reread what each neighbour tells her. Statements to weigh up:

  • The neighbours are friendly, but every one of them agrees that a chicken-killing dog cannot be kept.

  • Their polite advice carries a quiet judgement that Mrs Walpole cannot manage her own animal.

  • Some suggest brutal remedies and some gentler ones, so the town is not of one mind.

  • The townspeople are practical people for whom livestock matters more than feelings.

  • Their warm manner makes the cruelty of what they suggest all the more striking.

  • They pity Mrs Walpole, but they also expect her to fall into line.

  • Under the neighbourly talk lies a code that newcomers are simply expected to adopt.

Analytic verbs: exposes, hints, suggests, undercuts, reveals, mirrors, dramatises, unsettles.

‘Charles’

When Laurie starts kindergarten, he comes home each day with news of a classmate called Charles, who swears, hits the teacher and hurts the other children. His mother, the narrator, grows so absorbed in Charles’s misdeeds that she longs to meet his mother at the parent-teacher meeting. There the teacher tells her there is no Charles in the class; it is Laurie who has ‘had a little trouble adjusting’. The twist lets Jackson show how far a parent’s pride, and her fear of what others will think, can keep her from seeing what is in front of her. Annotate:

  • Human insecurity and dissatisfaction – how the mother describes Laurie’s changing behaviour; her eagerness to hear about someone else’s child.

  • Social judgement – door slamming; creating mess; refusing to follow instructions or social norms.

  • Violence beneath the veneer of civilisation – Charles’s attacks; the family’s appetite for hearing about them.

Questions to consider:

  1. The narrator describes her son as ‘a long-trousered, swaggering character’. What other clues show that ‘Charles’ is really Laurie?

  2. How do both parents encourage the idea that misbehaviour is interesting and worth talking about?

  3. The mother assumes Laurie’s ‘trouble adjusting’ must be Charles’s fault. What does this show about where she thinks bad behaviour comes from?

Sentence starters: The narrator’s pride in her son blinds her to...; Like many of Jackson’s characters, Laurie’s mother worries more about appearances than...; Jackson shows how easily a family can turn...; By keeping the twist until the last line, Jackson...

‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’

Mrs Wilson builds a whole picture of Boyd’s home, money and family from the fact that he is Black, and every detail of it is wrong. When Boyd politely says his family does not need Johnny’s old clothes, her generosity turns cold. Jackson uses this small domestic scene to show how well-meaning assumptions keep people in their ‘place’, and how charity can be a way of confirming who is above whom. Annotate:

  • Communities enforce conformity through social pressure – food given and withdrawn to reward or punish.

  • Social judgement and assumptions – food and clothing as signs of class.

Questions to consider:

  1. Johnny and Boyd’s game involves ‘running over’ Japanese soldiers, the enemy of the United States in the Second World War. What does this suggest about how children pick up the prejudices around them?

  2. What assumptions does Mrs Wilson make about Boyd’s family, and what do they reveal about her world-view?

  3. Mrs Wilson says, ‘Don’t think I’m angry, Boyd. I’m just disappointed in you.’ What word more accurately describes how she feels?

The title. The boys say the line as they wave each other through doorways. It comes from an early comic strip about two Frenchmen so excessively courteous that they could never get anything done, a joke about manners that are all show. Readings: Boyd’s easy use of the phrase undercuts Mrs Wilson’s idea of where he comes from; the title points to the gap between her courtesy and her respect; the boys’ mock politeness is more genuine than her real politeness; Jackson uses the comic strip to mock the performance of kindness. Endings. Jackson often closes on a short line that sums up where a character has been left, emotionally or socially, and lets the discomfort linger. In ‘The Villager’, ‘Her shoulders ached’ after Miss Clarence’s performance as a bohemian dancer. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘No matter how often or how firmly she knocked, no one ever came to the door’ leaves the protagonist stranded. Try: When Johnny and Boyd agree at the end that Mrs Wilson is ‘screwy’, Jackson suggests...; The final exchange of ‘After you, my dear Alphonse’ shows...

‘Flower Garden’

Mrs MacLane, newly widowed, moves from the city into a long-empty cottage, restores it, plants a beautiful garden and becomes friends with Mrs Winning. Mrs Winning, who lives with her husband’s family in the gloomy old Winning house, sees in the cottage the life she once imagined for herself. Then Mrs MacLane hires Mr Jones, a Black man, to help with the garden, and the town’s racism comes into the open. Afraid of being tarred with her friend’s ‘mistake’, Mrs Winning falls back into line with the town. Annotate:

  • Communities enforcing conformity – schedules and routines; traditions; characters feeling helpless.

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction – loneliness; admiring and envying another woman’s home and possessions.

  • Unexpected events or strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – dull colours against bright ones; moments of surprise.

Questions to consider:

  1. The two Mrs Winnings ‘had grown to look a good deal alike’, and ‘uncounted Winning children had played with almost identical toys’. What do these details suggest about Mrs Winning’s sense of herself as an individual?

  2. The neighbours tell Mrs Winning to have a word with Mrs MacLane ‘about that colored man’, but no one spells out what is wrong. What does that silence reveal about how prejudice works in the town?

  3. The walk up the hill to the Winning house feels different to Mrs Winning at different times. Choose two moments and explain what has changed.

Mrs Winning and Mrs MacLane. House: ‘This dreary old place’ against Mrs MacLane’s renovated cottage. Garden: the Winnings’ plain yard against a house surrounded by beautiful flowers. Child: Mrs Winning doesn’t show physical affection; Mrs MacLane’s son Davey becomes Howard’s friend. Husband: Mrs Winning lives under her husband’s family; Mrs MacLane is on her own now he is dead. Independence: Mrs MacLane ‘had some money in her own name’. Clothing: Mrs Winning puts ‘a scarf over her head’; Mrs MacLane wears a rose-coloured sweater, ‘a spot of color’ in the room. The townspeople’s role. When the neighbours tell Mrs Winning to have a word with Mrs MacLane, what part are they playing? Judges (passing verdict on others), followers (repeating what they have heard), gatekeepers (deciding who belongs), guardians (defending the town’s ways), insiders (protecting their own place in the group). The name ‘Winning’. The name promises victory, yet Mrs Winning loses again and again: her dream cottage, her friend, her own sense of right. Vocabulary: the irony of her name, a name she has married into, a hollow win, the victory of belonging, the price of acceptance, trapped by the family she joined, safe but diminished, rewarded for giving in, a triumph of conformity over conscience.

‘Elizabeth’

Elizabeth Style has a full name, a career and a firm manner, and at first she seems more certain of herself than most of Jackson’s women. Yet people call her ‘Miss Style’, ‘Liz’ and ‘Beth’, and none of the names seems to fit. She runs a failing literary agency with Robert Shax, whom she has worked beside for nearly eleven years; he leans on her for everything, and the agency has lost good clients, such as Jim Harris, along the way. By the end of the day she has made her move, inviting an old client, Jim Harris, to her apartment and imagining a new and more independent life with him. Annotate:

  • Conformity through years of habit – the routines she and Robert have fallen into; their lunches and drinks.

  • Human isolation, insecurity and dissatisfaction – solitude; windows; partitions, doors and separate rooms; grey against colour.

  • Strangers disrupting any sense of security – Daphne’s arrival; new colours and behaviour in the office.

  • A shifting sense of identity – daydreams of another life; memories; names.

Questions to consider:

  1. Elizabeth is woken from a dream: ‘Just before the alarm went off she was lying in a hot sunny garden’, and when she opened her eyes ‘it was raining’. How does this contrast show her dreaming of two different lives?

  2. Jackson calls her Elizabeth, but others call her ‘Liz’ or ‘Beth’. How do these names show the different roles she plays?

  3. At the end she buys drinks and crackers and tidies her apartment for Harris. What do these preparations reveal about the life she hoped for and the one she has?

Gender. Does Elizabeth fit 1940s ideas of how a woman should be? Qualities her society would have called masculine: brisk, in charge, ambitious, unsentimental, sharp-tongued, at home in business. Qualities it would have called feminine: anxious to be wanted, sensitive to slights, concerned with looks and age, house-proud, given to romantic daydreams. Elizabeth draws on both, which makes her a figure caught between roles that do not quite fit any real woman. Practice topic: How does Jackson challenge the roles expected of women and men in her stories? Starters: In a world of fixed roles for women and men...; Jackson’s women are often expected to...; Jackson’s men, by contrast,...; Characters such as Elizabeth... + resist, unsettle, step outside, turn upside down, expose the cost of, question or accept, obey, play along with, cling to, are trapped by, keep up the appearance of.

‘The Tooth’

Much of ‘The Tooth’ unfolds like a dream, which makes it one of the strangest stories in the collection. Clara Spencer takes a night bus to New York to have an aching tooth seen to, dosed with codeine and whisky. In her drugged state, the line between what is happening and what she imagines dissolves, and Jackson lets her slip out of her ordinary small-town life altogether. Annotate:

  • Dissatisfaction, a shifting sense of identity and isolation – mirrors and faces; grey against bright colour; dreams and half-sleep; the loss of her name.

  • Strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – drifting in and out of consciousness; Jim.

Questions to consider:

  1. Many stories open with a description of everyday routine. What does Jackson describe at the start of this story to show what normal life is like for the protagonist?

  2. For most of the story she is unnamed, until she finds the name ‘Clara’ written on one of her belongings. Clara means ‘clear’ or ‘bright’. What is significant about this?

  3. Many of Jackson’s protagonists end the story trapped. Is Clara? Compare her ending with one other story’s.

Jim, the man in the blue suit. His bright suit and easy confidence stand out against Clara’s foggy, faded sense of herself. He talks of a world far from bus depots, roadside diners and dentists’ chairs: ‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, ‘nothing to do all day but lie under the trees’, ‘The grass is so green and so soft’. He even mentions Samarkand, a fabled city on the Silk Road, the old trade route of silks, spices and stories. Readings: Jim is Clara’s own wish to escape, given a voice; he is just a kind stranger; she uses him to imagine herself as someone more exciting; his poetic speech offers her a new identity. Doors, windows and mirrors. As in ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘Like Mother Used to Make’ and ‘The Villager’, these objects frame glimpses of other lives the protagonist might enter, or be shut out of. Vocabulary: a glimpse of another life, a way out, a barrier, a life not lived, a self she does not recognise, the boundary between fantasy and fact, an escape from the domestic, a door that will not open, a reflection that does not fit. Practice question: What do the doors and windows in Jackson’s stories reveal about her characters’ longing for another life?

‘Of Course’

Mrs Tylor watches her new neighbour arrive and decides from her furniture and her tan suit that the Harrises are ‘nice people, obviously’. Like many of Jackson’s characters, she wants the world around her to be normal and respectable. Mrs Harris turns out to have very particular views: no movies, no radio, no newspapers. Mrs Tylor grows more uneasy with each revelation, yet all she can say is ‘Of course’, a phrase that shows both her horror of open disagreement and how automatically she falls back on politeness. Annotate:

  • Communities enforce conformity through expectations and social pressure – judging by clothes and furniture; quick assumptions.

  • Unexpected events or strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – newcomers; household chores; unfamiliar ways of living.

Questions to consider:

  1. Mrs Tylor is ‘far too polite’ to stand outside and stare, but she keeps an eye on the move while doing her chores. What does this say about her idea of politeness?

  2. Mrs Tylor stays agreeable while Carol is openly ‘sullen’ with the new boy. Which of them is being more honest?

  3. Mrs Tylor’s last line is that she will take Carol to the movies. Why does this small decision matter?

Neighbourliness and conformity.

  • Mrs Tylor ‘recognized finally the faint nervous feeling that was tagging her; it was the way she felt when she was irrevocably connected with something dangerously out of control’ – characters feel uneasy when neighbours do not behave in predictable or conventional ways.

  • ‘Carol shut her mouth obstinately and the little boy edged behind his mother’ – neighbourly relationships can reveal the tension between personal choice and social expectation.

  • Mrs Tylor approves of the woman’s ‘nice-looking tan suit’: ‘Nice people, obviously’ – it is important for neighbours to fit accepted notions of respectability.

  • Mrs Tylor: ‘There’s something so nice about congenial people right next door’ – neighbourliness is meant to involve easy social interactions.

  • Mrs Harris: ‘it means such a lot to have really thoughtful neighbors’ – being a ‘good neighbour’ can mean behaving in ways others see as conforming to their values.

Vocabulary: keeping the peace, fitting in, good manners, the wish to be liked, fear of seeming rude, the comfort of the familiar, unease with difference; agreeable, accommodating, uneasy, judgemental, quietly defiant; exposes, hints, undercuts, contrasts, reveals.

‘The Lottery’

Most readers know a lottery as a game of chance in which a name or number is drawn and someone wins a prize. Jackson relies on that familiarity. The villagers gather and gossip as if at a fete, and the ritual unfolds so calmly that nothing seems wrong until the last page, when the ‘winner’ is stoned to death by her neighbours. The shock of that ending lets Jackson show how a community can do something monstrous simply because it always has.

Character First half of the story End of the story
Tessie Hutchinson Turns up late, joking and chatting with the other women. Grows frightened once her family’s name comes up. Draws the slip with the black spot and cries that it ‘isn’t fair’ as the stones begin.
Bill Hutchinson Takes his turn without fuss and draws the marked slip for his family. Prises the paper from Tessie’s hand to show the crowd the black spot.
Mr Summers Runs the lottery, as he runs the square dances and the teen club; worries about the shabby black box. Oversees the final selection and keeps the ritual moving efficiently.
Old Man Warner Defends the tradition and criticises villages that want to stop it. Urges the villagers on as they close in.
Mrs Delacroix Chats in a friendly way with Tessie at the start. Picks up a stone so large she needs both hands and helps kill Tessie.
Mr Adams Mentions that other villages are talking of giving up the lottery. Still participates in the stoning, at the front of the crowd.
Davy Hutchinson Stands with his family through the draw, too young to understand it. Is handed a few pebbles of his own.
Bobby Martin Fills his pockets with stones with the other boys before the draw. The children who gathered the stones take part in the killing.

Roles in a society: the characters can be labelled enforcer, traditionalist, scapegoat, bystander, organiser, follower, enabler, accomplice, dissenter, conformist, victim, outcast. Tessie as a transgressor. Tessie’s name is drawn by chance, but Jackson gives her small differences from the start: she arrives late, saying she ‘Clean forgot what day it was’. Forgetting the day hints at a slight distance from the ritual, which sets her a little apart before the lottery does. The anchor story. ‘The Lottery’ powerfully represents almost every idea in the collection:

  • Isolation, insecurity and dissatisfaction: the moment the crowd turns, Tessie stands utterly alone; beforehand, the villagers’ nervous jokes and small talk hint at the fear they will not name.

  • Social judgement and exclusion: Tessie is cast out the instant she is chosen; compare Mrs MacLane, frozen out for employing Mr Jones in ‘Flower Garden’, and Boyd, misjudged by Mrs Wilson in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’.

  • Communities enforce conformity through traditions, rituals and social pressure: the lottery survives because no one is willing to be the first to stop.

  • Unexpected events disrupting routines and assumptions: one slip of paper turns a village gathering into an execution.

  • Violence beneath the veneer of civilised behaviour: friends and family kill Tessie with the same calm they bring to a town meeting.

  • A shifting sense of identity: in a single moment Tessie goes from neighbour to victim.
