Use these guides while you read each story. Each one lists the ideas to underline and the symbols to circle, questions to think about as you read, and close analysis of key moments.

‘The Daemon Lover’

A woman in her mid-thirties is preparing to meet her fiancé and get married. As the story progresses, it becomes clear she has been stood up. Searching for him, she is confronted by the different lives of other people and their indifference to her plight. It is easy to assume the ‘daemon’ of the title refers to the demon-like way her fiancé stands her up, but ‘daemon’ also means a supernatural force that guides a person. Jackson is exploring how the protagonist’s own feelings drive her to the story’s fateful ending. Annotate:

  • Unexpected events that expose our vulnerabilities – ripped, torn or ugly things beneath an apparently calm surface.

  • Dissatisfaction with our identities or lives – the neatness and orderliness of her apartment compared to the Roysters’ home; clothing.

  • Feeling isolated or excluded from wider society – doors and opportunities being opened or locked.

Questions to consider:

  1. Waiting restlessly, she realises she is ‘not satisfied with her clothes, her face, her apartment.’ What does she hope to achieve with her marriage?

  2. At the Roysters’, the couple are eating, their apartment is ‘full of music’ and their suitcases are out. How does this compare to her apartment, and what impression does it give of other people’s lives?

  3. The story ends with her knocking repeatedly on a door. What might the closed door symbolise?

Food and emptiness. There are recurring descriptions of the protagonist not eating: ‘all this coffee... no real breakfast’; ‘There was nothing to eat in the apartment’; ‘In the drugstore she found that there was nothing she wanted to eat.’ Possible readings: food makes the setting ordinary and realistic; the lack of food suggests her life is empty and hollow; her life has left her hungry and undernourished; she is too absorbed by her obsession with a future husband to eat. The strongest reading is that the lack of food suggests the emptiness of her life. Dressing for the wedding. She agonises over what to wear:

  • ‘she wanted to be soft, feminine’ – she wants to present herself as an idealised bride.

  • ‘it was very early in the year for a print dress’ – it is important to her that she observes the social mores of wider society.

  • nothing about the blue dress made it special for a wedding day – she is torn between behaving suitably and wanting to celebrate herself as a bride.

  • putting on her make-up ‘was another delicate balance’ between looking her best and deceiving as little as possible – she is balanced between portraying herself honestly and wanting to be thought beautiful.

  • the print dress looked made for ‘someone who would run freely, dance, swing it with her hips’ – she feels like an imposter in her own life.

All these quotes show a woman caught between behaving as she thinks others will find appropriate and following her own heart. For example: Jackson explores the protagonist’s personal desire to present herself as an idealised bride through her need to be ‘soft, feminine’. Word bank: explores, highlights, reveals, demonstrates, represents + the tension between private desire and social expectation; the pressure to conform and private doubts; individual morality is easily suppressed when it clashes with communal norms; the desire to belong outweighs personal ethics; societal dictates can become so deeply embedded they override personal desire; the fragility of self against the behemoth of social order; individual identity and social behaviour.

‘Like Mother Used to Make’

The phrase ‘like mother used to make’ suggests recipes and traditions passed down through generations of women. It is never used in the story, but it reflects the domestic, orderly way David Turner lives. His sense of order is unsettled when an unexpected stranger disrupts his evening and imposes an outsider’s view: the visitor assumes they are all in the home of a woman, eating food prepared by a woman. David goes along with these assumptions, humbly accepting societal expectations. Annotate:

  • Conformity through judgement and social expectations of gender – housekeeping (cooking, cleaning, keeping an orderly home), or being messy and slovenly (things broken or ripped).

  • Human isolation or insecurity – keys, locks, doors; people being ignored.

  • The disruption other people bring – unexpected events; being unsure how to respond.

Questions to consider:

  1. How is David described at the beginning of the story, and what does it suggest about his personality?

  2. When David starts cleaning, how does Marcia respond? What does this show about how each of them adheres to social conventions?

  3. The story ends with David alone in someone else’s apartment. What is Jackson saying about how hard it is to live a different lifestyle?

David and Marcia.

  • David: ‘David Turner... did everything in small, quick movements’; ‘The neat small table and four careful chairs’; ‘It pleased him to have only one key to his home’; it gave him ‘a pleasant feeling’, the only way into his ‘warm fine home’; ‘He had chosen a sedate, pretty pattern’; ‘He had always been partial to yellows and browns.’

  • Marcia: ‘Marcia’s home was bare and at random’; it was ‘too cluttered’; ‘Marcia’s bed was unmade’; ‘The window had been open all day and papers had blown wildly around the floor’; ‘Marcia arrived with a shout and fresh air and disorder’; ‘She was a tall handsome girl with a loud voice’; ‘Marcia was always hungry.’

Statements to weigh: compared with Marcia, David appears to enjoy traditionally feminine domestic activities; unlike David, Marcia is careless and immature, showing that Jackson disapproves of modern women; in contrast to Marcia’s chaotic lifestyle, David demonstrates traditional male structure and routine; in comparison to Marcia, David is careful, controlled and highly organised. At the end, David is isolated and excluded from his own home while Marcia is assumed to have baked the pie. Sentence starters: At the end of the story, David is exiled by...; Jackson suggests that David’s withdrawal is the result of...; In comparison, Marcia...; While Jackson presents David’s isolation as a symbol of..., Marcia...

‘The Villager’

Like many of Jackson’s characters, Miss Clarence is a woman in her mid-thirties who appears content but is prompted by events to reflect on an alternative life. She identifies strongly with being a ‘villager’, someone from the famously artistic, bohemian Greenwich Village. But she is now a secretary in ‘smart clothes’ rather than the dancer she once hoped to be. Inspecting the furnishings of a soon-to-be-vacated apartment, she imagines herself into its occupants’ lives, revealing one of Jackson’s ongoing concerns: people can feel both satisfied and dissatisfied with their lives at the same time. Annotate:

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction about identity, or satisfaction and confidence about self – opening and closing doors; furniture and household objects; clothing.

  • Social judgement and exclusion – household duties; men and careers.

Questions to consider:

  1. Miss Clarence came to Greenwich Village because ‘everyone who wanted to study dancing or sculpture’ came there. What does this suggest about how individual she is?

  2. Early on she is ‘Miss Clarence’; at the end she signs her name ‘Hilda Clarence’. What does this tell us about her sense of identity?

  3. Jackson’s stories often end with an action that sums up how events have made a character feel. What does the ending tell us about Miss Clarence’s experience?

Judgements and gender. Miss Clarence is the only woman who appears directly in the story; Artie Roberts and Harris are men. As she walks through the apartment she judges its owners: ‘she doesn’t even make coffee in the morning’; ‘Arthur was the artist, then, and Nancy...’. On the phone, Artie mentions he is going to Paris while his wife returns to her family in Chicago. Readings: society expects women to sacrifice their dreams; men have greater freedom to move through public life and careers; women are expected to quietly accept disappointment; men are often emotionally disconnected from women’s experience; women are expected to prioritise domesticity over careers. Miss Clarence and Harris.

  • Miss Clarence: ‘she had come to New York... because she wanted to be a dancer’; ‘The first thing Miss Clarence did was go to the window... But she could see only dirty roofs’; ‘She opened the cupboard’; ‘could Nancy, she wondered affectionately, be a dancer?’; ‘She had already decided that she could not use any of the furniture.’

  • Harris: ‘I hope to write... Going to get a job and write nights’; ‘This place is certainly nicer than mine’; ‘He wandered over and opened the bathroom door; when he glanced in Miss Clarence winced’; he ‘opened the kitchen door’; ‘What I need is a wife’; ‘I’m sorry about the furniture... Wish I could have found something.’

Both are hopeful artists without the lives they want; both judge the apartment; but Harris still believes in his future, while Miss Clarence can only borrow someone else’s. Practice topic: Shirley Jackson’s short stories reveal lack of satisfaction. Discuss. Starters: Throughout her short stories, Jackson reveals...; In each of her stories, Jackson’s characters demonstrate...; Although many of Jackson’s characters expose their...; While both characters...; The parallel activities of...; On the surface both...; Although apparently similar because of...; In comparison to...; The contrast between...; The disparity between...; This is juxtaposed...

‘The Witch’

A young boy takes a train ride with his mother and baby sister. The mother keeps the baby quiet by constantly feeding her toast and largely ignores her son’s questions and comments, casually teaching her children compliance. When a strange man sits down and tells the boy a shockingly violent story, the boy is delighted and the mother horrified. When the man suggests chopping off his little sister’s head, the boy concludes that the man is a ‘witch’, someone who lives beyond the conventions of normal society. Annotate:

  • Violence lies beneath the veneer of civilised behaviour – excitement about violence.

  • Social pressure and parenting create conformity – windows to an outside, different world; food and lollipops; the straps that hold the baby in a sitting position.

Questions to consider:

  1. Strangers on the train tell the boy he is ‘a fine big fellow’, and ‘These comments annoyed the little boy’. Why might he be irritated?

  2. The boy tells his mother he has seen a witch out of the window. She replies, ‘Fine.’ How does this show adults modelling social norms for children?

  3. Asked his age and name, the boy replies ‘Twenty-six. Eight hunnerd and forty eighty’ and ‘Mr. Jesus’. What does this tell us about children’s understanding of social conventions?

After the man leaves, the mother insists he ‘was just teasing’, but the boy decides he was more likely ‘a witch’. Readings to weigh: the boy believes adults who don’t conform to the rules of society must be mythical; the mother is trying to uphold normative values about how others should behave; everyone finds ways of rationalising aberrant behaviour; children accept odd behaviour more easily than adults; the mother doesn’t want her son to be frightened. How children are taught to behave: the baby is ‘strapped securely to the seat’ (restraint); she is ‘very quiet and busy with her rattle and her toast’ (kept quiet and compliant); ‘the little boy had a seat all to himself’ (independence, and isolation); he ‘ran across the aisle to pet his sister’s feet and beg her not to cry... and the little boy received a lollipop’ (good behaviour is rewarded). Practice topic: Shirley Jackson’s short stories consistently subvert societal expectations. Discuss. Vocabulary: preserving social order, moulded by cultural expectations, restricted by conventional attitudes, conditioned to bow to societal pressure, behaving according to accepted standards, adhering to social mores; disrupting the expectations of polite society, transgressing conventional boundaries, resisting the expectations of others, defying the conventions of others, pushing against societal mores.

‘The Renegade’

A renegade rebels against the accepted way of doing things, and this story has two. One is Mrs Walpole, who has moved from the city to a small country town where she feels like an outsider, unable to conform to local attitudes and insecure about managing domestic life compared to her more capable neighbours. The other is the dog, Lady, whose killing of chickens becomes the catalyst for Jackson to explore the tension between Mrs Walpole’s shaky identity and the apparent confidence of the people around her. Annotate:

  • Social judgement and pressure – household duties; time and schedules.

  • Violent attitudes and behaviours – the dog’s violence needing to be controlled through further violence; children’s attitudes to violence.

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction – comparing yourself to others; Mrs Walpole over-identifying with the dog.

Questions to consider:

  1. The story opens with a morning routine that has become disordered and rushed. How does the interruption make the protagonist feel?

  2. In what ways does Mrs Walpole try to conform to, or make herself acceptable to, the town?

  3. At the end she feels ‘the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat.’ Whose are the ‘harsh hands’?

Mrs Walpole compares herself to the ‘country folk’ around her and feels an outsider, but that does not necessarily mean she is treated as one. Re-read the advice she receives from each townsperson. Statements to weigh up:

  • The townspeople are outwardly kind and welcoming, but firm in their belief that a dog who kills chickens cannot be tolerated.

  • Although they speak politely, their comments reveal an underlying judgement about her failure to control the dog.

  • While many advocate harsh treatment, some offer gentler advice, revealing conflicting views about authority and control.

  • Jackson presents the townspeople as practical and community-minded, valuing order and responsibility above personal feelings.

  • The friendly tone of the townspeople contrasts with the harsh reality that they still expect the dog to be punished.

  • The townspeople show sympathy towards Mrs Walpole, yet also pressure her to conform to the expectations of country life.

  • Beneath the surface politeness of the town lies a strict set of shared values that outsiders are expected to accept.

Analytic verbs: understands, suggests, implies, reveals, reflects, evokes, symbolises, sympathises, recognises.

‘Charles’

A little boy starts kindergarten and becomes fascinated by a delinquent classmate called Charles. Each day he tells his parents about Charles’s misbehaviour: he swears, hits the teacher, punches other children and generally behaves terribly. His mother, who narrates the story, becomes so engrossed in Charles that she can’t wait to meet Charles’s mother. When she finally gets to school, she discovers there is no Charles at all, only her own son, who ‘had a little trouble adjusting’. Through this twist, Jackson explores how insecurity and fear of social judgement can cause people to avoid confronting uncomfortable truths about themselves and their families. Annotate:

  • Human insecurity and dissatisfaction – the ways the mother describes the changes in Laurie; anxiety about how others behave.

  • Social judgement – door slamming; creating mess; refusing to follow instructions or social norms.

  • Violence beneath the veneer of civilisation – violent actions; interest in violence.

Questions to consider:

  1. The narrator describes her son as ‘a long-trousered, swaggering character’. What other clues show that ‘Charles’ is really Laurie?

  2. How do both parents encourage the idea that misbehaviour is interesting and worth talking about?

  3. At the end, the mother suggests Laurie’s ‘trouble adjusting’ is due to Charles’s influence. What does this say about her belief that her son’s behaviour comes from outside influences?

Sentence starters: Many of Jackson’s characters fear social judgement and...; Characters in Jackson’s short stories frequently internalise the expectations of society and become harsh critics of...; The insecurity of characters like..., who believes...; The societal expectation to... puts pressure on...

‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’

Mrs Wilson makes a range of assumptions about the family life, finances and values of her son’s African-American friend, Boyd. This culminates in her angry reaction to his lack of gratitude for her ‘charitable’ offer of second-hand clothes, which Boyd and his family do not need because they are not, as she assumes, poor. Beyond exposing Mrs Wilson’s subtle racism, Jackson critiques the way people’s assumptions maintain social divisions and the established order. Annotate:

  • Communities enforce conformity through social pressure – food used to control behaviour and interactions.

  • Social judgement and assumptions – food and clothes representing social status and values.

Questions to consider:

  1. Johnny and Boyd play army tanks, ‘running over’ Japanese soldiers who fought against the United States in the Second World War. What does this suggest about how prejudice can be passed on to children?

  2. What assumptions does Mrs Wilson make about Boyd’s family, and what do they reveal about her world-view?

  3. Mrs Wilson says, ‘Don’t think I’m angry, Boyd. I’m just disappointed in you.’ What word more accurately describes how she feels?

The title. The boys use the phrase when they politely invite each other through a doorway. It comes from a comic strip in which two overly polite Frenchmen constantly defer to each other, satirising performative, superficial politeness that hides self-interest. Readings: the refined phrase challenges Mrs Wilson’s assumption that Boyd comes from an uneducated or disadvantaged background; the title highlights that her stereotypes are inaccurate and based on prejudice; it reflects manners and cultural awareness she does not expect Boyd to have; its exaggerated politeness mocks people like Mrs Wilson who perform kindness while holding racist attitudes; Jackson uses it ironically to expose the gap between outward politeness and genuine respect. Endings. Jackson’s stories often end with a brief, powerful line that reveals the protagonist’s emotional state, isolation or identity, leaving judgement, discomfort or tension. In ‘The Villager’, ‘Her shoulders ached’ suggests how burdened Miss Clarence has become by her attempt to maintain an artistic persona. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘No matter how often or how firmly she knocked, no one ever came to the door’ reinforces the protagonist’s isolation and suggests her search for connection, certainty and identity will remain unresolved. Try: In ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, Boyd’s concluding agreement that his mother is ‘screwy’ suggests that parents are...; At the very end, Boyd’s use of the catchphrase ‘After you, my dear Alphonse’ reinforces...

‘Flower Garden’

Mrs MacLane, a recent widow, moves to a country town, renovates a long-unoccupied cottage, grows a rich flower garden and befriends Mrs Winning. Mrs Winning is drawn to her lifestyle, seeing in the cottage and garden the life she might have had instead of the dreariness of her own home, where she lives mired in routine with her parents-in-law. But Mrs MacLane’s decision to hire an African-American man to help in her garden exposes the prejudice of the townspeople. Faced with being associated with Mrs MacLane’s transgression, Mrs Winning retreats to the safety of conforming to the town’s judgement. Annotate:

  • Communities enforcing conformity – schedules and routines; traditions; characters feeling helpless.

  • Insecurity and dissatisfaction – being alone; looking at and admiring other people’s belongings.

  • Unexpected events or strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – grey tones contrasted with images of colour; surprised reactions.

Questions to consider:

  1. The two Mrs Winnings ‘had grown to look a good deal alike’, and ‘uncounted Winning children had played with almost identical toys’. What do these details suggest about Mrs Winning’s sense of herself as an individual?

  2. Several characters tell Mrs Winning she should talk to Mrs MacLane ‘about that colored man’. No one ever says exactly what the problem is. What does this show about the power of unspoken attitudes in the town?

  3. Mrs Winning feels differently about walking up the hill to her house at different points. Describe two of these feelings and their causes.

Mrs Winning and Mrs MacLane. House: ‘This dreary old place’ against Mrs MacLane’s renovated cottage. Garden: the Winnings’ plain yard against a house surrounded by beautiful flowers. Child: Mrs Winning doesn’t show physical affection; Mrs MacLane’s son Davey becomes Howard’s friend. Husband: Mrs Winning lives under her husband’s family; Mrs MacLane is on her own now he is dead. Independence: Mrs MacLane ‘had some money in her own name’. Clothing: Mrs Winning puts ‘a scarf over her head’; Mrs MacLane wears a rose-coloured sweater, ‘a spot of color’ in the room. The townspeople’s role. When they advise Mrs Winning to speak to Mrs MacLane, which label fits best? Critics (people who judge and disapprove of others), followers (who go along with others’ opinions), gatekeepers (who decide who is accepted or excluded), cultural guardians (who protect a community’s traditions and values), insiders (people accepted within the group). The name ‘Winning’. ‘Winning’ means victory, yet Mrs Winning experiences the opposite at several points in the story. Vocabulary: the name reflects her desire to..., the name stands in contrast to..., her name is both..., the symbolism of her name reflects..., the connotations of her name; defeated by the reality of life, trapped by social expectations, confined by social convention, restricted by inherited traditions, unable to escape the pressures of society; the bittersweet victory of conformity, the safety of her position, the privilege of social approval, the quiet power of social acceptance, the rewards of compliance.

‘Elizabeth’

As one of Jackson’s clearly named characters, Elizabeth seems to know who she is and what she wants. But other characters call her ‘Miss Style’, ‘Liz’ and ‘Beth’, identities she clearly struggles with. She runs a literary agency with Robbie, an ineffectual man who bumbles through life and work, constantly relying on her for direction. The agency is struggling, weighed down by Robbie’s incompetence and his pattern of damaging relationships. By the end Elizabeth has had enough, arranging a meeting with an old client and imagining he may offer her a pathway to a better, more independent life. Annotate:

  • Conformity through years of habit – the rituals Elizabeth and Robbie share; meals and drinks.

  • Human isolation, insecurity and dissatisfaction – being alone; looking through windows; being separated by rooms, partitions or doors; grey tones contrasted with colour.

  • Strangers disrupting any sense of security – new and different colours, actions and behaviours.

  • A shifting sense of identity – imagining alternative lifestyles; thinking about the past; names.

Questions to consider:

  1. Elizabeth is woken from a dream: ‘Just before the alarm went off she was lying in a hot sunny garden’, and when she opened her eyes ‘it was raining’. How does this contrast show her dreaming of two different lives?

  2. Jackson calls her Elizabeth, but others call her ‘Liz’ or ‘Beth’. How do these names show the different roles she plays?

  3. At the end she tries to make her apartment more welcoming. What do her actions show about the life she was expected to lead and her current reality?

Gender. The story asks whether Elizabeth meets the expectations of 1940s femininity. Traits often coded as masculine: dismissive of emotional vulnerability, socially dominant, self-assured, uncomfortable with emotional intimacy, confident in public, defined by practicality rather than imagination. Traits often coded as feminine: emotionally perceptive and sensitive, anxious about social acceptance, conditioned to seek approval, responsible for domestic beauty and order, using imagination as escapism, attentive to appearances, dutiful to family. Elizabeth shows traits from both lists: she is a complex character trying to fit a world with restrictive norms around gender. Practice topic: How do Jackson’s short stories subvert traditional expectations of gender? Starters: Set in a world of conventional gender roles...; In Jackson’s stories, traditional gender roles are...; Jackson subverts every expectation of...; As they go about their lives, Jackson’s female characters feel constrained to...; The male characters in Jackson’s stories are bound by... + challenges, resists, disrupts, reframes, redefines, exposes the fragility of, rejects prescribed, unsettles the boundaries between or complies with, accepts, submits to, accommodates, adheres to, upholds, embraces the safety of, conforms outwardly, maintains appearances.

‘The Tooth’

One of the stranger stories in the collection, partly because much of the action is a dreamlike sequence. The protagonist travels to New York to see a dentist about her aching tooth, taking a great many painkillers. Her feverish dreams symbolise a different life she could live, and as the boundary between reality and fantasy blurs, Jackson alludes to the appeal of escaping an ordinary, suburban existence. Annotate:

  • Dissatisfaction, a shifting sense of identity and isolation – mirrors; physical appearances; torn, ripped or broken things; colourlessness contrasted with bright colours; dreams; uncertainty.

  • Strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – sudden consciousness and unconsciousness; strangers.

Questions to consider:

  1. Many stories open with a description of everyday routine. What does Jackson describe at the start of this story to show what normal life is like for the protagonist?

  2. For most of the story she is unnamed, until she finds the name ‘Clara’ written on one of her belongings. Clara means ‘clear’ or ‘bright’. What is significant about this?

  3. Jackson often ends with the protagonist trapped or dissatisfied. Is this how ‘The Tooth’ ends? Compare it with another story’s ending.

Jim, the man in the blue suit. The colour of his suit and his confidence contrast with her uncertainty and the greyness of her sense of self. He describes a place far removed from buses, truck-stop restaurants and dentists’ rooms: ‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, ‘nothing to do all day but lie under the trees’, ‘The grass is so green and so soft’. He evokes legends of Samarkand, an almost mythical place on the Silk Road, the ancient route along which traders carried cloth, spices, information and stories. Readings: she imagines Jim because he represents her desire to escape her ordinary, everyday life; Jim is simply a friendly stranger who comforts her; she uses the thought of Jim to transform herself into a different, more glamorous person; speaking lines of poetic beauty, Jim symbolises the possibility of becoming someone else. Doors, windows and mirrors. As in ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘Like Mother Used to Make’ and ‘The Villager’, doors and windows offer visions of a different life or opportunities, sometimes closed, that the protagonist could access. Vocabulary: thresholds into another self, glimpses of a parallel life, ...onto unrealised possibilities, ...to hidden identities, openings into alternate realities, portals to a different existence, reflections of lives not lived, moments of escape from..., windows into forbidden desires, doorways to self-discovery, glimpses beyond domesticity, openings into emotional truth, thresholds between reality and fantasy, ...onto hidden inner worlds, passages into uncertain futures, doorways to reinvention. Practice question: How does Jackson use doors and windows to reveal the dissatisfaction of her characters?

‘Of Course’

Mrs Harris moves in next door to Mrs Tylor. Mrs Tylor immediately profiles her and judges that the family must be ‘nice people, obviously’, another of Jackson’s characters wanting a world that safely conforms to her sense of what is normal and respectable. But Mrs Harris is not the kind of ‘nice’ person Mrs Tylor hoped for. She lists the forms of media and entertainment her family avoids, and Mrs Tylor becomes increasingly unsettled by their differences. Throughout her growing discomfort she can only answer ‘of course’, highlighting both her fear of open disagreement and the ease with which she conforms to her own sense of propriety. Annotate:

  • Communities enforce conformity through expectations and social pressure – approval of clothes and appearance; assumptions.

  • Unexpected events or strangers disrupting routines and assumptions – new people; housekeeping routines; other lifestyles.

Questions to consider:

  1. Mrs Tylor is ‘far too polite’ to go outside and stare at her new neighbours, yet she watches in a particular way as she does her chores. What does this tell us about what she thinks is ‘polite’?

  2. Mrs Tylor stays polite even as it becomes clear their values differ, while her daughter Carol behaves ‘sullenly’ towards the new neighbour’s son. Who is more honest about their feelings?

  3. At the end, Mrs Tylor says she will go with her daughter to the movies. Why is this significant in the context of the story?

Neighbourliness and conformity.

  • Mrs Tylor ‘recognized finally the faint nervous feeling that was tagging her; it was the way she felt when she was irrevocably connected with something dangerously out of control’ – characters feel uneasy when neighbours do not behave in predictable or conventional ways.

  • ‘Carol shut her mouth obstinately and the little boy edged behind his mother’ – neighbourly relationships can reveal the tension between personal choice and social expectation.

  • Mrs Tylor approves of the woman’s ‘nice-looking tan suit’: ‘Nice people, obviously’ – it is important for neighbours to fit accepted notions of respectability.

  • Mrs Tylor: ‘There’s something so nice about congenial people right next door’ – neighbourliness is meant to involve easy social interactions.

  • Mrs Harris: ‘it means such a lot to have really thoughtful neighbors’ – being a ‘good neighbour’ can mean behaving in ways others see as conforming to their values.

Vocabulary: social pressure, expectations, fitting in, acceptance, social norms, desire to belong, fear of judgement, expectations of neighbourly behaviour, pressure to appear polite, discomfort with difference; conventional, neighbourly, agreeable, uncomfortable, judgemental; highlights, reveals, challenges, resists, contrasts, suggests.

‘The Lottery’

A lottery is a form of gambling in which names or tickets are placed in a box and one is drawn at random, usually to win a prize. Because lotteries are so familiar, Jackson presents the ritual as calm, ordinary and completely accepted. The villagers gather, chat and wait as though taking part in a harmless community tradition, and this unsettling normality gives the story its disturbing atmosphere. Gradually the reader discovers that the ‘winner’ receives no reward but is violently sacrificed by the community. Through this shocking ending, Jackson shows how societies can destroy individuality and morality through blind conformity to tradition.

Character First half of the story End of the story
Tessie Hutchinson Arrives late, joking and chatting casually with neighbours. Becomes anxious and upset when her family is selected. Draws the marked paper and protests that it ‘isn’t fair’ as the villagers stone her.
Bill Hutchinson Quietly participates and draws the marked paper for his family in the first round. Forces the slip of paper out of Tessie’s hand, revealing the black spot.
Mr Summers Runs the lottery, as he runs the square dances and the teen club; worries about the shabby black box. Oversees the final selection and keeps the ritual moving efficiently.
Old Man Warner Defends the tradition and criticises villages that want to stop it. Urges the villagers on as they close in.
Mrs Delacroix Chats in a friendly way with Tessie at the start. Picks up a stone so large she needs both hands and helps kill Tessie.
Mr Adams Mentions that other villages are talking of giving up the lottery. Still participates in the stoning, at the front of the crowd.

Roles in a society: the characters can be labelled enforcer, traditionalist, scapegoat, bystander, organiser, follower, enabler, accomplice, dissenter, conformist, victim, outcast. Tessie as a transgressor. Although Tessie’s name is drawn at random, her early actions position her as a transgressor: she comes late because she ‘Clean forgot what day it was’. Forgetting the lottery suggests a small, perhaps unconscious, distance from the tradition that sets her slightly apart from the community before chance singles her out. The anchor story. ‘The Lottery’ powerfully represents almost every idea in the collection:

  • Isolation, insecurity and dissatisfaction: Tessie becomes completely isolated once the crowd turns against her; the villagers hide their fear and insecurity beneath cheerful small talk and routine.

  • Social judgement and exclusion: Tessie is cast out the moment she is chosen; compare Mrs MacLane, ostracised for hiring a Black gardener in ‘Flower Garden’, and Boyd, judged through racist stereotypes in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’.

  • Communities enforce conformity through traditions, rituals and social pressure: the villagers continue the lottery because social pressure and tradition discourage anyone from resisting.

  • Unexpected events disrupting routines and assumptions: Tessie’s selection suddenly transforms an ordinary community gathering into a horrifying event.

  • Violence beneath the veneer of civilised behaviour: neighbours, friends and family carry out a killing as calmly as a town meeting.

  • A shifting sense of identity: Tessie shifts from a cheerful member of the community to an isolated victim once she is selected.
