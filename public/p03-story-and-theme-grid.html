Use this grid when you plan a Section A essay. Find the idea in the topic, read down the column, and choose three or four stories: usually one or two where the idea is central, supported by others where it is present. Every rating below is based on what happens in the story itself, and the evidence under the grid shows where to find it. Key: •• = central to the story; • = present and usable; blank = not a strong choice for that idea.

Column headings: Insecurity = insecurity and dissatisfaction; Judgement = social judgement and exclusion; Unexpected = unexpected events and strangers expose our struggles; Violence = violence and cruelty beneath a civilised society; Conformity = conformity and tradition; Gender = gender roles, marriage and the domestic sphere; Children = children and how they learn adult values.

Insecurity and Dissatisfaction

Central: ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘The Villager’, ‘Flower Garden’, ‘Elizabeth’, ‘The Tooth’.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: she ‘was not satisfied with her clothes, her face, her apartment’, and pins her hopes on a marriage that never happens.

  • ‘The Villager’: her novel was read ‘up to page fifty and only carried now for effect’; the story ends, ‘Her shoulders ached.’

  • ‘Flower Garden’: Mrs Winning calls her home ‘This dreary old place’ and thinks of the cottage, ‘This could have been mine’.

  • ‘Elizabeth’: she believes ‘something wonderful would happen to change her whole life’.

  • ‘The Tooth’: Clara thinks, ‘you’d think my own face would know me’.

  • Present: David’s need for order in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’; Mrs Walpole’s resentment of her routine in ‘The Renegade’.

Social Judgement and Exclusion

Central: ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, ‘Flower Garden’, ‘Of Course’, ‘The Lottery’.

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: David is edged out of his own home, saying only ‘Guess I better be getting along, Marcia.’

  • ‘The Renegade’: Mrs Walpole, a newcomer, thinks of Mr White, ‘anyone would tell on a chicken-killing dog; but he doesn’t have to be so pleased about it’.

  • ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’: Mrs Wilson judges Boyd by race: ‘Boyd will eat anything.’

  • ‘Flower Garden’: the elder Mrs Winning says ‘you ought to talk to her about that colored man working there’; young Mrs Winning thinks, ‘pretty soon they’ll stop coming to me first, they’ll tell someone else to speak to me.’

  • ‘Of Course’: Mrs Tylor judges by appearances (‘Nice people, obviously’), then is judged in turn.

  • ‘The Lottery’: when Tessie protests, the crowd replies, ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’ and ‘All of us took the same chance.’

  • Present: the mocking strangers in ‘The Daemon Lover’; the mother scanning other mothers in ‘Charles’; the woman on the bus in ‘Elizabeth’ (‘Dried-up old maid’).

Unexpected Events and Strangers

Central: ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, ‘The Villager’, ‘The Witch’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘Flower Garden’, ‘Elizabeth’, ‘The Tooth’, ‘Of Course’. Almost every story has one, so choose the stories where the disruption reveals the most.

  • A man disappears: Jamie Harris in ‘The Daemon Lover’.

  • A man arrives: Mr Harris in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’ (‘I just thought I’d stop by’) and Harris in ‘The Villager’ (‘You’re Mrs. Roberts?’).

  • A stranger tells a story: the old man in ‘The Witch’ (‘shall I tell you about my little sister?’).

  • A phone call: ‘He’s been killing my chickens’ in ‘The Renegade’.

  • A newcomer: Mrs MacLane in ‘Flower Garden’; Daphne Hill in ‘Elizabeth’; the Harrises in ‘Of Course’.

  • A guide: ‘My name is Jim,’ in ‘The Tooth’.

  • Present: Boyd’s visit in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, where each fact about his family overturns Mrs Wilson’s assumptions; the revelation in ‘Charles’ (‘We don’t have any Charles in the kindergarten’); the second drawing in ‘The Lottery’.

Violence and Cruelty Beneath a Civilised Society

Central: ‘The Witch’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘The Lottery’.

  • ‘The Witch’: a pleasant elderly man in a blue suit says he ‘pinched her and I pinched her until she was dead’.

  • ‘The Renegade’: the twins explain the spiked collar: ‘The spikes cut her head off.’

  • ‘The Lottery’: ‘Although the villagers had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones.’

  • Present (cruelty rather than physical violence): the jeering strangers in ‘The Daemon Lover’; Laurie’s invented violence in ‘Charles’; the boys’ war game in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ (‘We stand them in the ground and run over them with tanks’); Howard’s racist name-calling and the town’s freezing-out of Mrs MacLane in ‘Flower Garden’; Elizabeth’s treatment of Daphne.

Conformity and Tradition

Central: ‘The Renegade’, ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, ‘Flower Garden’, ‘Of Course’, ‘The Lottery’.

  • ‘The Lottery’: ‘There’s always been a lottery’; ‘no one liked to upset even as much tradition as was represented by the black box.’

  • ‘Flower Garden’: after eleven years the two Mrs Winnings ‘had grown to look a good deal alike’; she is ‘officially a Winning’.

  • ‘The Renegade’: the grocer insists, ‘Won’t do to have a dog going around killing chickens, Mrs. Walpole.’

  • ‘Of Course’: Mrs Tylor keeps saying ‘Of course’, ‘feeling as though she had been saying nothing else all morning’.

  • ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’: Mrs Wilson’s charity follows the assumptions of her society, not the facts in front of her.

  • Present: the pressure to marry in ‘The Daemon Lover’; David’s surrender to politeness in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’; the mother’s lollipop rewards in ‘The Witch’.

Gender Roles, Marriage and the Domestic Sphere

Central: ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘Like Mother Used to Make’.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: she begins a letter, ‘Anne, by the time you get this I will be married.’

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: David’s pride in his home (‘it pleased him to have only one key to his home’) is taken over by Marcia, who claims the pie: ‘I haven’t made but two, three pies before’.

  • Present: ‘The Villager’ (Harris: ‘What I need is a wife.’); ‘The Renegade’ (Mrs Walpole’s morning revolves around her husband and children); ‘Flower Garden’ (Mrs Harris: ‘Let that husband of yours do the housework for a change.’); ‘Elizabeth’ (‘Because they were not married, Robbie was reluctant to take her anywhere’); ‘The Tooth’ (Clara’s last instruction to her husband is about the milkman and eggs); ‘Of Course’ (the absent Mr Harris sets every rule); ‘The Lottery’ (‘Wife draws for her husband’).

Children and How They Learn Adult Values

Central: ‘The Witch’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘Charles’, ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, ‘The Lottery’.

  • ‘The Witch’: Johnny is delighted, not frightened: ‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’

  • ‘The Renegade’: the twins describe the collar while Jack kisses the dog’s head.

  • ‘Charles’: Laurie invents Charles to report his own behaviour; the teacher: ‘We don’t have any Charles in the kindergarten.’

  • ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’: the boys have not absorbed Mrs Wilson’s prejudice. Johnny: ‘She’s screwy sometimes.’ Boyd: ‘So’s mine.’

  • ‘The Lottery’: ‘someone gave little Davy Hutchinson a few pebbles.’

  • Present: Howard’s name-calling in ‘Flower Garden’; Carol’s ‘I want to go to the movies’ in ‘Of Course’.

Strong Combinations

  • Community against an outsider: ‘The Renegade’, ‘Flower Garden’, ‘The Lottery’ (with ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ for prejudice in a single home).

  • Women and the life they hoped for: ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘The Villager’, ‘Elizabeth’, ‘The Tooth’.

  • The intruder who exposes the self: ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, ‘The Villager’, ‘The Witch’, ‘Of Course’.

  • Children as mirrors of adults: ‘The Witch’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘Charles’, ‘The Lottery’.
