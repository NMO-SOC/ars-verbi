Values in Picnic at Hanging Rock

Social Values

Order, Rules and Respectability

Appleyard College values discipline, punctuality and propriety above all. Mrs Appleyard’s instructions before the picnic about gloves, ‘tomboy foolishness’, snakes and returning ‘at about 8’ show a world that tries to anticipate and control every risk. The film questions whether these rules create real order or only the appearance of it: when the girls vanish, the rules offer no answer, and the institution begins to collapse.

Class and Social Hierarchy

Wealth and status decide who belongs. Sara, an orphan whose fees are ‘impossibly overdue’, is excluded from the picnic and treated harshly; Mrs Appleyard insists the college is not a ‘charitable institution’. Michael and Albert’s friendship across the class divide stands out as a rare challenge to these values.

Femininity and Restraint

The girls are dressed in white, corseted and gloved, praised for beauty and refinement. The film links this ideal of femininity to repression: the girls who climb the Rock shed their shoes, stockings and gloves, and the community’s reaction to the disappearance focuses obsessively on bodies and sexual danger.

Cultural Values

Rationality and Science

The characters trust measurement, investigation and explanation. Miss McCraw studies geometry and geology; Sergeant Bumpher interviews witnesses and organises searches; the doctor examines Irma. The film presents a world that does not yield to these methods, suggesting the limits of a purely rational worldview.

European Culture in the Australian Landscape

The college is a piece of England transplanted to the bush: Botticelli prints, Valentine’s poetry, cultivated flowers, high tea and classical music. Weir sets this imported culture against an ancient landscape it does not understand, revealing the colonists’ sense of being outsiders in the land they occupy.

Romanticism and the Sublime

Some characters, especially Miranda and Michael, are drawn to nature as something beautiful, spiritual and liberating. This Romantic longing for connection with nature coexists with fear: the Rock is both enchanting and terrifying.

Key Ideas and Vocabulary

The film can be strange and confusing, so it helps to know the main ideas before you watch. The most fundamental idea is that the natural world is powerful, overwhelming and mysterious, while the human world tries to measure, quantify and make rules to create a sense of control. Weir’s film explores an event that challenges every illusion of control his characters have. The vocabulary below will help you write about each key idea with precision.

Nature as a Fundamental Force

From the opening image of the Rock, the audience sees nature as monumental and powerful. But it is not only the Rock: Weir provides many vignettes of trees, birds, lizards and insects that present the varied, multifaceted natural world beyond human control. Some characters, like Edith, are afraid of and repelled by the untamed bush; others, like Miranda, are drawn to it, literally walking away from the constraints of the human world. Every other idea in the film relates to nature, so any essay on Picnic at Hanging Rock needs to analyse nature in some way.

  • Nature: natural environment, landscape, wilderness, Australian bush, surrounding landscape, untamed world, world of nature, natural realm, surrounding wilderness.

  • Nature as a mysterious force: elemental forces of nature, impenetrability of the wilderness, untameable power of nature, overwhelming nature of the wilderness, primordial landscape, inscrutable forces of nature, unknowable dimensions of the wilderness, enigmatic world of the Australian landscape, ancient and indifferent landscape.

The Human Need for Rationality and Control

Because life and nature are so overwhelming, people quantify, measure and control the world around them so that they feel powerful and clever. Throughout the film, characters try to explain the mystery of the disappearance, while others recognise the limits of human knowledge. Marion and Miss McCraw measure, calculate and put numbers on the world. As the party approaches the Rock, there is a short discussion about how old it is, as though knowing its age will help them understand it.

  • Order: structure, stability, certainty, predictability, clarity, understanding, balance, control, rationality, coherence.

  • Chaos: disorder, uncertainty, unpredictability, confusion, mystery, wilderness, irrationality.

  • Desire for knowledge: need to understand, the importance of learning, feeling of expertise, prioritisation of education, promotion of intellect, the search for reason, accumulation of information, desire to comprehend, need to make sense.

  • Measurement: quantification, imposition of order, reduction of data, transforming the unknowable, a means of establishing order, faith in mathematical understanding.

Class and Civilisation

As part of the effort to measure and control the world, humans have created ‘civilisation’, the opposite of the natural world. Appleyard College is its obvious symbol: a monumental colonial building surrounded by a manicured lawn in the middle of the Australian bush. Even nature is controlled there: plants grow in a greenhouse and exotic flowers are arranged in vases. Costume carries the same idea: girls are strapped into corsets every morning, policemen wear brightly polished buttons, Mrs Appleyard’s hair is a carefully coiffed beehive. The characters are arranged into serving classes and ruling classes. Everyone has rules to follow, and the rules make them feel they have tamed the world. As Weir reveals, they have not.

  • The ordered world of Appleyard College: carefully regulated environment, controlled world of the school, a world governed by rules and expectations, institutional world of Appleyard College, the structured life of the schoolgirls, artificial order of the school environment, the confined world of Appleyard College, the institutional structures that govern the girls, the school as a symbol of civilisation.

  • The social structure of Edwardian society: the rigid social structures of the time, conventions of polite society, social codes of the 1900s, the social mores of the time, constraints of the civilised world, accepted behaviours and conventions, the hierarchical nature of Edwardian society, the conventions of British colonial culture, the values of colonial society.

Time

Time is a tool of measurement, so it belongs with rationality and control, but it is important enough to deserve its own place. The characters are obsessed with time: the girls disappear on a specified date, there are clocks and watches everywhere, and the characters are disturbed when they stop. In contrast stands the natural world, which operates on a scale that is almost incomprehensible: the Rock is millions of years old. Weir also plays with the audience’s sense of time by slowing some of the action down and presenting it in a dreamlike, timeless way.

  • Human-scale time: temporal order on the world, systems of clocks, schedules and routines, linear notions of time, time as quantifiable, time as a boundary that separates and defines, linear progression of human life, human attempts to organise the passage of time, the measured rhythm of civilisation.

  • Time in the natural world: collapse of established routines, cyclical and fluid nature of time, a timescale that transcends human experience, transcending human life, shifting and deceptive in the natural world, geological time, the eternal cycles of nature, the ageless landscape.
