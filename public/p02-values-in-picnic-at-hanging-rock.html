Values in Picnic at Hanging Rock

Social Values

Order, Rules and Respectability

Appleyard College values discipline, punctuality and propriety above all. Mrs Appleyard’s instructions before the picnic about gloves, ‘tomboy foolishness’, snakes and returning ‘at about 8’ show a world that tries to anticipate and control every risk. The film questions whether these rules create real order or only the appearance of it: when the girls vanish, the rules offer no answer, and the institution begins to collapse.

Class and Social Hierarchy

Wealth and status decide who belongs. Sara, an orphan whose fees are ‘impossibly overdue’, is excluded from the picnic and treated harshly; Mrs Appleyard insists the college is not a ‘charitable institution’. Michael and Albert’s friendship across the class divide stands out as a rare challenge to these values.

Femininity and Restraint

The girls are dressed in white, corseted and gloved, praised for beauty and refinement. The film links this ideal of femininity to repression: the girls who climb the Rock shed their shoes, stockings and gloves, and the community’s reaction to the disappearance focuses obsessively on bodies and sexual danger.

Cultural Values

Rationality and Science

The characters trust measurement, investigation and explanation. Miss McCraw studies geometry and geology; Sergeant Bumpher interviews witnesses and organises searches; the doctor examines Irma. The film presents a world that does not yield to these methods, suggesting the limits of a purely rational worldview.

European Culture in the Australian Landscape

The college is a piece of England transplanted to the bush: Botticelli prints, Valentine’s poetry, cultivated flowers, high tea and classical music. Weir sets this imported culture against an ancient landscape it does not understand, revealing the colonists’ sense of being outsiders in the land they occupy.

Romanticism and the Sublime

Some characters, especially Miranda and Michael, are drawn to nature as something beautiful, spiritual and liberating. This Romantic longing for connection with nature coexists with fear: the Rock is both enchanting and terrifying.

Key Ideas and Vocabulary

Weir’s film can feel strange on first viewing, so it helps to go in knowing the ideas it keeps returning to. At its centre is a collision: on one side an ancient landscape that is vast and beyond explanation, on the other a colonial society that runs on clocks, rules and categories. The disappearance at the Rock tests every one of those systems, and none of them holds. The word lists below give you precise language for each idea.

Nature as a Fundamental Force

The film opens on the Rock before it shows a single person, and Weir keeps returning to it as a looming, brooding presence. He also fills the frame with smaller life: ants swarming over the picnic cake, a lizard on a boulder, birds calling, the Rock’s surface in close-up. Together these shots build a living world that exists entirely on its own terms. Characters split in how they respond. Edith finds the bush threatening and wants to stay put, while Miranda moves deeper into it, shedding her shoes and stockings as she goes. Because the other ideas in the film are all defined against the natural world, an essay on Picnic at Hanging Rock will almost always need to say something about it.

  • Nature: natural environment, landscape, wilderness, Australian bush, surrounding landscape, untamed world, world of nature, natural realm, surrounding wilderness.

  • Nature as a mysterious force: elemental forces of nature, impenetrability of the wilderness, untameable power of nature, overwhelming nature of the wilderness, primordial landscape, inscrutable forces of nature, unknowable dimensions of the wilderness, enigmatic world of the Australian landscape, ancient and indifferent landscape.

The Human Need for Rationality and Control

Faced with a world they cannot fully grasp, the characters reach for numbers, rules and explanations. Miss McCraw calculates and explains the Rock’s volcanic origin; Marion talks about people as if they were figures in a sum; Mr Hussey checks his watch; Sergeant Bumpher asks whether there was a man. After the disappearance the whole community wants an account that makes sense, and the film refuses to supply one. Notice how often a character offers a fact, a date or a theory as if it could tame what has happened.

  • Order: structure, stability, certainty, predictability, clarity, understanding, balance, control, rationality, coherence.

  • Chaos: disorder, uncertainty, unpredictability, confusion, mystery, wilderness, irrationality.

  • Desire for knowledge: need to understand, the importance of learning, feeling of expertise, prioritisation of education, promotion of intellect, the search for reason, accumulation of information, desire to comprehend, need to make sense.

  • Measurement: quantification, imposition of order, reduction of data, transforming the unknowable, a means of establishing order, faith in mathematical understanding.

Class and Civilisation

Colonial society sets itself up as the opposite of the bush. Appleyard College is the clearest example: a grand building with clipped lawns, placed in the middle of the Victorian countryside as if it could hold the landscape at bay. Inside, even nature is managed, from the plants in the glasshouse to the flowers arranged in vases. The same idea runs through costume and hierarchy: the girls in stockings and gloves, the police in polished buttons, Mrs Appleyard’s elaborately dressed hair, the maids and grooms who serve the gentry. Each layer of rules gives people the sense that the world has been tamed. The disappearance shows how thin that sense is.

  • The ordered world of Appleyard College: the college’s routines and regulations, an institution built on discipline, the timetabled life of the boarders, a manicured enclave in the bush, the college as an outpost of empire, the rule-bound world of the school, the protective walls of the institution, the headmistress’s regime.

  • The social structure of Edwardian society: a fixed class hierarchy, the manners of respectable society, colonial propriety, the codes of gentility, inherited rank and privilege, the expectations placed on young ladies, the divide between masters and servants, the imported values of Britain.

Time

Time could sit under rationality and control, since a clock is just another way of measuring, but the film gives it so much attention that it is worth treating on its own. The picnic happens on a precise date, watches and clocks appear again and again, and their stopping at the Rock disturbs everyone who notices. Against these human hours the Rock offers geological time, measured in millions of years. Weir also bends the audience’s own experience of time, using slow motion, dissolves and long, drifting shots so that the climb feels suspended outside ordinary hours.

  • Human-scale time: clock time, timetables and appointments, punctuality, the calendar, time as something to be counted and owned, deadlines and routines, the brief span of a human life, the steady march of the school day.

  • Time in the natural world: geological ages, deep time, stillness, suspended or frozen time, time that loops or stops, an ancient and enduring landscape, timelessness, rhythms older than human history.
