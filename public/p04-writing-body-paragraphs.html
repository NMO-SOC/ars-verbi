Body Paragraphs

The body paragraphs form majority of your essay. The body paragraphs are the main reasons that the contention your provided is true. Your body paragraphs need to be able to demonstrate your understanding of the text and the fluency of your language. You should ensure that every sentence (and thus every word) brings something towards your argument.

Structure

Below is the structure for a body paragraph using ‘Th.E. S.A.M.L.’ Method. This method of writing body paragraphs was developed in order to maximise possible marks. Only the ‘TH’ and ‘L’ elements of this structure need to be in the order given.

Th
Thematic-based topic sentence

Your topic sentence needs to be based upon a theme in the text, not a character.

✗ ‘Tessie Hutchinson is stoned to death by the villagers.’
✓ ‘Jackson suggests that blind loyalty to tradition allows ordinary people to commit extraordinary cruelty.’
E
Evidence

Quotes and scenes from the text that support the theme.

S
Symbols

Symbols that relate to this theme — including historical and social context, and setting.

A
Authorial intention

What was the author intending?

M
Metalanguage

Discuss the metalanguage the author uses around this theme.

L
Linking sentence

Link back to your contention.

Only the Th and L elements need to appear in this order — the middle elements can be woven through the paragraph.

Basic Example ‘Th.E S.A.M’

Jackson suggests that blind loyalty to tradition allows ordinary people to commit extraordinary cruelty. In ‘The Lottery’, Old Man Warner insists that “There’s always been a lottery”, dismissing the villages that have given it up as a “Pack of crazy fools”. The battered black box, which the villagers refuse to replace even as it grows “shabbier each year”, symbolises a ritual whose meaning has decayed while its authority remains. Writing in the years after the Second World War, Jackson warns that cruelty is most dangerous when it is habitual and shared. Her flat, matter-of-fact narration of the stoning, in which “someone gave little Davy Hutchinson a few pebbles”, makes the violence more disturbing by presenting it as ordinary. In this way, Jackson shows that loyalty to tradition can blind a community to its own cruelty.

Hinge Sentences

In your topic sentence, you can introduce two related ideas. This creates room for a more complex, insightful paragraph. But remember: when you shift focus from the first idea to the second, you must signal this change to the reader clearly. This is where the hinge sentence comes in. A hinge sentence acts like a bridge: it acknowledges what you’ve already explored, then transitions to the second idea you introduced in your topic sentence. Here’s how that works:

By charting Mrs Winning’s retreat from her friendship with Mrs MacLane, Jackson conveys how the fear of exclusion pushes characters to abandon their own judgement, ultimately suggesting that a community which rewards conformity turns its members into the enforcers of its prejudice.

Evidence + Analysis of First Idea

In ‘Flower Garden’, Mrs Winning’s friendship with Mrs MacLane survives only until the town begins to disapprove of Mrs MacLane hiring Mr Jones. When her mother-in-law urges her to “talk to her about that colored man working there”, Mrs Winning’s private thoughts reveal what really moves her: “pretty soon they’ll stop coming to me first, they’ll tell someone else to speak to me.” Jackson shows that her concern is not for her friend, or even for what is right, but for her own place in the community.

Hinge Sentence (transitioning to Second idea)

However, Jackson does not leave Mrs Winning as a mere victim of social pressure; she shows her becoming its agent.

Analysis of Second Idea

When Mrs MacLane asks whether the town’s coldness is “because of Mr. Jones working here”, Mrs Winning denies it, then privately thinks, “The nerve of her, trying to blame the colored folks.” Having absorbed the town’s prejudice, she now voices it as her own. Her final gesture, as she “swung around without speaking and started, with great dignity, back up the hill toward the old Winning house”, is deeply ironic: the “dignity” she preserves is the dignity of belonging, bought at the cost of her friend. By using a hinge sentence, you show your reader that you’re not just stacking ideas but developing an argument, deepening your interpretation, and showing the author’s nuanced message.
