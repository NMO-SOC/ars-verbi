Language and Style

On this page
  • Everyday Detail
  • Dialogue and Polite Speech
  • Irony
  • Unnamed and Symbolic Names

An Understated, Ordinary Voice

Jackson writes in a plain, calm, almost conversational style. She describes terrible things in the same tone as a morning’s errands. In ‘The Lottery’, the stoning is introduced through the same matter-of-fact narration as the gathering of the villagers and their chat about tractors and taxes. This flatness is deliberate: by refusing to signal horror, Jackson makes the reader feel the horror of how normal cruelty has become.

Everyday Detail

Jackson builds her worlds from small, concrete details: coffee, dishes in the sink, the neat chairs in David’s apartment, a lollipop, a bus ticket. These details make the settings feel real and familiar, which is what makes the disruptions so unsettling. They also carry meaning: the protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’ repeatedly finds ‘nothing to eat’, and her lack of appetite suggests the emptiness of her life.

Dialogue and Polite Speech

Characters in Jackson’s stories are often unfailingly polite while saying cruel or prejudiced things. Mrs Wilson’s generosity in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ is full of assumptions; the neighbours in ‘The Renegade’ cheerfully suggest ways to kill a dog; Mrs Harris in ‘Of Course’ imposes her views through seemingly friendly conversation. Listen for the gap between the tone of what is said and what it means.

Irony

Irony runs through the collection: a lottery whose ‘prize’ is death; a sunny summer day for a murder; a title, ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, that mocks performative politeness; a mother in ‘Charles’ who never suspects the badly behaved boy is her own son.

Unnamed and Symbolic Names

Some characters have no name at all: the protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’, ‘the little boy’ in ‘The Witch’. This makes them seem anonymous and interchangeable, as if their stories could belong to anyone. Other names are pointed: Laurie suggests a crown of laurels, a symbol of glory; Walpole has been read as meaning ‘foreigner’; Clara means ‘clear’ or ‘bright’; Mrs Winning belongs to the family that always wins.

Setting and Weather

Jackson often sets disturbing events against pleasant weather. The village of ‘The Lottery’ gathers on a ‘clear and sunny’ morning ‘with the fresh warmth of a full-summer day’. Ask whether the weather in a story reinforces ordinary life, contrasts with it, mirrors the characters’ feelings, or creates an ironic contrast with them.

Open Endings

Jackson rarely explains. We never learn what happened to Jamie Harris, who the man on the train really was, or where Clara Spencer is going. The reader is left uneasy, and must decide for themselves what the story reveals.
