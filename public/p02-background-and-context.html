Overview

Peter Weir’s Picnic at Hanging Rock (1975) opens with a title card dating its events to St Valentine’s Day, 1900: a party of schoolgirls from Appleyard College picnics at Hanging Rock, near Mount Macedon in Victoria, and several members of the party disappear without trace. The film never explains what happened. Instead, it studies how a rigid, ordered colonial society reacts when something happens that its rules, clocks and explanations cannot contain. Knowing where the story comes from, when it is set and how it was made will help you write about Weir’s choices rather than just the plot.

Joan Lindsay’s Novel

The film is adapted from Joan Lindsay’s 1967 novel of the same name. Lindsay deliberately blurred fact and fiction, presenting the disappearance as if it might have happened and declining to say whether it did. That ambiguity became part of the story’s legend: many readers and viewers came to believe the events were real. A final chapter that Lindsay cut from the novel, offering a strange, quasi-supernatural resolution, was published after her death; it plays no part in the film.

Analysis

You are studying the film, not the novel. Weir keeps Lindsay’s refusal to resolve the mystery and makes it the centre of the viewing experience: the audience, like the characters, is left wanting an answer that never arrives. When you write about ‘unresolved mysteries’, remember that this is a deliberate construction, not a gap.

Peter Weir and the Australian New Wave

Picnic at Hanging Rock was one of the defining films of the Australian New Wave, a revival of local filmmaking in the 1970s supported by new government film funding. Directed by Peter Weir from a screenplay by Cliff Green, it was a major critical and commercial success and helped bring Australian cinema to international audiences. Appleyard College was filmed at Martindale Hall in South Australia, while the picnic and search scenes were shot at Hanging Rock itself.

Analysis

The New Wave was partly about Australians telling stories about their own landscape and history. Weir’s film looks back at colonial society with a mixture of beauty and unease: the European culture of the college looks lovely but out of place against the ancient Australian bush. This tension between imported civilisation and the land is at the heart of most essay topics.

Victoria in 1900

In 1900 Victoria was still a British colony; Federation would come on 1 January 1901. Wealthy colonial society modelled itself on Britain: its schools taught deportment, French, poetry and the accomplishments expected of young ladies, and its social life was governed by strict rules about class, dress and behaviour. Girls were laced into corsets, gloved and hatted outdoors, and expected to be decorative, obedient and chaste. Class divisions were sharp. The Fitzhuberts are landed gentry; Albert, their groom, is a working man who grew up in an orphanage; Sara, his sister, is a ward whose fees go unpaid. Mrs Appleyard’s college depends on wealthy parents’ money and on its reputation for respectability.

Analysis

The rules of this society explain much of what we see: the gloves Mrs Appleyard allows the girls to remove only ‘once the drag has passed through Woodend’, the horror at Miss McCraw being seen without her skirt, the police assumption that the explanation must be a man. Weir shows how fragile these conventions are once they meet something beyond them.

Hanging Rock

Hanging Rock is a volcanic formation in the Macedon Ranges, north-west of Melbourne, and lies on the traditional lands of the Dja Dja Wurrung, Taungurung and Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung peoples. Geologically it is relatively young, a point Miss McCraw makes in the film, but in human terms it is ancient. Its jagged pinnacles, narrow passages and balanced boulders give it the maze-like quality Weir’s camera exploits.

Analysis

The colonial characters treat the Rock as a picnic spot and a curiosity to be measured. The film suggests that it existed long before them and will outlast them. The settlers’ lack of any real relationship with the land, including the Indigenous history they overlook, is part of what makes it seem so alien and threatening to them.

The Look and Sound of the Film

Cinematographer Russell Boyd gave the film its dreamy, soft-focus look, with diffused light, slow motion and lingering shots of the landscape. The soundtrack mixes Gheorghe Zamfir’s haunting pan flute, original music by Bruce Smeaton, European classical pieces such as the slow movement of Beethoven’s Fifth Piano Concerto, and an unsettling low rumble associated with the Rock.

Analysis

These choices create the film’s central tension between beauty and menace. The images are lovely, almost like paintings, but the sound and pacing keep the viewer uneasy. See the Film Techniques section for how to use them in essays.
