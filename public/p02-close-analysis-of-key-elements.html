Because there is little dialogue or character development in this film, you need to spend most of your time analysing setting, costume, objects, sound and structure. The notes below work through the key elements of the film in order, with evidence, possible interpretations and sentence starters for turning them into analysis.

The Opening and Closing Scenes

The film opens with a prologue card: On Saturday 14th February 1900 a party of schoolgirls from Appleyard College picnicked at Hanging Rock near Mt. Macedon in the state of Victoria. During the afternoon several members of the party disappeared without trace... This statement sets up the central mystery, but it also suggests a historical event, even though the story is invented. The credits then play over the Rock emerging as if from the mists of time, establishing it as a monument to the endurance of nature, all in under a minute and a half. The film ends with a deep voice explaining what happens to Mrs Appleyard and her college, and noting that the missing girls were never found. The final images are quasi-historical footage of the picnic, in disjointed camera work that further suggests the historical ‘truth’ of the film. Throughout the film, Weir contrasts human lives with the natural world: humans need to understand, measure and create rules for their lives, but nature is impervious to all of them. The scenes that bookend the film make this contrast clear.

  • The prologue outlining a specific date implies that humans need to measure and quantify experiences so that they can understand them.

  • Placing the events in a real, geographical space creates the sense of a real, human tragedy that is recognisable and understandable.

  • The Rock gradually emerging from the mist hints at the permanence of the natural world, as opposed to the ephemerality of human lives.

  • Clouds passing slowly over the immutable Rock suggest that nature is timeless and immeasurable against human lives.

  • The final voice-over detailing the fates of the characters creates the impression of events that can be quantified and understood.

  • The disjointed, quasi-historical style of the final footage lends the film a reassuring sense of reality in an otherwise mysterious and confusing narrative.

  • The lingering final images of the girls reinforce the idea that nature is mysterious and unknowable.

Contrasting the Opening and Closing

The two sequences are similar, but not identical. Some statements describe both; others suit one more than the other. Ideas to sort and compare:

  • although people like to think of themselves as separate from nature, they are an integral part of the environment;

  • the beauty of nature is reflected in the youthful grace of the girls;

  • statements of fact are like natural features: fixed, measurable and knowable;

  • human events sit within the natural landscape;

  • nature possesses a power that exceeds human control;

  • humanity is fragile when contrasted with the strength of nature;

  • nature exists outside human understanding;

  • nature exposes the limitations of human knowledge;

  • knowledge is as stable and reliable as a rock.

Sentence building: The film’s framing scenes / In the opening and concluding moments / Both the opening and the closing of the film / The scenes that bookend Weir’s film / By framing his film with scenes that... + share / mirror / reflect / echo + the sense that / a concern with / the idea that / the perception of / a view that / an understanding of... + however / yet / despite this / on the other hand / in contrast...

Picnicking

It seems the only reason anyone goes to Hanging Rock is to picnic. Michael is there with his aunt and uncle (and Albert, although as a servant he has to eat on his own), and the girls of Appleyard College have their own picnic. Both groups eat in a highly ritualised fashion. Michael and his family gather around a tiny table, sipping tea from cups and saucers and staring into space. The girls offer up a toast to ‘St. Valentine’ as Miranda ritualistically cuts the cake. All of this says a great deal about the conventions and customs of the time. Weir deliberately juxtaposes the elements of human civilisation against the ancient bushland. As you watch, look for human objects that seem out of place in the wilderness: the tablecloth, china cups and saucers, the cake, white dresses, parasols, gloves and hats, the drag and its harnessed horses, Mr Hussey’s watch. Word bank for incongruity: are presented as incongruous against / contrast sharply with / appear discordant with / appear at odds with / provide an intrusive presence in / seem strangely detached from + the ancient landscape / this untamed wilderness / the elemental forces of nature / this rugged terrain / this expansive bushland / a world remote from civilisation. Sentence starters: As the characters eat...; In the picnicking scenes...; When the characters picnic...; In comparison to the upper-class characters, who eat..., Albert...

After the Meal

After they have finished eating, Edith looks at Michael’s family and remarks that they ‘might be the only living creatures in the whole world’. Weir then focuses on a group of ants who seem to be having their own picnic of the St Valentine’s cake. By zooming in on the ants, Weir reminds us that the human picnic is only one small event in a teeming natural world that carries on regardless of it, and that civilisation’s rituals are no defence against nature reclaiming what humans leave behind. Having eaten, the humans show how ‘civilised’ they are through actions only humans perform:

  • Mr Hussey wants to know the time.

  • Irma talks about wearing diamonds in the bath.

  • Marion asks if she can take measurements of the Rock.

  • Miss McCraw reads.

  • Mademoiselle compares a human girl to a piece of art.

These actions can be read in several ways: human beings are driven by a desire to make sense of their surroundings; people seek order in an otherwise chaotic world; individuals strive to categorise and define the world around them; the pursuit of knowledge reflects a deep-seated need for control; people feel compelled to shape the unfamiliar into a meaningful pattern. Topic sentence to develop: Weir’s film examines the human impulse to impose a civilised order that is at odds with the natural world. Linking phrases: Throughout the picnicking scene...; Weir depicts...; In doing so, Weir demonstrates...; This suggests that...; This implies that...; This is reinforced by...; Furthermore...; Similarly...; In addition...; Weir further emphasises this through...

Thresholds

A threshold is a border between two places; a doorway, for example, is a threshold between the inside of a house and the outside world. Thresholds matter in Picnic at Hanging Rock because there are two distinct worlds in the film: the civilised, confined and restricted world of Appleyard College, and the wild, transcendent natural world of Hanging Rock. To pass from one world to the other, the girls cross physical thresholds that symbolise their transition from one way of thinking and behaving to another.

  • Threshold: boundary, transition, barrier between, liminal space, passage from... to..., crossing over from... to..., the demarcation between, entry into a world of..., leaving behind.

  • Civilised world: sanctioned space, the known, the familiar, order, control, institutional world, safety, routine, obedience.

  • Natural world: the unknown, wilderness, discovery, freedom, instinct, the primal self, otherness, ungoverned nature, untamed nature.

The Gate

When the girls arrive at the base of the Rock there is a physical gate they must pass through. The symbol is simple, but Weir adds extra layers of meaning to the action of opening a gate between two worlds:

  • Immediately before the gate scene, Michael and Albert, men from different classes, share a drink.

  • Miranda, Irma and Marion, the three girls who go missing, sit at the head of the drag. Miranda leaps from the drag to open the gate.

  • Miranda removes her hat, leaving it in the drag, to open the gate.

  • Just before Miranda opens the gate, there is a close-up of a decrepit notice board, its bulletins unreadable from age and weathering.

  • Immediately after the gate is opened, Miranda sees a group of birds flying through the sky, and the harnessed horses rear and cry out.

Sentence starters: When the schoolgirls first arrive at the base of the Rock, Miranda opens a gate, symbolising...; Weir reinforces the significance of this symbol by..., which highlights...; Moreover...

The Stream

When Miranda, Marion, Irma and Edith set out to climb the Rock (which they were warned not to do), they must cross a stream. Albert, watching, makes a crude, sexualised remark about the girls’ appearance. Miranda throws a flower into the stream as she crosses, almost like an offering. Weir then provides a close-up of the stream, revealing its murky, organic naturalness. Possible interpretations:

  • Miranda’s flower offering suggests the girls are not simply crossing water: they are entering a kind of ritual, perhaps even making a pact with the natural world.

  • Albert’s voyeuristic commentary frames the crossing as a moment of exposure, as if crossing the stream strips away the girls’ protection and makes them vulnerable.

  • The murkiness of the stream suggests obscured vision, foreshadowing the incomprehensible nature of what is about to occur.

  • The doubling of thresholds creates a sense of inevitability, as though the Rock is drawing the girls towards it through successive layers of boundary.

The Rocks

The final threshold is the rocks themselves. At the top, the girls find not a single rock but an immense series of rocks that have formed into tunnels and passageways. Miranda, Marion and Irma disappear through one such passageway at the end of the scene. Words to describe how Weir presents the rocks: maze-like, labyrinthine, mysterious, disorienting, enthralling, drawing in, deep, cavernous, immersive, consuming, primordial, magnetic, twisting, spiralling, hypnotic, powerful; the rocks form a pathway or entrance way. Sentence frame: When the girls get to the top, Weir depicts the rocks as... and..., symbolising how they offer a threshold to a world that is...

Time Stops

After the picnic, the girls lounge about and one recites Shakespeare’s sonnet ‘Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day’, a poem full of references to time: seasons, months, days. At about this point Mr Hussey notices that his watch has stopped. Even though he says they need to be careful of the time, the four girls head up the Rock for the purpose of measuring it. On the Rock they gossip about Sara, and Irma compares her to a ‘doomed’ baby deer. Then they move in slow motion, taking off their shoes and stockings. Each character’s words reveal an attitude towards time:

  • Miranda: ‘Don’t wear it anymore. Can’t stand it ticking above my heart.’ – she feels constrained by human constructs of time and seeks to escape them.

  • Miss McCraw: ‘Stopped at twelve. Never stopped before. Must be something magnetic.’ – she is dependent on time as a scientific construct and searches for a rational cause.

  • Mr Hussey: ‘Well after two, I’d say. We’d better be careful. I promised Mrs Appleyard I’d have you back at the college by eight.’ – he is compliant towards the conventions and expectations of schedules.

  • Marion: ‘I should like to take a few measurements at the base of the rock, if we have time.’ – she feels a sense of authority about the measurement and regulation of time.

  • Irma: ‘We’ll be back long before tea.’ – she is confident, perhaps naively, in the relentless, predictable passage of time.

  • Miranda: ‘Everything begins and ends at exactly the right time and place.’ – she is fatalistic about, even acquiescent to, a larger order that blurs temporal boundaries.

  • Edith: ‘When are we going home?’ – she is reliant upon institutional order and patterns.

Attitude vocabulary: fatalistic about, constrained by, reflective about, acquiescent to, feel a sense of authority about, dependent on, searching for, compliant towards, have a sense of agency + the relentless passage of time, adherence to human constructs of time, the ephemerality of time, time as a scientific construct, the measurement and regulation of time, a blurring of temporal boundaries, humanity’s relationship with time, the conventions and expectations of schedules, a reliance upon institutional order and patterns. Miranda’s attitude differs from the others: where most characters measure, obey or depend on time, she rejects her watch and accepts that events happen at their ‘right time’, as though she belongs to nature’s timescale rather than the college’s.

Film Techniques and Time

Weir also plays with time through cinematic devices. Action frequently happens in slow motion, suggesting time is not always linear. Compare two short scenes:

  • Miranda crosses the stream (00:23:20–00:23:43): a young character, outdoors in nature, in slow motion, with ancient-sounding pan pipes in the background.

  • Mrs Appleyard waits (00:34:49–00:35:08): an old character, indoors, in silence except for the ticking of a clock.

Statements to weigh up:

  • Weir explores the tension between Edwardian society’s obsession with schedules and the timelessness of the natural world.

  • Weir suggests that humanity’s need to measure time reflects a broader desire to impose order on an inherently mysterious universe.

  • By disrupting conventions of time, Weir reveals the limitations of human understanding and rational thought.

  • Weir’s film illustrates that time may be fluid or multidimensional, rather than strictly linear.

  • While some people live by the constraints of time, Weir demonstrates that others escape these limitations and transcend normal experience.

Trying to Make Sense of What Happened

The drag returns to Appleyard College without Miranda, Irma, Marion and Miss McCraw. The burning question on Mrs Appleyard’s lips is not what has happened but ‘Why are they so late?’, which she asks of herself and of Mademoiselle as she steps down from the drag. This highlights a key focus of the authorities in the aftermath: attempting to make rational sense of an irrational situation.

What someone says or does What it shows about ‘rational’ thinking
Mr Hussey, the first to give an account, tells Mrs Appleyard and then Sergeant Bumpher: ‘Nobody knows what happened... we were all asleep.’ Being unconscious or asleep prevents the rational mind from witnessing, so reason is absent at the very moment of the event.
Sergeant Bumpher asks Edith ‘Was there a man?’ and the doctor checks her for evidence of assault. For the authorities, the most rational explanation of girls disappearing is a male attacker: they look for an answer that fits their assumptions.
Mrs Bumpher reports the town’s view: ‘It couldn’t be local. No one around here would do a thing like that.’ According to the community’s rational thinking, a threat cannot come from within but must come from outside, because the community needs to believe in its own respectability.
Michael tells Sergeant Bumpher he saw three girls climbing the Rock, then recalls that it was four. Human memory is not rational or reliable, yet it is all the investigation has to work with.
Edith, back at the Rock, remembers ‘It was a cloud... it was red’ and that Miss McCraw was climbing without her skirt, in her drawers. Bumpher ignores the cloud and fixates on the missing skirt. Bumpher seeks a rational explanation that fits with his existing worldview, dismissing anything inexplicable.

Sentence starters:

  • People try to make rational sense of the disappearances in Picnic at Hanging Rock by...

  • Through various reactions to the disappearances, Weir suggests that people possess a deep psychological need to...

  • Bumpher’s failure to engage with Edith’s revelation about the ‘red cloud’ portrays how rational society is unable to...

  • The film reveals that rational thinking is often less concerned with discovering the truth than preserving a sense of...

  • Ultimately, Weir portrays the disappearances as an event that resists rational explanation, exposing the limitations of...

Costume, Hair and Undress

Because this is a period drama, costume and styling are deeply symbolic. Mrs Appleyard has a towering pompadour, whereas Miranda’s hair is long, blonde and naturalistic. The policemen’s uniforms have brass buttons, while the workers wear shirt sleeves and waistcoats (not wearing a jacket was almost shockingly casual in Edwardian times). Michael wears a top hat even at a picnic.

Hair

When the picnic party arrives back at the college, Mademoiselle’s hair is different from when she left. Mrs Appleyard descends the stairs with her hair very firmly in place, although later in the film this changes.

  • Miranda: naturalistic, natural, untamed, romantic, innocent – connecting her to nature and freedom.

  • Mademoiselle: carefully arranged before the picnic; dishevelled, tangled and in a state of disarray after it – the day at the Rock has loosened the order she brought with her.

  • Mrs Appleyard: formal, rigid, upright, severe, restrained, carefully arranged – embodying institutional control, until it unravels in her final scenes.

What Each Group Wears

Watch the scenes from 00:38:14 to 00:45:56 and notice how costume signals class and the conventions that govern each character: gloves, hats and accessories as formal; jewels and watches as signs of wealth; flowers as a connection with nature or the romantic; uniforms as a job or profession; high necks or buttoned-up shirts as formal. Look closely at Mrs Appleyard, Mr Hussey, the policemen, the searchers (the Indigenous tracker wears a red coat), the doctor, Michael and Mademoiselle.

A State of Undress

Before the girls disappear into the Rock, they take off their shoes and stockings, and Edith reports that Miss McCraw ‘looked funny’ because she was not wearing her skirt. Phrases you could use to interpret this: free from social constraints, drawn to wilderness, divesting themselves of convention, leaving social order behind, guided by instinct, embracing freedom, celebrating the natural world, returning to a state of nature, immersed in the landscape, connected to a natural order, becoming one with nature, engaging in pagan ritual.

Michael’s Quest

The film is really three intersecting stories: the missing girls, Mrs Appleyard’s victimisation of Sara, and Michael Fitzhubert’s personal journey. In this part of the film, Weir suggests that answers to the mystery cannot be found through rational inquiry alone, but through a willingness to be open to the mystery and power of nature. The big question is: why is Michael the only one who finds a missing girl?

A Different Mindset

  • Michael followed the girls partly up the Rock on the day they disappeared.

  • When Sergeant Bumpher asks ‘As the girls were jumping the creek, what were you thinking of?’, Michael does not answer in words but points to a swan vase containing a native flower (one of the few times a native cut flower is shown).

  • After Michael points to the swan vase, the scene segues to an image of Miranda and then of a real swan.

  • Michael says, ‘I wake up every night in a cold sweat. Just wondering if they’re still alive.’

  • Albert believes that if ‘the cop... the tracker and the bloody dog can’t find them, no one bloody can.’ But Michael believes ‘they may be out there dying of thirst.’

This evidence supports several readings: Michael is guided by emotion rather than logic; he feels a spiritual or mystical connection to Miranda; he is less constrained by conventional society; he has developed a personal connection to the landscape; his youthful imagination lets him see possibilities others cannot; he is undertaking his own journey of self-discovery.

An Individual Search

While most characters have given up on finding the girls, Michael cannot let the mystery go.

  • On the first day he searches with Albert and they find nothing. He leaves a trail of note paper to show where he has searched – a rational method.

  • He decides to spend the night on the Rock, sending Albert home to tell his uncle he is staying the night in Woodend. There is an orange-red, mystical sunset.

  • The next day he searches alone.

  • At the top of the Rock he falls asleep, and we hear the voices of the girls describing the Rock from earlier in the film.

Words for analysis: undergoes a..., open to..., driven by emotions of..., moves beyond, reflects the nature of..., drawn to..., personal, crosses a threshold by..., enters a different..., guided by, attuned, receptive to. Sentence frame: Weir suggests that Michael succeeds in finding one of the girls where others fail because...

Irma Returns

After Albert rescues Michael, he rushes back to the Rock to find the source of the white lace fragment Michael handed him, and finds Irma. At first he is wary, thinking she must be dead, but after nudging her he realises she is alive. Everyone should be delighted, but the discovery unsettles them, because it raises more questions. Where are the others? How did the searchers miss Irma? Why was she not dead after a week on the Rock? As the doctor says: ‘Remarkable.’

Writing About Mystery

Evidence from quotes and cinematography:

  • When Albert finds Irma there is an eerie sound of wind rushing through the Rock.

  • As the news spreads, the townspeople gather to find answers or renew the search.

  • Outside Bumpher’s house, a man shouts, ‘We’ve got a right to know.’

  • Mrs Appleyard tells her students they can ‘thank the Lord for Irma’s merciful recovery’.

  • Mrs Appleyard chides Miss Lumley, saying that ‘this makes it worse’.

  • Irma recuperates shrouded beneath white, gauzy fabric.

  • As the doctor lists Irma’s injuries, he tells Bumpher that ‘it’s very strange’.

  • The head gardener says that ‘there’s some questions got answers, some haven’t’.

Analytic phrases: evokes a sense of, suggests, highlights how, demonstrates, imbues the scene with, reveals, elicits the feeling of, signals, implies, examines, accentuates, brings into focus. Mystery phrases: resistant to definitive explanations, steeped in ambiguity, demand rationality in everyday life, are subject to forces beyond understanding, human understanding has limits, believe that everything can be explained with effort and application, suggestive of forces beyond logic, imbued with a dreamlike uncertainty, veiled from rational explanation, shrouded in mystery, must accept the limits of human comprehension. Practice topic: Peter Weir’s film explores a world filled with mysteries beyond human understanding. Discuss. Possible topic sentences:

  • Weir suggests that humanity’s desire to impose order on the world is ultimately challenged by forces beyond its understanding.

  • Weir’s film demonstrates that rational explanations are often inadequate when confronted with the mysteries of the natural world.

  • Picnic at Hanging Rock explores the tension between humanity’s need for answers and the existence of mysteries that remain unresolved.

  • By surrounding key events with ambiguity, Weir emphasises that complete understanding is often unattainable.

  • Picnic at Hanging Rock demonstrates the discomfort that people experience when faced with the inexplicable.

Linking language: The film further explores this idea when...; Weir’s exploration of... is exemplified by...; The significance of this... is evident in...; Weir reinforces this through...; This idea is visually represented by...; This becomes particularly evident when...; moreover, again, furthermore, likewise, what is more, more profoundly; while, however, yet, even though, although, whereas.

Mrs Appleyard’s Story

Mrs Appleyard is one of the film’s most important characters. She has created the school’s ethos and rules, so she represents a form of institutional authority that seeks to organise and control the lives of women. Her status and reputation depend on how the girls present themselves to the world. When the girls disappear under mysterious circumstances, and evidence emerges that their corsets and clothing had been removed, her standing as a figure of authority, respectability and decorum is irreparably damaged. She is ultimately a tragic figure: like many tragic characters she cannot bend or adapt, clinging desperately to social status, order and control. This rigidity leads to her downfall. She dies, apparently, ‘while attempting to climb the rock’, the natural force that challenged her belief in human order and exposed the limits of her authority.

Mrs Appleyard’s Nature

  • Her formal, ornate and old-fashioned hairstyle.

  • The watch she wears pinned to her chest.

  • Most of her interactions with others involve reminding them of the rules.

  • Her unsympathetic interactions with Sara Waybourne.

  • Her dark clothing and black dresses.

  • In the second half of the film, she wears a brooch with a photograph of her husband.

  • Her belief that finding ‘only one’ girl ‘makes it worse’.

  • Her insistence that Appleyard College is not a ‘charitable institution’.

  • She is typically shown at her desk, standing higher than other characters, or in an over-the-shoulder shot.

  • The dinner where she remembers the good old days at Bournemouth, when ‘nothing changed – ever’.

Vocabulary: authoritarian, controlling, representative of institutional authority, strict, fragile, fearful of disorder, inflexible, rigid, elitist, lonely, conventional, rule-bound, cold, distant. Sentence frames: Weir depicts Mrs Appleyard’s... nature through...; Weir creates a portrait of Mrs Appleyard as... and... by...

Mrs Appleyard’s Setting

Weir conveys Mrs Appleyard not only through what she wears and how she behaves, but through the spaces she occupies, chiefly her study and sometimes her bedroom.

  • The clock that constantly and audibly ticks in her study conveys her obsession with order, schedules and discipline.

  • The large portrait of Queen Victoria, surrounded by the English flag, above her desk represents her need to display her reverence for authority and status.

  • The pictures of her husband and ancestors on her study walls symbolise her attachment to history and convention.

  • The picture of a tiger on her study wall illustrates both the power of her authority and hidden emotional depths.

Mrs Appleyard’s End

Because Mrs Appleyard represents institutional authority, the events at the Rock, which challenge human order and certainty, have a profound effect on her.

  • On the night before her death, she dines with Mademoiselle de Poitiers, wearing softer, less severe clothing: the only time we see her dressed this way.

  • At dinner, she reminisces about annual holidays in Bournemouth with her late husband, describing it as ‘a delightful place’.

  • She admits she had come to ‘depend so much on Greta McCraw’ and her ‘masculine mind’, wondering, ‘How could she allow herself to be spirited away?’

  • The final image of her shows her in black mourning clothes and a funeral veil, staring directly into the camera, as we are told she died ‘while attempting to climb the rock’.

Question to answer: What are the consequences of Mrs Appleyard’s controlling mindset?

Unresolved Mysteries

You could write an essay about unresolved mysteries that goes: There’s a rock. It does strange things. End of story. It would not get a good mark. Instead, consider the evidence for each mystery and weigh up possible interpretations.

1. Why Does Irma Return but the Others Stay Missing?

Evidence:

  • When Miranda says at the picnic that she no longer wears her pretty jewelled watch, Irma says that if it were hers, she would wear it ‘always... even in the bath.’

  • Before the girls disappear, Irma says to the prone Edith, ‘Poor Edith, we’ll be back soon.’

  • Irma cannot recall what happened to her on the Rock.

  • Miranda, Marion and Miss McCraw are all linked in their names; Irma is different.

  • When Irma revisits Appleyard College, she wears a vivid red dress. The cloud Edith saw at the Rock was red.

Possible interpretations: Irma belongs more to the world of civilisation than to the mysterious world of nature; Irma belongs to both worlds; one girl returning heightens the mystery and uncertainty of what happened to the others, a reminder that some mysteries can never be solved; nature allows no rational explanation of its mysteries, even to those who experienced them; Irma has been transformed by her experiences on the Rock, offering a glimpse of what might have happened to the other girls.

2. Why Does the Rock Affect People Differently?

Evidence:

  • On the day of the picnic, many characters at the Rock (including Michael’s uncle and aunt) are overcome by sleepiness.

  • While Miranda, Marion and Irma are drawn to the Rock, Edith calls it ‘horrible’.

  • Michael and Albert remain unaffected by the sleepiness that overtakes others on the day of the picnic.

  • When Edith returns to the Rock with Sergeant Bumpher and Mademoiselle, she seems unaffected by its mysterious power.

  • When Bumpher and the first search party climb the Rock, they stop halfway, overcome by heat and exhaustion.

  • When Michael searches the Rock alone, he becomes disoriented, struggles to climb and eventually falls asleep.

  • After Irma returns, the final search shows the party gathered at the base of the Rock in a relaxed, almost picnic-like atmosphere.

Possible interpretations: the more people attempt to investigate and control the mysteries of the Rock, the more it resists them; the Rock’s power is shaped by each person’s willingness, or inability, to embrace the unknown; those who approach the Rock with openness are more receptive to its power; the Rock invites people to abandon the constraints of civilisation and enter a timeless world; the Rock reveals people’s true nature rather than affecting everyone the same way; it rewards curiosity but frustrates the desire for certainty; its power cannot be predicted or explained by human logic.

3. What Happened to Sara?

Weir creates a trail of deliberately conflicting clues about Sara’s fate.

  • Before Mrs Appleyard sees Sara for the last time, Sara sits on her bed beneath a painting of a woman holding two babies, repeating to herself, ‘We are not a charitable institution.’

  • Mrs Appleyard tells Mademoiselle that Sara’s guardian has come to collect her, though this appears not to have happened. In their final meeting she instead tells Sara, ‘You will be returned to the orphanage.’

  • Immediately after this, we see luggage being packed into a carriage, although it later becomes clear this is happening at Michael’s house.

  • After her final meeting with Mrs Appleyard, Sara smiles to herself with an expression of quiet resolution.

  • On the night of Sara’s death, Albert dreams of his sister, who tells him, ‘I’ve come a long way to see ya, and now I must go.’

Possible interpretations: Mrs Appleyard drives Sara to end her own life; Mrs Appleyard murders Sara; Sara’s fate mirrors the disappearances, suggesting the Rock’s influence extends beyond the landscape; the search for certainty about Sara’s death is as futile as the search for the missing girls; Sara’s fate shows that human actions in ‘civilisation’ are just as mysterious as nature; Sara escapes institutional control only through death.

4. What Happened to Mrs Appleyard?

Evidence:

  • In her final scene, Mrs Appleyard wears mourning clothes, as though grieving the loss of her school, her reputation or even herself.

  • In her final scene, she wears an expression of quiet resolve, echoing Sara’s demeanour before her death.

  • She admits she had come to ‘depend’ on Miss McCraw and cannot understand how her masculine intellect could be ‘spirited away... like a silly schoolgirl.’

Possible interpretations: Mrs Appleyard confronts the mysterious power of the Rock and is overwhelmed by it; she is seeking answers at the Rock; she is unhappy with her status and identity and seeks transformation on the Rock; by climbing the Rock herself, she abandons the ordered world she spent her life defending. Time: Finite and Infinite

Humans measure time in years, months, weeks, days, all the way down to microseconds, as though it gives them control. Most characters measure out their lives, but there are notable exceptions: Miranda has an almost mystical belief that everything happens at ‘exactly the right time’. One of Weir’s central points is that the natural world has boundless, apparently infinite time, while humans have only a brief, finite time to live.

  • Time as finite and measurable: ‘What time is it?’; the grandfather clock in Mrs Appleyard’s study; the regular beat of classical music; ‘can’t stand the ticking above my heart’ (Miranda, of her watch); the date and year at the opening of the film; Minnie and Tom rushing to get dressed because time has got away from them; ‘Blowed if me watch hasn’t stopped’; ‘When are we going home?’; ‘I’d wear it always’ (Irma, of Miranda’s watch); ‘We’d better be careful. I promised Mrs Appleyard...’.

  • Time as infinite and endless: ‘Everything begins and ends at exactly the right time and place’; slow-motion filming of Miranda; the thrumming of the Rock; ‘What we see and what we seem are but a dream, a dream within a dream’; the haunting pan flutes; the footage of the picnic at the end of the film, simulating the limited frames of historic film; the Rock is ‘quite young, geologically speaking’; ‘Waiting a million years, just for us’; the girls losing track of time and dancing on top of the Rock; the opening shot of Hanging Rock emerging from mist.
