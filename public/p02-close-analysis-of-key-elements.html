Weir tells this story with very little dialogue and almost no explanation of what his characters are thinking, so most of the meaning sits in what we see and hear: setting, costume, props, sound and the order of scenes. The notes below follow the film from start to finish, pairing each key moment with evidence, readings and sentence starters.

The Opening and Closing Scenes

The film opens with a prologue card: On Saturday 14th February 1900 a party of schoolgirls from Appleyard College picnicked at Hanging Rock near Mt. Macedon in the state of Victoria. During the afternoon several members of the party disappeared without trace... The card states the mystery outright, and its precise date and place give the impression of a true story, although the events are fiction. Over the credits the Rock slowly takes shape through mist, ancient and unmoved. At the close a narrator’s voice reports what became of Mrs Appleyard and the college and repeats that the girls were never found, while the screen shows grainy, jerky images of the picnic, as if they were old documentary film. Weir wraps an unexplained event in the trappings of a historical record. The two framing sequences set up the contrast the whole film explores. People want dates, places and outcomes they can record; the Rock offers none of these, and it outlasts every record.

  • The dated prologue reflects a habit of pinning experience down to the day and hour, as though that makes it knowable.

  • Naming a real place grounds the story in a map the audience could visit, making the loss feel concrete.

  • The Rock taking shape through the mist presents nature as something that was there long before the characters and will remain long after them.

  • Cloud shadows sliding across the Rock turn hours into seconds, making human time look brief beside it.

  • The closing narration gives the illusion of a case that has been tidied up and filed.

  • The grainy, jerky final footage borrows the look of an old newsreel, offering the audience a false sense of documentary certainty.

  • The last, lingering shots of the girls leave the audience with faces rather than answers.

Contrasting the Opening and Closing

The two sequences are similar, but not identical. Some statements describe both; others suit one more than the other. Ideas to sort and compare:

  • people see themselves as standing apart from the landscape, yet the film keeps placing them inside it;

  • the girls’ youth and grace seem to echo the beauty of the bush;

  • facts seem as solid as the Rock itself;

  • the story is a small event set within a much older place;

  • the landscape holds a power that no one in the film can direct;

  • people seem breakable beside the permanence of stone;

  • the natural world sits beyond the reach of explanation;

  • the Rock shows how little people actually know;

  • what we know is as fixed as the landscape.

Sentence building: Weir opens and closes his film with... / The prologue and the final narration both... / By beginning and ending with... + suggest / frame / establish / return to + the belief that / a concern with / the hope that / the illusion that... + yet / but / even so / at the same time...

Picnicking

Picnics are the reason anyone visits Hanging Rock in this film. The Fitzhuberts eat at a little folding table with Michael, sipping tea from fine china in near silence, while Albert, as their servant, eats apart. The college party holds its own meal, complete with a toast to ‘St. Valentine’ and Miranda cutting a heart-shaped cake. Both picnics are ceremonies with fixed rules, and they tell us a lot about how this society behaves even when it is supposed to be relaxing. Weir sets these rituals against the old, rough bush around them. Watch for things that look out of place: the white tablecloth, the china, the iced cake, the girls’ white dresses and gloves, parasols and hats, the drag with its harnessed horses, Mr Hussey’s pocket watch. Word bank for incongruity: look out of place in / clash with / jar against / sit awkwardly within / intrude upon / seem cut off from + the ancient bush / the rugged slopes of the Rock / the indifferent landscape / a world older than the colony / the wild country around them. Sentence starters: During the picnic, Weir...; The ritual of the meal...; Even at leisure, the colonists...; While the Fitzhuberts sit at their table, Albert...

After the Meal

When the meal is over, Edith looks across at the Fitzhuberts and says they ‘might be the only living creatures in the whole world’. Weir immediately cuts to ants swarming over the remains of the St Valentine’s cake. The joke is pointed: the picnic is one small gathering in a landscape crowded with other life, and the moment people stop eating, the bush begins taking back what they have left. After eating, the picnickers fall back on habits that mark them as ‘civilised’:

  • Mr Hussey checks his watch.

  • Irma says she would wear Miranda’s jewelled watch even in the bath.

  • Marion wants to measure the Rock.

  • Miss McCraw reads.

  • Mademoiselle likens Miranda to a Botticelli angel.

Ways to read these moments: the colonists turn every experience into something they can count, own or label; even their leisure is a way of making the strange familiar; their habits are a kind of armour against a place they do not understand; knowing the time, the measurement or the artistic comparison gives them a feeling of mastery. Topic sentence to develop: Through the rituals of the picnic, Weir shows a society that carries its rules into the bush and finds that the bush takes no notice of them. Linking phrases: During the picnic...; Weir presents...; This shows...; Here Weir suggests...; The effect is...; The same idea returns when...; Weir develops this through...; Similarly...; In addition...; By contrast...

Thresholds

A threshold is the line where one space becomes another, like a doorway between a house and the street. The film is built around two such spaces: the enclosed, rule-bound world of the college and the open, unknowable world of the Rock. On the way from one to the other the girls pass several physical boundaries, and each marks a further step away from the habits and expectations they started the day with.

  • Threshold: boundary, border, crossing point, doorway, the point of no return, stepping out of..., passing into..., the edge of..., moving beyond.

  • Civilised world: the enclosed and familiar, rules and timetables, protection, propriety, supervision, routine, the known.

  • Natural world: the open and unmapped, the wild, instinct, release, danger, wonder, the unknown.

The Gate

At the foot of the Rock the drag stops at a gate, and someone has to open it. A gate is an obvious symbol, but Weir surrounds this one with details that deepen it:

  • Immediately before the gate scene, Michael and Albert, men from different classes, share a drink.

  • The three girls who will vanish, Miranda, Irma and Marion, ride at the front of the drag, and it is Miranda who jumps down to open the gate.

  • Miranda removes her hat, leaving it in the drag, to open the gate.

  • Just before the gate opens, the camera rests on an old notice board, its notices faded past reading.

  • As soon as the gate swings open, Miranda watches birds wheel overhead, and the horses rear and whinny in their harness.

Sentence starters: By having Miranda open the gate herself, Weir...; The weathered notice board suggests...; The horses’ panic as the gate swings open...

The Stream

To begin their climb, against Mrs Appleyard’s orders, Miranda, Marion, Irma and Edith must first cross a creek. Albert, watching from the bank, makes a crude comment about the girls. Miranda drops a flower into the water as she crosses, and Weir then gives us a close shot of the creek itself, dark and full of weed. Possible readings:

  • The flower reads like a gift or offering, turning a simple crossing into something closer to a rite.

  • Albert’s leering remark reminds us how exposed the girls are once they leave the party behind.

  • The cloudy water hints that what follows will be impossible to see clearly.

  • Gate, then creek, then rocks: each boundary pulls the girls one step further in, building a sense that the outcome is already decided.

The Rocks

The last boundary is the Rock itself. Near the top the girls find a maze of boulders, monoliths and narrow gaps rather than a single summit, and at the end of the sequence Miranda, Marion and Irma pass through one of these gaps and out of sight. Words to describe how Weir presents the rocks: towering, labyrinthine, enclosing, ancient, hypnotic, looming, cave-like, swallowing, silent, monumental, beckoning, impenetrable; the gaps work as doorways or passages. Sentence frame: At the summit, Weir frames the rocks as... and..., so that the final passage the girls enter becomes a doorway into...

Time Stops

As the girls rest after lunch, one of them reads Shakespeare’s sonnet ‘Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day’, a poem preoccupied with seasons, months and the passing of beauty. Soon afterwards Mr Hussey discovers that his watch has stopped. Despite his warning about the time, four girls set off up the Rock, ostensibly to measure it. Partway up they gossip about Sara, and Irma calls her a ‘doomed’ little deer. Then, in slow motion, they take off their shoes and stockings. Each character’s words reveal an attitude towards time:

  • Miranda: ‘Don’t wear it anymore. Can’t stand the ticking above my heart.’ – she feels constrained by human constructs of time and seeks to escape them.

  • Miss McCraw: ‘Stopped at twelve. Never stopped before. Must be something magnetic.’ – she is dependent on time as a scientific construct and searches for a rational cause.

  • Mr Hussey: ‘Well after two, I’d say. We’d better be careful. I promised Mrs Appleyard I’d have you lot back at the college by eight.’ – his first loyalty is to the timetable he has promised to keep.

  • Marion: ‘I should like to make a few measurements of the rock if we have time.’ – she treats time as one more quantity to manage.

  • Irma: ‘We’ll be back long before tea.’ – she is confident, perhaps naively, in the relentless, predictable passage of time.

  • Miranda: ‘Everything begins and ends at exactly the right time and place.’ – she is fatalistic about, even acquiescent to, a larger order that blurs temporal boundaries.

  • Edith: ‘When are we going home?’ – she is reliant upon institutional order and patterns.

Attitude vocabulary: bound by, resistant to, trusting in, anxious about, governed by, indifferent to, at ease with, unsettled by + the clock, the college’s timetable, the schedule promised to Mrs Appleyard, time as fixed and measurable, a time older than the colony, the stopping of the watches, a sense of fate. Miranda’s attitude differs from the others: where most characters measure, obey or depend on time, she rejects her watch and accepts that events happen at their ‘right time’, as though she belongs to nature’s timescale rather than the college’s.

Film Techniques and Time

Weir also plays with time through cinematic devices. Action frequently happens in slow motion, suggesting time is not always linear. Compare two short scenes:

  • Miranda crosses the stream (00:23:20–00:23:43): a young girl, in the open air, in slow motion, to the sound of pan pipes.

  • Mrs Appleyard waits (00:34:49–00:35:08): an older woman, alone indoors, with nothing to hear but a ticking clock.

Statements to weigh up:

  • The colonists’ fixation on punctuality looks absurd beside the Rock’s age.

  • Counting the hours is one more way the characters try to keep control of a world they do not understand.

  • When the watches stop, so does the characters’ confidence in their own reasoning.

  • The film imagines time as something that can stretch, pause or loop rather than simply move forward.

  • Some characters are ruled by the clock, while others, like Miranda, seem to step outside it.

The Search for an Explanation

The drag comes back to the college without Miranda, Irma, Marion or Miss McCraw. Mrs Appleyard’s first question is not what has happened but ‘Why are they so late?’, which she asks aloud as Mademoiselle climbs down. From here on, the adults’ main project is to fit an inexplicable event into a rational account.

What someone says or does What it shows about ‘rational’ thinking
Mr Hussey, the first to give an account, tells Mrs Appleyard and then Sergeant Bumpher: ‘No one knows. No one saw her leave. We were asleep.’ Being unconscious or asleep prevents the rational mind from witnessing, so reason is absent at the very moment of the event.
Sergeant Bumpher asks Edith ‘Was there a man?’ and the doctor checks her for evidence of assault. For the authorities, the most rational explanation of girls disappearing is a male attacker: they look for an answer that fits their assumptions.
Mrs Bumpher reports the town’s view: ‘It couldn’t be local... No one around here would do a thing like that.’ The townspeople decide the danger must have come from outside, because the alternative would threaten their picture of themselves as decent.
Michael tells Sergeant Bumpher he saw three girls climbing the Rock, then recalls that it was four. Human memory is not rational or reliable, yet it is all the investigation has to work with.
Edith, back at the Rock, remembers ‘It was a cloud... it was red’ and that Miss McCraw was climbing without her skirt, in her drawers. Bumpher ignores the cloud and fixates on the missing skirt. Bumpher seeks a rational explanation that fits with his existing worldview, dismissing anything inexplicable.

Sentence starters:

  • In the days after the picnic, the townspeople try to explain the disappearance by...

  • The police inquiry reveals how badly the community needs...

  • When Bumpher brushes past Edith’s ‘red cloud’, Weir shows that official reasoning cannot...

  • The search for an explanation seems driven less by the truth than by a wish to protect...

  • By leaving the disappearance unexplained, Weir exposes the limits of...

Costume, Hair and Undress

In a period film, clothes and hair carry a great deal of meaning. Mrs Appleyard’s hair is piled high in a severe pompadour, while Miranda’s falls long, fair and loose. The police wear brass-buttoned uniforms; the working men go about in shirt sleeves and waistcoats, which in 1900 was startlingly informal. Michael wears a top hat even to a picnic.

Hair

Compare Mademoiselle’s hair when she leaves for the picnic with its state when she returns. Mrs Appleyard, meanwhile, comes down the stairs with every strand in place, which will not last.

  • Miranda: naturalistic, natural, untamed, romantic, innocent – connecting her to nature and freedom.

  • Mademoiselle: carefully arranged before the picnic; dishevelled, tangled and in a state of disarray after it – the day at the Rock has loosened the order she brought with her.

  • Mrs Appleyard: formal, rigid, upright, severe, restrained, carefully arranged – embodying institutional control, until it unravels in her final scenes.

What Each Group Wears

In the scenes from 00:38:14 to 00:45:56, notice what each costume says about rank and the rules a character lives by: gloves and hats for formality; jewellery and watches for wealth; flowers for romance or nature; uniforms for a role or trade; high collars and buttoned shirts for propriety. Look at Mrs Appleyard, Mr Hussey, the police, the search party (the Aboriginal tracker is dressed in a red coat), the doctor, Michael and Mademoiselle.

A State of Undress

Before they vanish, the girls take off their shoes and stockings, and Edith later reports that Miss McCraw looked ‘funny’ because she was climbing without her skirt. Phrases for interpreting this: shedding the rules of the college, stepping out of their roles, answering the pull of the landscape, unburdened, instinctive, exposed, vulnerable, freed from propriety, closer to the earth, ritualistic, trance-like.

Michael’s Quest

Three stories run side by side in the film: the disappearance, Mrs Appleyard’s mistreatment of Sara, and Michael Fitzhubert’s private search. Michael’s thread suggests that reasoning and procedure get nowhere with the Rock, and that only someone willing to be drawn in by it can bring anything back. The question to keep in mind: why is it Michael who finds a missing girl?

How Michael Thinks

  • On the day of the picnic, Michael followed the girls part of the way up the Rock.

  • Asked by Sergeant Bumpher ‘As the girls were jumping the creek, what were you thinking of?’, Michael gives no answer in words; he points to a swan-shaped vase holding a native flower, a rare native bloom among the film’s imported ones.

  • Weir follows the gesture with a shot of Miranda and then of a living swan.

  • Michael says, ‘I wake up every night in a cold sweat. Just wondering if they’re still alive.’

  • Albert believes that if ‘the bloody cop and the bloody... tracker and the bloody dog can’t find ‘em, well, no one bloody can.’ But Michael believes ‘they may be out there dying of thirst.’

Readings this evidence supports: Michael follows feeling rather than reasoning; he is bound to Miranda in a way he cannot put into words; he cares less for social convention than his family does; the Rock has taken hold of him personally; his imagination lets him sense what others miss; the search is also a search for who he is.

An Individual Search

While most characters have given up on finding the girls, Michael cannot let the mystery go.

  • He first searches with Albert and finds nothing, marking his route with scraps of paper as he goes, a methodical approach.

  • He chooses to stay on the Rock overnight and sends Albert back to tell his uncle he is spending the night in Woodend. The sunset glows a deep orange-red.

  • The next day he searches alone.

  • Near the summit he falls asleep, and the soundtrack replays the girls’ voices from the day of the picnic.

Words for analysis: drawn in by..., gives himself up to..., guided by feeling, obsessed with..., unlike the searchers who..., passes beyond..., the Rock claims..., changed by..., receptive, haunted. Sentence frame: Weir suggests that Michael succeeds in finding one of the girls where others fail because...

Irma Returns

Once Albert has brought Michael down, he goes back up the Rock to trace the scrap of white lace Michael was clutching, and finds Irma. He hesitates, assuming she is dead, then realises she is breathing. It should be a moment of pure relief, yet it makes everything harder: if Irma is here, where are the others, how did the searchers miss her, and how has she survived a week on the Rock? The doctor can only call it ‘Remarkable.’

Writing About Mystery

Evidence from quotes and cinematography:

  • As Albert reaches Irma, the soundtrack fills with a hollow rush of wind through the rocks.

  • Word gets out and townspeople crowd in, some demanding answers, some ready to search again.

  • Outside Bumpher’s house, a man shouts, ‘We’ve got a bloody right to know.’

  • Mrs Appleyard tells her students they can ‘thank the Lord for this merciful deliverance of our dear classmate Irma’.

  • Mrs Appleyard chides Miss Lumley, saying that ‘this makes it worse’.

  • Irma lies recovering under pale, gauzy drapes.

  • As the doctor lists Irma’s injuries, he tells Bumpher that her unmarked feet are ‘very strange’.

  • The head gardener says that ‘there’s some questions got answers and some haven’t’.

Analytic phrases: creates a sense of, hints at, draws attention to, unsettles, lends the scene, leaves the audience with, underlines, complicates, foregrounds, withholds. Mystery phrases: refuses a clear answer, leaves the question open, beyond the reach of reason, the comfort of explanation is denied, an ambiguity the film never resolves, clues that lead nowhere, the limits of what people can know, a puzzle without a key, the inexplicable, a silence at the heart of the story. Practice topic: Peter Weir’s film explores a world filled with mysteries beyond human understanding. Discuss. Possible topic sentences:

  • Weir shows that the colonists’ confidence in their own order cannot survive an event they cannot explain.

  • The police inquiry demonstrates how quickly reasoning runs out in the face of the Rock.

  • Picnic at Hanging Rock sets the townspeople’s hunger for answers against a mystery that offers none.

  • By withholding explanation at every turn, Weir makes uncertainty the experience of watching the film.

  • Picnic at Hanging Rock shows how uneasy people become when the world will not explain itself.

Linking language: Weir returns to this idea when...; A further example is...; This is echoed in...; The same pattern appears when...; This becomes clearer still in...; similarly, likewise, in the same way, beyond this; however, yet, by contrast, whereas, although.

Mrs Appleyard’s Story

Mrs Appleyard built the college’s rules and its reputation, and she stands for an institution whose job is to shape and supervise young women. Her standing depends on how those young women appear to the world. When the girls vanish, and the inquiry turns up the detail that Irma’s corset is missing, the scandal strikes at everything she has staked her name on. She is a tragic figure in the classic sense: she cannot bend, and so she breaks. Holding ever tighter to status, routine and control, she unravels as each of them fails. We are told she died ‘while attempting to climb the rock’, the very place that defeated her idea of order.

Mrs Appleyard’s Nature

  • Her formal, ornate and old-fashioned hairstyle.

  • The watch she wears pinned to her chest.

  • Nearly every conversation she has turns into a reminder of the rules.

  • Her unsympathetic interactions with Sara Waybourne.

  • Her dark clothing and black dresses.

  • Later in the film she wears a brooch holding a photograph of her late husband.

  • Her belief that finding ‘only one’ girl ‘makes it worse’.

  • Her insistence that Appleyard College is not a ‘charitable institution’.

  • She is typically shown at her desk, standing higher than other characters, or in an over-the-shoulder shot.

  • At dinner she recalls holidays in Bournemouth, where ‘nothing changed – ever’.

Vocabulary: imperious, severe, proud, status-conscious, brittle, anxious, unbending, joyless, isolated, snobbish, exacting, repressed, formidable. Sentence frames: Through..., Weir presents Mrs Appleyard as...; Mrs Appleyard’s... is revealed when...

Mrs Appleyard’s Setting

Weir characterises Mrs Appleyard through the rooms she inhabits as much as through her clothes and behaviour, above all her study, and occasionally her bedroom.

  • The loud, steady ticking of the clock in her study keeps her need for order audible in every scene she is in.

  • The portrait of Queen Victoria, framed by the flag, above her desk shows where her loyalties lie: with empire, rank and authority.

  • Photographs of her husband and forebears on the walls tie her to the past and to inherited respectability.

  • A painting of a tiger on the study wall hints at both the force of her authority and something wilder kept out of sight.

Mrs Appleyard’s End

Everything Mrs Appleyard stands for depends on order and certainty, so the events at the Rock hit her harder than anyone.

  • The night before she dies, she dines with Mademoiselle in a softer gown than we have seen her wear before.

  • Over dinner she remembers holidays in Bournemouth with her late husband, ‘a delightful place’.

  • She admits she had come to ‘depend so much on Greta McCraw’ and her ‘masculine intellect’, wondering, ‘How could she allow herself to be spirited away?’

  • Her last image is of a woman in black mourning dress and veil, looking straight into the camera, as the narrator reports she died ‘while attempting to climb the rock’.

Question to answer: What does Mrs Appleyard’s need for control cost her, and others?

Unresolved Mysteries

A weak essay on this idea says little more than the Rock is strange and nobody knows why. A strong one works through each unanswered question, sets out the evidence and tests more than one reading against it.

1. Why Is Irma the Only One to Come Back?

Evidence:

  • When Miranda says she has stopped wearing her jewelled watch, Irma says she would wear it ‘always... even in the bath.’

  • Before the girls disappear, Irma says to the prone Edith, ‘Poor Edith. We’ll go back soon.’

  • Irma has no memory of what happened on the Rock.

  • The three who are never found share an initial: Miranda, Marion and Miss McCraw. Irma does not.

  • When Irma visits the college again she is dressed in vivid red, the same colour as the cloud Edith saw.

Possible interpretations: Irma’s love of jewels and comfort ties her to the human world, so the Rock lets her go; she belongs partly to both worlds; her return deepens the mystery instead of solving it; even someone who was there cannot explain what the Rock did; she comes back changed, a hint of what the others may have become.

2. Why Does the Rock Act on Each Person in Its Own Way?

Evidence:

  • At the picnic, a drowsiness settles over many of the adults, the Fitzhuberts included.

  • Miranda, Marion and Irma are drawn upwards; Edith calls the place ‘nasty’.

  • Michael and Albert stay alert while others doze.

  • Brought back to the Rock with Bumpher and Mademoiselle, Edith seems untouched by it.

  • The first police search party turns back halfway, beaten by heat and fatigue.

  • Searching alone, Michael loses his way, struggles on the rocks and finally sleeps.

  • In the last search after Irma is found, the party lounges at the foot of the Rock as if at another picnic.

Possible interpretations: the harder people push to investigate, the less the Rock gives them; its effect depends on how open each person is to what cannot be explained; those who come with curiosity are drawn in, while those who come with fear or procedure are kept out; it tempts people out of their social roles and into a different kind of time; it exposes character rather than acting on everyone equally; it frustrates anyone who demands certainty; no rule predicts who it will take.

3. What Happened to Sara?

Weir gives us clues about Sara that point in different directions at once.

  • Before her last interview with Mrs Appleyard, Sara sits on her bed beneath a painting of a mother with two babies, repeating ‘This is not a charitable institution.’

  • Mrs Appleyard tells Mademoiselle that Sara’s guardian has fetched her, which does not seem to be true; what she actually tells Sara is ‘You will be returned to the orphanage.’

  • The next shot shows luggage loaded into a carriage, though we later realise it is at the Fitzhuberts’ house.

  • Leaving Mrs Appleyard, Sara gives a small, private smile, as if she has made up her mind.

  • That night Albert dreams of his sister, who says, ‘I’ve come a long way to see ya, and now I must go.’

Possible interpretations: Sara takes her own life, pushed to it by Mrs Appleyard; Mrs Appleyard kills her; Sara’s death is the Rock’s influence reaching back into the college; wanting certainty about Sara is as hopeless as wanting it about the girls; Weir suggests the ‘civilised’ world holds mysteries as dark as the bush; death is Sara’s only way out of the institution.

4. What Happened to Mrs Appleyard?

Evidence:

  • Her last appearance is in mourning dress, as if she is grieving for her college, her reputation, or her own sense of who she is.

  • Her face in that scene is calm and set, much like Sara’s before her death.

  • She confesses how much she relied on Miss McCraw, and cannot fathom how such a mind could be ‘spirited away... like a silly schoolgirl.’

Possible interpretations: she goes to face the Rock and it destroys her; she goes in search of answers; she wants to be free of the life she has built; by climbing, she gives up the order she defended all her life; she follows the path Sara took, out of an unbearable situation. Activity: Arrange the four mysteries on a spectrum from mysterious, unsettling and disturbing to makes sense, can be accepted. Which do you find least and most explicable, and why?

Time: Finite and Infinite

The characters cut time into years, months, days and hours, and the cutting gives them a sense of control. Most live by the clock; Miranda is the striking exception, trusting that everything happens at ‘exactly the right time’. Weir keeps placing the short, counted span of a human life against the Rock’s vast and uncounted age.

  • Time as finite and measurable: ‘What time is it?’; the grandfather clock in Mrs Appleyard’s study; the regular beat of classical music; ‘can’t stand the ticking above my heart’ (Miranda, of her watch); the date and year at the opening of the film; Minnie and Tom rushing to get dressed because time has got away from them; ‘Blowed if me watch hasn’t stopped’; ‘When are we going home?’; ‘I’d wear it always’ (Irma, of Miranda’s watch); ‘We’d better be careful. I promised Mrs Appleyard...’.

  • Time as infinite and endless: ‘Everything begins and ends at exactly the right time and place’; slow-motion filming of Miranda; the thrumming of the Rock; ‘What we see and what we seem are but a dream, a dream within a dream’; the haunting pan flutes; the footage of the picnic at the end of the film, simulating the limited frames of historic film; the Rock is ‘quite young, geologically speaking’; ‘Waiting a million years, just for us’; the girls losing track of time and dancing on top of the Rock; the opening shot of Hanging Rock emerging from mist.

Organising Evidence for Essays

For each key idea, gather evidence from three areas before you plan. Tick off what you can use, then note quotes, actions or scenes for each.

  • Characters: Miranda, Marion, Irma, Edith, Sara, Mrs Appleyard, Mademoiselle, Miss McCraw, Michael, Albert, Bumpher.

  • Symbols: the Rock, thresholds, animals, the swan, flowers, costume, hairstyles, mirrors, watches, clock sounds, contrasting settings.

  • Film techniques: slow motion, the prologue card, the closing voice-over, pan-pipe music, classical music, thrumming sounds, shrieking sounds, science-fiction-like sounds.

Guiding questions for each idea:

  • Nature as a fundamental force: how do these elements show that nature is powerful, mysterious, frightening, fascinating or beyond human control?

  • Rationality and control: how do they show that humans yearn for order and certainty, as opposed to the irrationality and disorder of nature?

  • Class and civilisation: how do they show aspects of institutions, authorities, social divisions, rules and conventions?

  • Time: how do they show aspects of time, systems, routines, measurement and cycles?

A Paragraph Frame

Topic: Picnic at Hanging Rock presents a natural world that people can observe but never grasp. Discuss.

  • Topic sentence: From its opening shot of the Rock emerging from mist, Picnic at Hanging Rock presents the bush as something the characters can see but never penetrate.

  • Initial examples: When..., Weir shows that the landscape...; Through..., he makes the Rock seem...; As a result, the bush appears...

  • Link: The same impenetrability returns when...

  • Further examples: As..., the audience sees...; By..., Weir leaves...

  • Synthesising sentence: Across these moments, the natural world remains... and..., always just beyond understanding.

A second topic sentence to try: Almost every time the colonists venture into the bush around Hanging Rock, Weir shows them ill at ease.

Unpacking Topics into Sub-Ideas

Is the attempt to explain the disappearance in Picnic at Hanging Rock doomed from the start? Discuss. List other ways to say ‘explain’ and ‘doomed’, then pick a sub-idea:

  • Every explanation the community offers falls apart, leaving the Rock as silent as before.

  • The police inquiry, with its questions about men and missing skirts, shows official reasoning looking in the wrong place.

  • Weir builds the film so that the audience, too, is denied an answer.

  • The search fails to explain the Rock but succeeds in exposing the searchers: their fears, prejudices and need for respectability.

  • Even without answers, the characters keep asking, and Weir suggests the asking itself is part of being human.

Weir shows that the structures people build to keep order give way when they meet the bush. To what extent do you agree?

  • From the stopped watches to the failed searches, Weir shows each of the colony’s systems faltering at the Rock.

  • Within days of the picnic, the college’s calm routines give way to rumour, anger and loss.

  • Police, school and town alike prove unable to act effectively against something they cannot name.

  • Rank and office protect no one: the headmistress, the sergeant and the colonel are all left helpless.

  • Yet order does not collapse at once; it frays slowly, as each pretence of control is tested and found wanting.

In Picnic at Hanging Rock, the respectable surface of colonial society hides what lies beneath it. Discuss. Split the topic into two halves: the respectable surface, and what lies beneath. Gather words and examples for each, then write a paragraph that first shows the polished face Weir gives his characters and settings, and then shows what that face conceals. In Picnic at Hanging Rock, the real danger to order comes from the people who enforce it rather than from the Rock. Do you agree? List dangers that come from the Rock against dangers that come from society’s own enforcers, then write a paragraph on each side. A process for time and order topics: underline the key words; list other ways of saying them; go back to your notes for examples; group the examples into three or four sets, one per body paragraph; write a topic sentence for each set; then draft. Practise on: Set against the age of the Rock, human attempts at order look small; In Picnic at Hanging Rock, the land itself seems ageless and unreadable; In Picnic at Hanging Rock, the bush makes the college’s rules and routines seem absurd.
