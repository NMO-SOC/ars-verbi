Connecting the Stories

The hardest part of writing on a collection is finding links between stories that look nothing alike. These activities practise connecting stories through shared ideas, symbols and types of character, the move that lifts a Section A essay into the top band.

Naming, or Not Naming, Protagonists

A surprising number of Jackson’s protagonists are never named. In ‘The Daemon Lover’ we never learn the woman’s name, though we learn Jamie’s and those of many people she meets. ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, by contrast, opens with David Turner’s full name. Describing the two protagonists. The woman in ‘The Daemon Lover’ is unsettled, adrift, waiting, hesitant, between one life and the next, easily shaken, unsure of her worth, defined by the man who has not come. David Turner is precise, fastidious, settled, careful, house-proud, attached to routine, sure of his tastes, quietly content – at least until his evening is taken over. Model paragraph:

Jackson often withholds a name from characters whose hold on their own identity is slipping, while those she names begin with a firmer sense of who they are. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, the nameless protagonist spends her wedding day hunting the city for Jamie. Without a name of her own, she exists only as a woman about to be married, and when the marriage fails to arrive, so does her sense of self. Jackson tracks this through her jumpy thoughts and growing suspicion of the strangers she questions, until she is reduced to knocking on a door that no one opens. David Turner, introduced by his full name in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, seems the opposite: he knows exactly how he likes his tea towels, his colours and his single key. Yet Jackson shows how little protection this gives him. When Mr Harris arrives and assumes the apartment and the pie are Marcia’s, David lets him believe it and is pushed out of his own home. Set side by side, the two stories suggest that a name may signal stability, but neither character’s identity survives contact with other people’s expectations.

Topic sentence options:

  • Jackson leaves some characters nameless to show how uncertain they are about themselves, and names others whose identities seem secure.

  • Withholding a name is one of Jackson’s ways of showing a self that is fading or unformed.

  • Several of Jackson’s characters are known by their roles – the mother, the boy, the bride – rather than as individuals.

  • Jackson’s nameless protagonists are often the most anxious, while her named characters seem steadier, at least at first.

  • A name in Jackson’s stories can be a sign of belonging, and its absence a sign of drifting.

Evidence: ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘Anxiously she pulled through the dresses in the closet’; she ‘hesitated until a door at the end opened’; ‘suddenly horribly aware of her over-young print dress’; ‘There’s more than you think when you look at me.’ ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: ‘did everything in small, quick movements’; ‘He had always been partial to yellows and browns’; ‘It pleased him to have only one key to his home’; ‘I like things this way.’ Paragraph frame: In [story], Jackson never names / introduces by name...; This suggests that...; When [character]..., we see...; Jackson reinforces this through...; By contrast, in [story]...; At first [character] seems..., but...; Taken together, the two stories suggest...

Windows and Doors

Jackson’s characters often stand at windows, and when they do they are usually thinking about some other life.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘She went to the window and saw that it was undeniably a fine day. It occurred to her that perhaps she ought not to wear the blue silk dress; it was too plain, almost severe, and she wanted to be soft, feminine.’

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: ‘The window had been open all day and papers had blown wildly around the floor. David closed the window, hesitated over the papers, and then moved away quickly.’

  • ‘The Villager’: ‘The first thing Miss Clarence did was go to the window... far off to the left, a high building crowned with flower gardens. Someday I’ll live there, she thought.’

  • ‘The Witch’: ‘The little boy was looking out the window... “I saw a witch,” he said.’

Each time, the character is inside one life while looking at, or turning away from, another. Words to describe this: longing, escape, a life not lived, imagination, restlessness, confinement, the wider world, hope, fantasy, disappointment, distance, curiosity, the ordinary versus the possible. Sentence starters: Jackson places her characters at windows to show...; Looking out of the window, [character]...; The view from [character]’s window suggests...; Across the collection, windows frame...; When [character] closes the window, Jackson suggests... Doors. Doors and locks appear just as often. If a window shows a life a character might want, a door is the point where they are let in or kept out: the door no one answers in ‘The Daemon Lover’, David’s single key in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, the doors Harris opens in ‘The Villager’. Word bank: access, entry, refusal, being locked out, retreat, privacy, the threshold of another life (ideas); knock, wait, hesitate, enter, retreat, hope, give up (actions); desperation, relief, rejection, embarrassment, loneliness, hope (feelings).

Social Judgement Across the Stories

Few of Jackson’s characters can stop thinking about what other people will make of them, and that worry shapes almost everything they do.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘Why don’t I go to the police–you go to the police for a missing person. And then thought, What a fool I’d look like.’ She would rather keep searching than be laughed at.

  • ‘The Villager’: ‘He wandered over and opened the bathroom door; when he glanced in Miss Clarence winced.’ She fears the mess will be taken for hers.

  • ‘The Renegade’: ‘anyone would tell on a chicken-killing dog; but he doesn’t have to be so pleased about it.’ She suspects the town is enjoying her embarrassment.

They judge in turn: the woman in ‘The Daemon Lover’ finds fault with her own face and apartment; David resents the shop clerks who fail to recognise him; Miss Clarence criticises the Robertses’ housekeeping; Mrs Walpole measures herself against the ‘country folk’ and comes up short. Some minor characters, though, seem not to care at all: the stranger in ‘The Witch’ ignores the mother’s disapproval, the Robertses leave their untidy apartment open to strangers, and the men of ‘The Renegade’ discuss killing the dog with no thought for its owner. Topic sentence options:

  • Jackson’s protagonists act less on what they want than on what they imagine others will think.

  • Jackson sets her anxious protagonists beside outsiders who do not care what anyone thinks.

  • The fear of being watched becomes a kind of prison for many of Jackson’s characters.

  • In Jackson’s stories, the people who ignore social judgement are often the most unsettling.

Sentence starters: In [story], [character] worries that...; Jackson uses [character] to show...; The same fear appears in [story], where... | When [character] thinks ‘...’, Jackson reveals...; [Character]’s choice to ‘...’ shows...; The detail of ‘...’ suggests...; [Character]’s reaction to ‘...’ exposes... | This shows...; As a result...; Similarly...; By contrast...; However...

Racism and Social Judgement

In ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ and ‘Flower Garden’, racism is one form of the social judgement that runs through the whole collection. It harms the people it targets, like Boyd and Mr Jones, but it also governs the people who practise it, and nobody in either story is better off for it.

  • Reasons for social judgement: protecting status; fear of being judged in turn; wanting to fit in; anxiety about losing respectability; the comfort of feeling superior; prejudice treated as common sense; keeping up polite appearances; copying what others do; not wanting to cross powerful neighbours.

  • Impacts of social judgement: hurt and humiliation; confusion about how to respond; people pushed to the edge of the community; feelings kept hidden; quiet resistance; friendships broken; the existing order kept in place; defensiveness when prejudice is exposed.

Model paragraph: How does Jackson explore racial prejudice in her short stories?

  • Topic sentence: Of all the forms of judgement in her stories, racism is the one Jackson shows most clearly keeping people in their place.

  • Introduce and analyse an example: In ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, Mrs Wilson’s kindness to Boyd depends on his family being poor. Her confident announcement that ‘Boyd will eat anything’ and her questions about his father’s work assume a hardship that does not exist. Each time Boyd corrects her, her sense of her own generosity loses its footing, until she withdraws the gingerbread and claims to be merely ‘disappointed’. Her prejudice, Jackson suggests, is less about Boyd than about her need to feel above him.

  • Link to a further example: Boyd is untouched by Mrs Wilson’s assumptions, but in ‘Flower Garden’ the cost of racism falls on someone with far less protection.

  • Introduce and analyse an example from a second story: When Mrs MacLane hires Mr Jones to work in her garden, she breaks a rule no one in the town will say aloud. The neighbours advise Mrs Winning to speak to her ‘about that colored man’, and Mrs Winning, fearing that ‘they’ll stop coming to me first’, abandons her friend. The flourishing garden, the clearest sign of Mrs MacLane’s new life, fails as the town’s coldness grows, and a fallen branch finally crushes it.

  • Synthesising sentence: Across both stories, Jackson shows racism working as a quiet system of control that shapes the choices of those who practise it as much as the lives of those it targets.

Elizabeth and Clara: Confidence and Uncertainty

Elizabeth and Clara look like opposites: Elizabeth plans and acts, while Clara drifts through her day in a drugged haze. Sort these adjectives into ‘Elizabeth’, ‘Clara’ or ‘both’: anxious, decisive, commanding, insecure, uneasy, unstable, torn, practical, confident, timid, stubborn, unsettled, assured, self-conscious, determined, lost. Sentence frames: At first Elizabeth seems far more... than Clara in ‘The Tooth’, but both women are...; Elizabeth acts... when she..., yet she is also...; Clara appears... because..., but she shows she can be... when she... Analyses of protagonists to match to characters (one analysis may suit more than one character):

  • behind a brisk manner, she needs other people’s approval more than she admits;

  • starts out hesitant and gradually becomes surer of what she wants;

  • keeps up a polished front that other people cannot see past;

  • acts confident in order to hide how frightened she is;

  • drifts between different versions of herself without settling on one;

  • swings between certainty and doubt;

  • starts the day sure of her plans and ends it alone and bewildered;

  • daydreams of a better self because real life feels out of her control;

  • feels secure only because she does exactly what is expected;

  • clings to her home and routines for reassurance.

Characters to match: the woman in ‘The Daemon Lover’, David (‘Like Mother Used to Make’), Miss Clarence (‘The Villager’), Mrs Walpole (‘The Renegade’), Mrs Wilson (‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’), Laurie’s mother (‘Charles’), Mrs Winning (‘Flower Garden’), Elizabeth, Mrs Tylor (‘Of Course’), Clara (‘The Tooth’). Then place each protagonist on a line from sure of themselves to uncertain, once for the start of their story and once for the end. Practice topic: In Jackson’s stories, characters become who society expects them to be. Discuss.

Organising Evidence for Essays

For each key idea, gather material from three or four stories: who the characters are, what they feel and do, and the quotations and symbols that carry the idea. Use the Key Vocabulary notes to remind yourself what each idea covers, and the Story Study Guides to find matching stories and symbols.

Social Judgement and Exclusion

Topic: Jackson’s characters are often shut out, and just as often shut themselves away. Discuss.

  • Topic sentence: Jackson’s protagonists are frequently left on the outside, either because they do not fit or because they fear they will not.

  • Initial examples from one story: In [story], [character] is excluded when...; Jackson draws attention to this through...; The result is...

  • Link: [Story] shows a different kind of exclusion, one that [character] chooses...

  • Further examples from another story: Here Jackson...; Through [character]’s..., we see...

  • Synthesising sentence: Read together, these stories show that...

A second topic sentence to try: In Jackson’s stories, characters often push others out in order to keep their own place secure.

Insecurity and Dissatisfaction

How does Jackson depict the frustrations that ordinary people keep to themselves? List other ways to say ‘frustrations’ and ‘keep to themselves’, then choose a sub-idea:

  • Jackson’s characters feel hemmed in by the sameness of their days.

  • Family duty breeds a resentment no one voices.

  • Living up to other people’s expectations leaves little room for one’s own.

  • A respectable surface hides bitter disappointment.

  • Ordinary life reminds many characters of what they meant to become.

  • Unable to change anything, the characters daydream instead.

  • Private grudges and self-doubt drive what characters do.

  • Jackson uses chores, errands and meals to expose unhappiness.

In Jackson’s stories, characters’ discontent comes both from within and from the world around them. Discuss. Sub-ideas:

  • Hardly anyone in Jackson’s stories is happy with their lot.

  • Much of the characters’ misery grows out of their own self-doubt.

  • The characters expect more from life than it is ever likely to give.

  • Small towns and tight-knit circles narrow what characters are allowed to be.

  • What characters want keeps colliding with what the people around them expect.

  • Distrust of others, and of themselves, keeps characters stuck.

  • Society hands characters an identity, and they grow unhappy inside it.

  • The judgement characters fear most often comes from inside their own heads.

Unexpected Events

In Jackson’s stories, the safest places can suddenly become dangerous. Discuss. Treat it in two halves: the safety of homes, towns and routines, and the moments that turn them dangerous. Gather words and examples for each, then write one paragraph that first shows the safe world Jackson builds and then shows how she breaks it. In Jackson’s stories, everyday life is predictable on the surface and fragile underneath. This topic is less about the shocks themselves than about what they show: how little it takes to crack the routines people rely on. List evidence of ‘predictable’ and of ‘fragile’, then write a paragraph on each.

The Violence Beneath a Civilised Society

A process to follow: underline the key words; list other ways to say them; go back to the stories and your notes for examples; group the examples into three or four sets, one per body paragraph; write a topic sentence for each set; then draft. Practise on:

  • In Jackson’s stories, good manners often cover something much darker. To what extent do you agree?

  • Jackson’s stories find their most shocking violence in ordinary routines. Discuss.

  • Behind the respectable lives in Jackson’s stories lies something dark. Discuss.

  • In Jackson’s stories, cruelty hides in the most familiar places. Discuss.
