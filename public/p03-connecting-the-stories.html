Connecting the Stories

When you study a collection, you need to make connections between stories that can seem completely different. The activities below practise linking stories through shared ideas, symbols and character types, which is exactly what high-scoring Section A essays do.

Naming, or Not Naming, Protagonists

Many of Jackson’s protagonists have no name. The protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’ is never named, although her fiancé and many people she meets are. By contrast, David Turner’s name opens ‘Like Mother Used to Make’. Describing the two protagonists. The protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’ is nebulous, unsure, uncertain, undecided, operating in a liminal space, between life stages, doubtful, searching for direction, plagued by self-doubt, torn between possibilities (of/about herself, who she is, her identity, who she would like to be). David Turner is definite, particular, sure, individual, well defined, set in his habits, secure, clear about, guided by firm principles, anchored by routine (about himself, who he is, his identity, his aspirations). Model paragraph:

Many of Jackson’s unnamed characters exist in a state of uncertainty about who they are, while characters with names often appear more stable and confident. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, the unnamed protagonist wanders through the city searching for her missing fiancé. Her lack of a name reflects her weak sense of identity and how much she depends on becoming a ‘bride-to-be’. As she moves through apartments, streets and memories, she struggles to define herself outside the expectations of romance and marriage. Jackson develops this uncertainty through the protagonist’s fragmented thoughts and growing paranoia, suggesting that her identity is fragile and unstable. By contrast, in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, David Turner’s full name makes him seem more socially established and certain of his place in the world. However, Jackson still reveals cracks beneath this confidence as David desperately tries to appear sophisticated and domestically capable in front of Marcia and his guests. Although his name gives him a clearer public identity, his anxiety during the dinner party shows how easily constructed identities can fall apart. Through these contrasting characters, Jackson suggests that unnamed characters are often trapped in uncertainty and transition, while named characters possess greater social stability, even if that stability is ultimately fragile.

Topic sentence options:

  • While some of Jackson’s characters are unnamed to reflect their shifting sense of themselves, the naming of other characters reflects their individual sense of identity.

  • By withholding names from certain protagonists, Jackson emphasises their uncertain sense of self, in contrast to named characters whose identities appear more firmly constructed.

  • Many of Jackson’s characters have names that reflect their role rather than their sense of individuality, while other characters remain unnamed, representing their inner discontent.

  • Jackson often presents unnamed protagonists as psychologically uncertain figures, while named characters appear to possess greater social and personal stability.

  • In many of Jackson’s stories, anonymity reflects an unstable or transitional identity, whereas named characters are characterised by a stronger sense of certainty and control.

Evidence: ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘Anxiously she pulled through the dresses in the closet’; she ‘hesitated until a door at the end opened’; ‘suddenly horribly aware of her over-young print dress’; ‘There’s more than you think when you look at me.’ ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: ‘did everything in small, quick movements’; ‘He had always been partial to yellows and browns’; ‘It pleased him to have only one key to his home’; ‘I like things this way.’ Paragraph frame: In [story title], the unnamed/named character...; The character’s [lack of a name / full name] reflects...; As [character]..., Jackson suggests...; Jackson develops this idea through...; However, in [story title]...; Although [character] appears..., Jackson reveals...; Through these contrasting characters, Jackson suggests...

Windows and Doors

In Jackson’s stories windows are highly symbolic: when characters look out of them, they are often thinking about a different life they could have lived.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘She went to the window and saw that it was undeniably a fine day. It occurred to her that perhaps she ought not to wear the blue silk dress; it was too plain, almost severe, and she wanted to be soft, feminine.’

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: ‘The window had been open all day and papers had blown wildly around the floor. David closed the window, hesitated over the papers, and then moved away quickly.’

  • ‘The Villager’: ‘The first thing Miss Clarence did was go to the window... far off to the left, a high building crowned with flower gardens. Someday I’ll live there, she thought.’

  • ‘The Witch’: ‘The little boy was looking out the window... “I saw a witch,” he said.’

In each, a character is having one kind of experience in their own world while seeing, or avoiding, a different kind of experience through the window. Words to describe this: certainty, escape, possibility, freedom, discipline, logical thinking, separation between worlds, wanting more from life, comfort, belonging, imagination, the divide between, longing, desire for change, seeing beyond ordinary life, emotional escape, new experiences, responsibility, domestic harmony, dreams, fantasy, another life, hope, discontent with ordinary life, different futures, order, control. Sentence starters: Throughout her fiction, Jackson uses windows as symbols of...; In many of her stories, Jackson positions windows as a means of revealing...; Repeatedly, Jackson uses windows to emphasise...; Throughout Jackson’s work, windows function as representations of...; In story after story, Jackson uses windows to draw attention to...; Jackson’s recurring use of windows highlights...; Across her stories, windows act as portals into...; Jackson repeatedly depicts windows as...; In many of Jackson’s stories, windows reveal... Doors. Her stories also feature many doors and locks. While windows symbolise possible different lives, doors represent actual ways to enter other lives or to shut other people out: the door that never opens in ‘The Daemon Lover’, David’s single key in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, the doors Harris opens in ‘The Villager’. Word bank: new experiences, opportunities for change, the ability to enter, access to another life, emotional withdrawal, exclusion from, being shut out, a sense of rejection, feeling of isolation (ideas); seek, desire, realise, resist, hope for, yearn, imagine, believe (what characters do); desperation for, anxiety, protection, hidden feelings, vulnerability, exclusion, social isolation, optimism, faith in transformation (feelings).

Social Judgement Across the Stories

One of the powerful forces oppressing Jackson’s characters is the social judgement they all feel. Her protagonists spend a lot of energy worrying about what others will think.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: ‘Why don’t I go to the police–you go to the police for a missing person. And then thought, What a fool I’d look like.’ She is worried about looking foolish and being pitied.

  • ‘The Villager’: ‘He wandered over and opened the bathroom door; when he glanced in Miss Clarence winced.’ She is worried that the dirty bathroom will be judged as hers.

  • ‘The Renegade’: ‘anyone would tell on a chicken-killing dog; but he doesn’t have to be so pleased about it.’ Mrs Walpole is worried that her neighbours are enjoying her humiliation.

The protagonists also judge others and themselves: the protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’ judges her own appearance and apartment; David judges the clerks who don’t recognise him; Miss Clarence judges the Robertses’ housekeeping; Mrs Walpole judges her own failures against the ‘country folk’. In comparison, many minor characters seem almost immune to judgement: the stranger in ‘The Witch’ doesn’t care what the mother thinks; the Robertses leave their messy apartment open for others to see; the grocer and the other men in ‘The Renegade’ don’t consider how the dog’s owner feels. Topic sentence options:

  • In Jackson’s stories, the protagonists often shape their behaviour based on how they think others will judge them.

  • Jackson contrasts anxious protagonists with confident outsiders who ignore social expectations and judgement.

  • Many of Jackson’s protagonists become trapped by their awareness of how they appear to others.

  • Throughout her collection, Jackson suggests that people who are unaffected by social judgement can appear threatening, strange or powerful.

Sentence starters: In the story..., the protagonist...; Jackson uses the character of... to demonstrate how...; Jackson develops this idea in... through the character of...; This idea is evident in... where... | Their belief that ‘...’ reveals...; ...’s decision to ‘...’ highlights...; When they ‘...’ it demonstrates...; In the scene where ‘...’ Jackson evokes...; Their reaction to ‘...’ demonstrates...; Jackson’s description of ‘...’ emphasises...; At the moment where they ‘...’ the reader can see...; The reference to ‘...’ reinforces... | This also reveals...; This further highlights...; This also demonstrates...; Through this, Jackson conveys...; As a result...; Furthermore...; Moreover...; In contrast...; However...

Racism and Social Judgement

The racism in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ and ‘Flower Garden’ is part of Jackson’s larger concern with how social judgement shapes society. Racism controls not only its victims, like Mr Jones in ‘Flower Garden’, but also those who act with prejudice. Like any form of social judgement, it makes everyone unhappy.

  • Reasons for social judgement: desire to maintain social status; fear of being judged by others; pressure to conform to community expectations; anxiety about losing belonging or respectability; need to feel morally or socially superior; acceptance of racist assumptions as ‘normal’; desire to appear polite; pressure to behave like others; worry about offending people with social power.

  • Impacts of social judgement: emotional hurt or humiliation; confusion about how to react; individuals become outsiders within the community; people hide their true thoughts and feelings; resistance to stereotypes; misunderstanding of relationships; reinforcement of the accepted social order; defensiveness and insecurity about being exposed as prejudiced.

Model paragraph: How does racism affect the characters in Shirley Jackson’s short stories?

  • Topic sentence: While social judgement of all kinds is present in her stories, Shirley Jackson explicitly depicts racism as one way of maintaining social order in society.

  • Introduce and analyse an example: The story ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ presents Mrs Wilson’s racism towards her son’s African-American friend, Boyd, as stemming from her need to position Boyd and his family as poor and working class in order to confirm her own identity as a superior middle-class white person. When Boyd resists Mrs Wilson’s racist assumptions, such as her expectation that he ‘will eat anything’, she is left ‘disappointed’ because her belief that all people of colour must conform to certain stereotypes has been proven wrong.

  • Link to a further example: However, while Boyd is able to withstand the controlling force of racism in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, the same is not true of the victim of social judgement in ‘Flower Garden’.

  • Introduce and analyse an example from a second story: In this story, the white Mrs MacLane unwittingly transgresses the unspoken racist attitudes of the locals and hires an African-American gardener. This results in her ostracism, and Mrs MacLane asks her one-time friend ‘what on earth is gone wrong?’ Mrs Winning is motivated by her recurrent thought that ‘something bad has happened’ and that she is losing her own privileged place within the small community by associating with Mrs MacLane. At the end of the story, Mrs MacLane’s garden is damaged by a broken branch, symbolising how social judgement and exclusion have broken her dream of a beautiful and fulfilled country life.

  • Synthesising sentence: Through these two stories, Jackson reveals how racism operates as a powerful controlling force within society, dictating the actions of both perpetrators and victims.

Elizabeth and Clara: Confidence and Uncertainty

In many ways Elizabeth and Clara seem completely different: Elizabeth is determined and decisive, while Clara floats through her story in a kind of delirium. Adjectives to sort into ‘Elizabeth’, ‘Clara’ or ‘both’: anxious, decisive, authoritative, insecure, uneasy, unstable, conflicted, grounded, confident, timid, principled, unsettled, assured, self-conscious, determined, steadfast. Sentence frames: While at first glance Elizabeth appears... compared with Clara from ‘The Tooth’, who is more..., underneath they are both...; Even though Elizabeth behaves in a... manner when she..., she is also... because...; Although Clara seems... because she..., she also demonstrates that she can be... when she... Analyses of protagonists to match to characters (one analysis may suit more than one character):

  • beneath a confident exterior, the character’s self-perception remains surprisingly fragile and dependent on social validation;

  • appears tentative and directionless in the beginning, but gradually develops a firmer understanding of themselves;

  • presents an assured and carefully constructed identity that resists external scrutiny;

  • confidence is not innate but carefully performed in order to conceal deep anxieties;

  • deeply uncertain, drifting between competing versions of herself without ever fully committing to one;

  • oscillates between confidence and self-doubt, never fully inhabiting either state;

  • begins the story with absolute conviction, yet ends it isolated, confused and uncertain of her own identity;

  • idealised visions of herself reflect a desperate desire for certainty and control;

  • projects confidence because she unquestioningly conforms to social expectations;

  • attachment to familiar domestic spaces reveals her need for stability and reassurance.

Characters to match: the protagonist of ‘The Daemon Lover’, David (‘Like Mother Used to Make’), Miss Clarence (‘The Villager’), Mrs Walpole (‘The Renegade’), Mrs Wilson (‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’), the mother in ‘Charles’, Mrs Winning (‘Flower Garden’), Elizabeth, Mrs Tylor (‘Of Course’), Clara (‘The Tooth’). Then place every protagonist on a spectrum from sure of themselves to uncertain, once for the beginning of their story and once for the end. Practice topic: In the world of Shirley Jackson, identity is constructed in response to the demands of society. Discuss.

Organising Evidence for Essays

For each key idea, collect evidence from three or four stories: the characters, their feelings and actions, and the quotes and symbols that show the idea. Re-read the summary of the idea in the Key Vocabulary notes, and use the annotation guides in the Story Study Guides to find stories and symbols that fit.

Social Judgement and Exclusion

Topic: Jackson’s stories are tales of exclusion and isolation. Discuss.

  • Topic sentence: Many of Jackson’s protagonists experience profound isolation because they struggle to fit society’s expectations of normality.

  • Initial examples from one story: In [story], Jackson shows how people are excluded when...; Jackson emphasises this idea by...; This exclusion leads to...

  • Link: In [story], Jackson presents another kind of social exclusion, when...

  • Further examples from another story: This story explores... and...; Through [character]’s experience of..., Jackson...

  • Synthesising sentence: Together, these stories suggest that...

A second topic sentence to try: Jackson’s stories also suggest that people often exclude others in order to protect their own sense of security and belonging.

Insecurity and Dissatisfaction

How does Jackson portray the inner frustration of ordinary lives? Brainstorm words for ‘inner frustration’ and ‘ordinary lives’, then choose a sub-idea:

  • Many of Jackson’s characters feel trapped by ordinary life.

  • The stifling effects of family expectations lead to quiet dissatisfaction.

  • Social expectations can leave people living with unmet expectations.

  • Beneath everyday appearances lies deep emotional disappointment.

  • For many characters, ordinary life brings frustration and the sense of unmet potential.

  • Unable to change their circumstances, Jackson’s characters often long for something more.

  • Insecurity and inner grievances quietly shape many of Jackson’s protagonists.

  • Through everyday routines and settings, Jackson reveals hidden despair.

Dissatisfaction in Jackson’s stories is both self-created and socially imposed. Discuss. Sub-ideas:

  • Jackson’s world is one of dissatisfaction and disillusionment.

  • For Jackson’s protagonists, unhappiness is rooted in their own insecurities.

  • In the world of Jackson’s short stories, discontent is fuelled by unrealistic expectations.

  • Beyond their own choices, many characters are constrained by the limitations of the community.

  • Jackson explores the tension between individual desires and social conformity.

  • Unable to trust others, or their own judgement, Jackson’s protagonists are trapped in unhappiness.

  • Jackson reveals a world populated by those who form unhappy identities reinforced by society.

  • Although they imagine themselves judged by others, Jackson’s characters are victims of their own self-censorship.

Unexpected Events

Jackson’s stories depict moments when the safety of everyday life becomes threatening. Discuss. This topic has two parts: everyday life is routine and safe; changes to routine and convention can reveal dangers. Brainstorm vocabulary and examples for ‘safety of everyday life’ and ‘threatening ideas’, then write one paragraph: in the first half, show how Jackson establishes a ‘safe world’; in the second, how she ‘threatens’ it. In Jackson’s stories, ordinary life is both predictable and fragile. Here the focus is less on the unexpected events themselves than on what they reveal about how easily the structures of everyday life can be broken. Brainstorm ‘predictable’ against ‘fragile’, then write a paragraph responding to each part.

The Violence Beneath a Civilised Society

An essay process: circle key words in the topic; brainstorm alternative vocabulary; refer back to the stories and your notes to brainstorm examples; sort examples into three or four groups as the basis for body paragraphs; plan the topic sentence for each group; write the essay. Try it on:

  • Politeness in Jackson’s short stories often masks something far more sinister. To what extent do you agree?

  • In Jackson’s stories, violence emerges most shockingly from everyday life. Discuss.

  • Jackson’s stories reveal darkness beneath respectable appearances. Discuss.

  • Jackson’s stories suggest that ordinary social settings can conceal disturbing cruelty. Discuss.
