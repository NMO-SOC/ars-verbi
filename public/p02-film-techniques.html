Film Techniques

List of Techniques

Film Techniques in Picnic at Hanging Rock
Technique Definition / Effect Example in the film
Extreme long shot Shows a subject from a great distance, often dwarfed by its surroundings. The girls appear tiny against the towering pinnacles of the Rock, emphasising human insignificance.
Low-angle shot Camera looks up at a subject, making it seem powerful or threatening. The Rock is frequently shot from below, so it towers over and dominates the characters.
High-angle shot Camera looks down on a subject, making it seem small or vulnerable. Marion looks down on the picnickers ‘like a lot of ants’; Mrs Appleyard is often positioned above others.
Framing How elements are arranged within the frame to direct attention or suggest relationships. Characters are enveloped by foliage or enclosed by rock walls, appearing subsumed by nature.
Slow motion Footage played slower than real time, creating a dreamlike or significant effect. Miranda at the stream and the final picnic images, suggesting a moment outside ordinary time.
Soft focus and diffused light Blurred edges and hazy light that soften the image. The morning at Appleyard and the picnic glow like paintings, idealising the girls and the bush.
Dissolve One image fades gradually into another, linking them. Michael’s swan vase gives way to Miranda and then to a real swan, linking the three.
Voice-over Narration heard over the images. Miranda’s opening ‘dream within a dream’; the closing report of Mrs Appleyard’s death.
Diegetic sound Sound that exists within the world of the film. The ticking of clocks, the thrum of the Rock, cicadas and birdsong.
Non-diegetic sound Sound added for the audience, such as the score. Zamfir’s pan flute and Bruce Smeaton’s music over the landscape.
Soundscape The combination of music, effects and ambient sound that builds atmosphere. A low rumble and shrieking, insistent noise during the climb create unease.
Costume Clothing used to communicate character, class and ideas. White muslin, gloves and hats at the college; Irma’s red dress on her return; Mrs Appleyard’s black mourning clothes.
Mise-en-scène Everything placed in the frame: setting, props, costume, lighting, actors’ positions. Mrs Appleyard’s study with its grandfather clock and portrait, signalling order and authority.
Symbolic colour Colour used to carry meaning. White suggests innocence and conformity; red links Miss McCraw’s dress, the red cloud and Irma’s return.
Cross-cutting Cutting between two scenes happening at the same time. Moving between the college and the Rock, or between Sara and Albert on the night of her death.
Close-up Tightly frames a face or object to reveal emotion or detail. Stopped watches, Miranda’s face, Sara’s photograph of Miranda.
Point-of-view shot Shows what a character sees. Michael watching the girls cross the stream; the girls looking up at the Rock.
Archival effect Footage styled to look like old film. The final picnic images simulate the limited frames of historic film, as if the past is a lost record.

Framing the Rock

How Weir frames the Rock is one of the most reusable pieces of evidence in the film. The camera presents the Rock as something that acts on the characters. Useful vocabulary:

  • What the Rock does (verbs): towers over, dwarfs, encloses, conceals, swallows, lures, beckons, traps, dominates, overwhelms.

  • What the Rock is (nouns): labyrinth, barrier, gateway, prison, predator, refuge, mystery, force, portal, wilderness.

  • What the Rock is like (adjectives): towering, ancient, mysterious, claustrophobic, menacing, alluring, hypnotic, overwhelming, otherworldly, unknowable.

  • How characters are framed within nature: vulnerable, subsumed, enveloped, immersed, diminished, overshadowed.

‘Through low-angle shots that make the Rock tower over the climbing girls, Weir portrays nature as an ancient, dominating force that dwarfs human ambition.’

Sound and Music

The soundtrack is one of Weir’s main tools for creating mystery. Notice how each element connects to an idea:

Sounds in Picnic at Hanging Rock and the ideas they connect to
Sound What it connects to
Classical music (such as Beethoven)
  • The human construct of measurable time
  • European order imposed on beauty
Pan pipes
  • The timelessness and haunting allure of nature
  • Liberation from social convention
String quartet and parlour music
  • The constraints of etiquette
  • The preservation of social hierarchy
Thrumming of the Rock
  • The inexplicable power of the natural world
  • Its ancient rhythms
Shrieking, insistent noise
  • Human horror and discomfort in the face of the unknowable
Ticking clocks
  • Humanity’s desire to measure, schedule and control
Science-fiction-like beeping
  • Nature governed by incomprehensible forces
  • The transcendence of conventional time

‘Through the soundtrack, Weir demonstrates that humanity’s obsession with measuring time is ultimately pointless when compared with the timelessness of nature.’

Some of the music has a time signature (like most music you hear), some has a drifting, ethereal time signature (the pan pipes), and some has no time signature at all (the thrumming of the Rock). Music also creates mood. Other connections to consider: people’s desire to create structures and societal conventions; the quest for individual identity outside social mores; temporal order imposed on beauty; liberation from social convention; frustration with unanswered questions. Other topic sentences for a paragraph that refers only to sound:

  • The soundtrack of Weir’s film underscores how humanity’s desire to impose order contrasts with the mysteries of the natural environment.

  • Through the soundscape of his film, Weir presents how humans have a limited and incomplete understanding of enigmatic natural forces.

  • Through the music of the film, Weir suggests that nature offers liberation from the restrictive expectations of Edwardian society.

  • The music and soundscape of Picnic at Hanging Rock evoke the timeless rhythms of nature and the artificiality of human structures and conventions.

Costume

Costume tells us about class, conformity and transformation.

  • The schoolgirls: white dresses, gloves and hats suggest elegance, innocence and respectability, but also fragility and restraint. Those who climb remove hats, gloves, shoes and stockings; Edith keeps hers on.

  • Mrs Appleyard: dark, severe dresses, an ornate, old-fashioned hairstyle and a watch pinned to her chest. Later she wears a brooch with her late husband’s photograph, and at the end her carefully arranged hair becomes dishevelled.

  • Miss McCraw: a dark red dress and a veil to keep flies off her face.

  • Mademoiselle: lace blouses, frills, pearls, lace gloves and softly waving hair – elegant and romantic.

  • Miss Lumley: plaid, simple printed blouses, spectacles and muddy colours – sensible, muted, utilitarian.

  • Irma’s return: a vivid red dress among the white uniforms, linking her to the red cloud and marking her transformation.

  • Uniforms: the police, the doctor and the searchers (including the Indigenous tracker in a red coat) are identified by their roles, showing how society organises people by function and class.
