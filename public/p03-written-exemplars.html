Sample 1 - Model The Lottery and Other Stories Paragraph

Topic: The desire for social acceptance shapes the behaviour of many of Jackson’s characters. Do you agree? This paragraph uses a structure that works well for a collection: topic sentence, story one, link, story two, synthesising sentence. In many of her stories, Jackson depicts characters whose need for social acceptance is so strong that they repress or lie about aspects of themselves to appear more desirable or successful to others. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, the unnamed protagonist seeks social acceptance and happiness through marriage. Though the protagonist knows she is ‘haggard and lined’, she is unable to accept that on the day of her supposed wedding, her ‘young’ fiancé is missing because he has used and jilted her. When she finally locates him, her unwillingness to admit the truth results in her coming back to his door ‘many times’, where she is always locked out, representing that this dream is not possible for her. ‘The Villager’ also features a character who lies to herself and others in an attempt to appear more successful. In this story, Miss Clarence tells herself she has ‘a good job’ and is living ‘better than she would have in her home town’. But this attitude disguises a discontent that she has not pursued the dreams of being a dancer she had when she was younger. She compulsively lies to a stranger that she is a ‘dancer’, revealing her desire to be seen as someone more accomplished and interesting than she believes herself to be. At the story’s end, Jackson describes how the protagonist’s ‘shoulders ached’, symbolically representing the burden of carrying dissatisfaction. Through these examples, Jackson demonstrates that the desire to be accepted and admired by others can cause individuals to construct false identities and deny the bitter reality of their lives.

Sample 2 - Model The Lottery and Other Stories Essay

Topic: Jackson’s stories suggest that ordinary social settings can conceal disturbing cruelty. Discuss. Introduction

Shirley Jackson’s The Lottery and Other Stories (1949) is set in the most familiar places imaginable: city apartments, trains, small-town streets and village squares. Yet beneath these ordinary surfaces Jackson uncovers cruelty that is all the more disturbing for being so casual. Through stories such as ‘The Lottery’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘The Witch’ and ‘Flower Garden’, Jackson suggests that cruelty is learned, normalised and enforced by respectable people, and that the polite rituals of community life can disguise, and even sustain, a deep lack of compassion. Body Paragraph 1

Jackson’s most chilling insight is that the familiar rituals of community life can sanction violence, as ‘The Lottery’ makes devastatingly clear. The story opens on a morning that is ‘clear and sunny, with the fresh warmth of a full-summer day’, and the villagers gather to chat about ‘planting and rain, tractors and taxes’. This deliberately ordinary setting, narrated in a calm, understated voice, lulls the reader into expecting a harmless civic event. The tradition is upheld by authority figures, Mr Summers and Mr Graves, and defended by Old Man Warner, who dismisses villages that have abandoned it as a ‘pack of crazy fools’ and insists ‘there’s always been a lottery’. Even Mr Adams, who mentions that other villages are ‘talking of giving up the lottery’, joins the crowd at the end. That ‘although the villagers had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones’ suggests a violence that has outlived any meaning the tradition once had. By embedding a murder in the routine of a village fair, Jackson exposes how communities can perform cruelty while believing themselves civilised. Body Paragraph 2

Jackson further suggests that cruelty is passed on through everyday family life, as children learn from the adults around them what is normal. In ‘The Renegade’, Mrs Walpole’s neighbours respond to her dog’s killing of chickens with cheerful, increasingly violent advice, and her own children delight in imagining how a spiked collar would make ‘the spikes cut her head off’. Their laughter turns a domestic problem into a scene of shocking cruelty, and the story ends with Mrs Walpole ‘suddenly feeling the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat’, as if the community’s violence had turned on her. Similarly, in ‘The Witch’, a friendly stranger on a train tells a little boy that he ‘cut off her head and her hands’, and the boy responds not with fear but with breathless excitement: ‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’ The pleasant setting of a train journey, complete with a baby ‘busy with her rattle and her toast’, makes the boy’s fascination deeply unsettling. Through these children, Jackson reveals that cruelty is not an aberration but something absorbed and repeated within ordinary homes. In ‘The Lottery’, too, ‘someone gave little Davy Hutchinson a few pebbles’. Body Paragraph 3

However, Jackson’s cruelty is not always physical: in many stories it takes the quieter form of exclusion, enforced through polite social behaviour. In ‘Flower Garden’, the town never openly attacks Mrs MacLane; instead, neighbours advise Mrs Winning to speak to her ‘about that colored man’, and Mrs MacLane recognises that soon ‘they’ll tell someone else to speak to me’. The cruelty lies in silence and withdrawal, culminating as Mrs Winning turns away ‘without speaking’ and walks ‘with great dignity’ back to the old Winning house. The phrase ‘great dignity’ is bitterly ironic: respectability itself is the instrument of exclusion. In ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, Mrs Wilson’s apparently generous offers to Boyd are built on racist assumptions, and her offence when he declines shows how prejudice can hide behind kindness. In these stories, Jackson suggests that the most damaging cruelty is often performed by people who believe they are behaving well. Conclusion

Across The Lottery and Other Stories, Jackson reveals that ordinary social settings are not refuges from cruelty but often its source. Whether in the ritual of the village lottery, the casual advice of neighbours, a story told on a train, or the polite withdrawal of a friend, Jackson exposes a society in which violence and prejudice are normalised beneath a civilised surface. Her quiet, matter-of-fact style ensures that readers recognise these communities as their own, and that recognition is what makes her stories so disturbing.

Sample 3 - Essay Plan

Topic: Unexpected encounters force confrontation with uncomfortable truths. How is this idea explored in Jackson’s stories? Contention

Jackson uses unexpected encounters to expose the dissatisfaction, prejudice and cruelty that already lie beneath ordinary lives, though her characters do not always confront these truths; often they retreat from them. BP1: Jackson uses unexpected encounters to expose individuals’ dissatisfaction with their identities and their desire for a different life.

  • ‘The Villager’: Harris’s unexpected arrival prompts Miss Clarence to invent a more successful identity as a ‘dancer’, exposing her disappointment with her own life; ‘Her shoulders ached.’

  • ‘The Tooth’: the man in the blue suit offers Clara visions of ‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, exposing her longing to escape her grey life.

BP2: Jackson portrays unexpected encounters as exposing the prejudices and cruelty that exist beneath the polite surface of society.

  • ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’: Boyd’s visit exposes Mrs Wilson’s racist assumptions beneath her ‘kindness’.

  • ‘The Witch’: the stranger’s violent story exposes the boy’s fascination with cruelty and the mother’s powerlessness to control what he learns.

  • ‘Of Course’: the new neighbours expose the pressure to conform behind neighbourly politeness.

BP3: However, Jackson also suggests that unexpected encounters do not always lead to genuine confrontation; characters often avoid the truth.

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: Mr Harris’s arrival exposes David’s passivity, but David fails to challenge Marcia’s assumption of ownership over his home.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: the protagonist refuses to admit she has been jilted and keeps knocking at a door that never opens.

  • ‘Charles’: Laurie’s mother learns the truth only at the very end, after weeks of choosing not to see it.
