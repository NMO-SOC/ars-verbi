Sample 1: Model The Lottery and Other Stories Paragraph

Topic: The desire for social acceptance shapes the behaviour of many of Jackson’s characters. Do you agree? This paragraph uses a structure that works well for a collection: topic sentence, story one, link, story two, synthesising sentence. In many of her stories, Jackson depicts characters whose need for social acceptance is so strong that they repress or lie about aspects of themselves to appear more desirable or successful to others. In ‘The Daemon Lover’, the unnamed protagonist seeks social acceptance and happiness through marriage. Though the protagonist knows she is ‘haggard and lined’, she is unable to accept that on the day of her supposed wedding, her ‘young’ fiancé is missing because he has used and jilted her. When she finally locates him, her unwillingness to admit the truth results in her coming back to his door ‘many times’, where she is always locked out, representing that this dream is not possible for her. ‘The Villager’ also features a character who lies to herself and others in an attempt to appear more successful. In this story, Miss Clarence tells herself she has ‘a good job’ and is living ‘better than she would have in her home town’. But this attitude disguises a discontent that she has not pursued the dreams of being a dancer she had when she was younger. She compulsively lies to a stranger that she is a ‘dancer’, revealing her desire to be seen as someone more accomplished and interesting than she believes herself to be. At the story’s end, Jackson describes how the protagonist’s ‘shoulders ached’, symbolically representing the burden of carrying dissatisfaction. Through these examples, Jackson demonstrates that the desire to be accepted and admired by others can cause individuals to construct false identities and deny the bitter reality of their lives.

Sample 2: Model The Lottery and Other Stories Essay

Topic: Jackson’s stories suggest that ordinary social settings can conceal disturbing cruelty. Discuss. Introduction

Shirley Jackson’s The Lottery and Other Stories (1949) is set in the most familiar places imaginable: city apartments, trains, small-town streets and village squares. Yet beneath these ordinary surfaces Jackson uncovers cruelty that is all the more disturbing for being so casual. Through stories such as ‘The Lottery’, ‘The Renegade’, ‘The Witch’ and ‘Flower Garden’, Jackson suggests that cruelty is learned, normalised and enforced by respectable people, and that the polite rituals of community life can disguise, and even sustain, a deep lack of compassion. Body Paragraph 1

Jackson’s most chilling insight is that the familiar rituals of community life can sanction violence, as ‘The Lottery’ makes devastatingly clear. The story opens on a morning that is ‘clear and sunny, with the fresh warmth of a full-summer day’, and the villagers gather to chat about ‘planting and rain, tractors and taxes’. This deliberately ordinary setting, narrated in a calm, understated voice, lulls the reader into expecting a harmless civic event. The tradition is upheld by authority figures, Mr Summers and Mr Graves, and defended by Old Man Warner, who dismisses villages that have abandoned it as a ‘pack of crazy fools’ and insists ‘there’s always been a lottery’. Even Mr Adams, who mentions that other villages are ‘talking of giving up the lottery’, joins the crowd at the end. That ‘although the villagers had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones’ suggests a violence that has outlived any meaning the tradition once had. By embedding a murder in the routine of a village fair, Jackson exposes how communities can perform cruelty while believing themselves civilised. Body Paragraph 2

Jackson further suggests that cruelty is passed on through everyday family life, as children learn from the adults around them what is normal. In ‘The Renegade’, Mrs Walpole’s neighbours respond to her dog’s killing of chickens with cheerful, increasingly violent advice, and her own children delight in imagining how a spiked collar would make ‘the spikes cut her head off’. Their laughter turns a domestic problem into a scene of shocking cruelty, and the story ends with Mrs Walpole ‘suddenly feeling the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat’, as if the community’s violence had turned on her. Similarly, in ‘The Witch’, a friendly stranger on a train tells a little boy that he ‘cut off her head and her hands’, and the boy responds not with fear but with breathless excitement: ‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’ The pleasant setting of a train journey, complete with a baby ‘busy with her rattle and her toast’, makes the boy’s fascination deeply unsettling. Through these children, Jackson reveals that cruelty is not an aberration but something absorbed and repeated within ordinary homes. The same acceptance of violence appears in ‘The Lottery’, where ‘someone gave little Davy Hutchinson a few pebbles’. Body Paragraph 3

However, Jackson’s cruelty is not always physical: in many stories it takes the quieter form of exclusion, enforced through polite social behaviour. In ‘Flower Garden’, the town never openly attacks Mrs MacLane; instead, neighbours advise Mrs Winning to speak to her ‘about that colored man’, and Mrs MacLane recognises that soon ‘they’ll tell someone else to speak to me’. The cruelty lies in silence and withdrawal, culminating as Mrs Winning turns away ‘without speaking’ and walks ‘with great dignity’ back to the old Winning house. The phrase ‘great dignity’ is bitterly ironic: respectability itself is the instrument of exclusion. In ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, Mrs Wilson’s apparently generous offers to Boyd are built on racist assumptions, and her offence when he declines shows how prejudice can hide behind kindness. In these stories, Jackson suggests that the most damaging cruelty is often performed by people who believe they are behaving well. Conclusion

Across The Lottery and Other Stories, Jackson reveals that ordinary social settings are not refuges from cruelty but often its source. Whether in the ritual of the village lottery, the casual advice of neighbours, a story told on a train, or the polite withdrawal of a friend, Jackson exposes a society in which violence and prejudice are normalised beneath a civilised surface. Her quiet, matter-of-fact style ensures that readers recognise these communities as their own, and that recognition is what makes her stories so disturbing.

Sample 3: Essay Plan

Topic: Unexpected encounters force confrontation with uncomfortable truths. How is this idea explored in Jackson’s stories? Contention

Jackson uses unexpected encounters to expose the dissatisfaction, prejudice and cruelty that already lie beneath ordinary lives, though her characters do not always confront these truths; often they retreat from them. BP1: Jackson uses unexpected encounters to expose individuals’ dissatisfaction with their identities and their desire for a different life.

  • ‘The Villager’: Harris’s unexpected arrival prompts Miss Clarence to invent a more successful identity as a ‘dancer’, exposing her disappointment with her own life; ‘Her shoulders ached.’

  • ‘The Tooth’: the man in the blue suit offers Clara visions of ‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, exposing her longing to escape her grey life.

BP2: Jackson portrays unexpected encounters as exposing the prejudices and cruelty that exist beneath the polite surface of society.

  • ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’: Boyd’s visit exposes Mrs Wilson’s racist assumptions beneath her ‘kindness’.

  • ‘The Witch’: the stranger’s violent story exposes the boy’s fascination with cruelty and the mother’s powerlessness to control what he learns.

  • ‘Of Course’: the new neighbours expose the pressure to conform behind neighbourly politeness.

BP3: However, Jackson also suggests that unexpected encounters do not always lead to genuine confrontation; characters often avoid the truth.

  • ‘Like Mother Used to Make’: Mr Harris’s arrival exposes David’s passivity, but David fails to challenge Marcia’s assumption of ownership over his home.

  • ‘The Daemon Lover’: the protagonist refuses to admit she has been jilted and keeps knocking at a door that never opens.

  • ‘Charles’: Laurie’s mother learns the truth only at the very end, after weeks of choosing not to see it.

Section A Sample Essays: The Lottery (Low, Medium and High)

Topic: In Jackson’s ‘The Lottery’, the villagers conform because they are afraid of being excluded. To what extent do you agree?
Sample Essay I (Low)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson shows a village that follows a ritual without question. I agree that the villagers conform because they are afraid of being excluded. Jackson uses tradition, Tessie Hutchinson and the crowd to show this.
Body Paragraph 1

Jackson shows that tradition keeps the villagers in line. The villagers all come to the square on time.Ideas & interpretationScene-setting with no link to the question. Say what the punctuality shows about conformity. Mrs Graves and Mrs Delacroix talk and laugh while they wait. This shows the villagers are friendlyLanguage & techniqueA vague judgement. Friendliness does not support the claim about fear, so this point needs a different reason., but they still follow the lottery because it is what they have always done. Old Man Warner says ‘There’s always been a lottery’, which shows they will not change.
Body Paragraph 2 So fear of breaking with the ritual is what keeps them coming back.

Jackson shows that people silence anyone who breaks with the group. Tessie is the only one who complains. She says ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’. The other villagers tell her to be a good sport. This shows people are afraid of being different, so they make her quiet.Ideas & interpretationGood instinct, but the claim is asserted rather than shown. Use the words of the speakers as evidence.
Body Paragraph 3 So conformity is enforced by pressure as much as by belief.

The final scene shows the crowd acting on its fear. At the end, the villagers pick up stones. Old Man Warner tells everyone to come on. This shows the crowd is scared and wants to join in so they are not left out.
Conclusion This is the result of conforming out of fear of being excluded.

Jackson shows the villagers conform because they are afraid. So I agree with the statement.Structure & evidenceThe conclusion restates the position with no reason. Explain how the story supports the claim.
Sample Essay II (Medium)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson presents a village whose members gather each June to take part in a ritual none of them questions. I agree that the villagers conform out of fear of being excluded, though the story suggests the fear is learned rather than simply felt. Jackson builds the pressure through the power of tradition, the crowd’s manners and the way Tessie is isolated.
Body Paragraph 1

The villagers do not discuss the lottery; they simply attend it.Ideas & interpretationA precise topic sentence that makes a claim about behaviour rather than describing events. Old Man Warner dismisses the talk of change as ‘Pack of crazy fools’, and insists ‘There’s always been a lottery.’ His certainty shows that the ritual survives because nobody is willing to be the one who breaks it. The narration confirms this, noting that no one liked to upset ‘even as much tradition as was represented by the black box’.
Body Paragraph 2 That is why the ritual survives, even though nobody defends it.

Tessie’s protest is what exposes the fear. When she cries ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’, her neighbours do not argue the point. Mrs Delacroix calls ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’, and Mrs Graves reminds her that ‘All of us took the same chance.’ The phrase is a warning: conformity is presented as fairness, and dissent is treated as poor mannersLanguage & techniqueStrong analysis of how the story uses the language of fairness to enforce conformity..
Body Paragraph 3 Conformity is therefore presented as the reasonable choice.

The stoning shows what exclusion finally looks like. Once Tessie is singled out, the crowd moves towards her, and Old Man Warner’s ‘Come on, come on, everyone’ pushes the reluctant villagers into the action. The fear of being left out is greater than any sympathy for TessieIdeas & interpretationA clear claim about the crowd's priorities, supported by the sequence of events., and the villagers join in rather than stand apart.
Conclusion Fear of exclusion, not sympathy, decides what the crowd does.

Jackson shows that the villagers conform because exclusion is worse than the violence they must commit. The ritual is kept alive by people who would rather be part of the crowd than face being the outsider. The fear is real, so even if it is never spoken, it shapes every choice the villagers make.
Sample Essay III (High)

Introduction

Shirley Jackson opens ‘The Lottery’ with a morning that is ‘clear and sunny’, and the pleasantness is the first thing the story asks us to distrustIdeas & interpretationA sharp opening that treats the setting as part of the argument, not as description.. I agree that the villagers conform because they fear exclusion, but Jackson’s argument is more unsettling than that. The villagers are not forced. They have learned to read their own belonging as something they must keep up, and the fear of being separated from the crowd does the rest. Jackson builds this through the normality of the ritual, the inheritance of tradition and the isolation of Tessie.
Body Paragraph 1

The story frames the ritual as ordinary. The children assemble first, ‘of course’, and the women talk among themselves while they wait. Mrs Hutchinson arrives late, having ‘clean forgot what day it was’, which suggests the lottery is so routine that it can be forgotten and still be attended. Conformity starts before anybody is asked to conformStructure & evidenceStrong structural point: it tells the assessor where the argument begins., because the day has already been made normal.
Body Paragraph 2 Conformity therefore begins in the ordinary, before any fear is needed.

Old Man Warner shows how conformity is passed down. He calls the north village’s doubts ‘Pack of crazy fools’, then offers the proverb ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’. He is not defending the lottery on grounds of safety; he links it to survival, so anyone who questions it threatens the community’s shared sense of its own security. The narration’s aside that no one liked to upset ‘even as much tradition as was represented by the black box’ shows that the tradition has outlasted any reason for it.
Body Paragraph 3 The tradition survives because each generation learns the same fear.

Tessie’s protest is what exposes the mechanism. Her ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’ is met with ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’, and then with Mrs Graves’s ‘All of us took the same chance.’ That reply turns fairness into a matter of who is willing to play alongLanguage & techniquePrecise word choice that turns a phrase into an argument about power.. The crowd’s first move against her is verbal before it is physical. By the time the stones fly, Tessie has already been reduced to someone who must be silenced so that everyone else can feel safe in their place.
Conclusion Exclusion is the mechanism, and the crowd’s fear of it keeps the ritual going.

Jackson’s village conforms because each person has been taught that the group’s approval is the only safety available. The black box, the proverbs and the good-sport rhetoric all teach that the outsider is the real danger. I agree with the statement, though the story suggests that fear of exclusion is not merely a motive. It is the mechanism that keeps the lottery alive.

Section A Sample Essays: The Lottery (Low, Medium and High)

Topic: Jackson suggests that tradition is more powerful than reason. Do you agree?
Sample Essay I (Low)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson shows a village that keeps a ritual even though nobody can explain why. I agree that tradition is more powerful than reason in the story. Jackson uses the villagers’ sayings, the black box and the response to Tessie Hutchinson to show this.

Body Paragraph 1

Old Man Warner says ‘There’s always been a lottery’. This shows that tradition is important to the villagers. He also calls the people who want to stop ‘Pack of crazy fools’. This shows that tradition wins over reason. Tradition is what keeps the lottery going. So the village keeps the lottery because it has always done so, not because it makes sense.

Body Paragraph 2

The saying ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’ is repeated as if it were a reason. Nobody questions it. This shows the saying is more powerful than thinking. So reason never gets a hearing in the village.

Body Paragraph 3

At the end, the villagers pick up stones, and ‘Come on, come on, everyone’ is said. This shows tradition is stronger than the peopleIdeas & interpretationAsserted rather than shown: say how the ending makes tradition stronger, using the crowd's actions.. So the ending proves the same point.

Conclusion

Jackson shows tradition is more powerful than reason. So I agree with the statement.

Sample Essay II (Medium)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson presents a village that continues a ritual no one can justify. I agree that tradition is more powerful than reason, though the story suggests that tradition survives partly through fear. Jackson builds this through the saying the villagers repeat, the black box nobody will replace and the way the crowd answers Tessie Hutchinson’s appeal to fairness.

Body Paragraph 1

Jackson shows that the villagers keep the lottery because of what they have always done, not because of any reason they can give. Old Man Warner insists ‘There’s always been a lottery’, and the proverb ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’ is repeated as though it explains everything. The proverb ties the ritual to the harvest, a practical concern, but nobody checks whether the connection is real; the saying is accepted because it is old. Tradition therefore has force even where the reason behind it has disappeared.

Body Paragraph 2

The black box shows how far the village has let tradition outlast its meaningStructure & evidenceThe object is used as evidence for the idea, which is the step the medium band needs.. The narration notes that no one liked to upset ‘even as much tradition as was represented by the black box’, and Mr Summers’s repeated talk of making a new box never leads to one. The village would rather keep an old box than change a ritual whose reason has been lost. The object carries the practice when the reasons have gone.

Body Paragraph 3

Reason is answered with social pressure, not argument. When Tessie protests ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’, Mrs Delacroix replies ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’, and Mrs Graves adds that ‘All of us took the same chance.’ Neither reply addresses whether the drawing was fair; both appeal to belonging. So when reason is raised, tradition answers with the pressure of the group, and the ritual continues.

Conclusion

Jackson shows that the lottery survives because tradition has outlived its reasons and reason has no way to win against the group. I agree, though fear of exclusion is part of why tradition wins.

Sample Essay III (High)

Introduction

Shirley Jackson opens ‘The Lottery’ with a morning that is ‘clear and sunny’, and with a village that gathers without asking why. I agree that tradition is more powerful than reason, but the story shows that this power is not simply habit. Jackson builds it through the proverbs the villagers repeat, the black box they will not replace and the social pressure that answers Tessie Hutchinson’s appeal to fairness.

Body Paragraph 1

The proverbs carry tradition as if it were knowledge. The saying ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’ links the ritual to survival, and Old Man Warner warns that without it ‘we’d all be eating stewed chickweed and acorns’. The claim is a warning rather than an argumentIdeas & interpretationSays what kind of claim it is, and what that does to the question. Naming the move is what lifts the paragraph.. It sets the ritual against hunger, which is why questioning the lottery sounds reckless. Reason has no part in a proverb that presents the ritual as the thing that keeps people fed, and so tradition gains its power by seeming practicalLanguage & techniquePrecise language for how the proverb works: it sounds sensible even though its reason is never tested..

Body Paragraph 2

The black box shows that tradition can outlast its own materials. The narration says the villagers ‘had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones’. The ritual has become detached from its objects, yet its violence remains. Tradition persists in what it demands even when its form has gone, and the stones are the one part of the ritual the village has never let go of.

Body Paragraph 3

Reason has no hearing once the crowd has decidedStructure & evidenceA clear thematic topic sentence about reason and the group, not about a single character.. Tessie’s argument is about fairness, ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’, and she is answered with ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’ and ‘All of us took the same chance.’ The replies concern belonging, not fairness. ‘Come on, come on, everyone’ turns the crowd towards her, and ‘A stone hit her on the side of the head’ shows where the argument ends. So tradition wins not by being right but by being sharedIdeas & interpretationA confident conclusion to the argument. It answers the question by saying what kind of power tradition has..

Conclusion

Jackson shows that tradition is more powerful than reason because it does not need to be justified: it survives in proverbs, in objects and in the pressure to belong. I agree, though the story also shows what that power costs.
