Story Summaries and Analyses

The collection contains twenty-five stories. The twelve below are the key stories for essays: learn them well enough to use them flexibly across many topics.

‘The Daemon Lover’

Summary : On the morning she is to marry Jamie Harris, an unnamed woman of thirty-four prepares anxiously, changing her dress, tidying her apartment and stocking food for their new life. Jamie does not arrive. She goes looking for him, questioning shopkeepers, a newspaper seller and neighbours, who respond with indifference, suspicion or mockery. Following vague leads, she reaches an apartment building where she thinks she hears voices and laughter behind a door, but no one answers her knocking. Analysis : The protagonist is defined by deep-seated dissatisfaction: she is ‘not satisfied with her clothes, her face, her apartment’ and hopes marriage will transform her life. The recurring lack of food (‘There was nothing to eat in the apartment’) suggests a life that is empty and hollow. ‘Daemon’ can mean demon, but also a guiding or inspiring spirit; Jamie is both, offering hope and then vanishing cruelly. The casual cruelty of strangers (‘the boy grinned insolently at her’) shows how easily individual suffering is dismissed. The ending, with the door that never opens, is one of Jackson’s starkest images of exclusion.

‘Like Mother Used to Make’

Summary : David Turner keeps a neat, carefully chosen apartment and enjoys cooking and domestic order. His neighbour Marcia is loud, untidy and always hungry. David invites her to dinner and bakes a cherry pie. When Mr Harris, a man from Marcia’s office, knocks, Marcia lets him assume the apartment and the cooking are hers. David, unable to assert himself, is gradually pushed out and ends up retreating to Marcia’s cluttered apartment, hearing laughter from his own home down the hall. Analysis : Jackson contrasts David’s control (‘small, quick movements’, ‘the neat small table and four careful chairs’) with Marcia’s disorder (‘Marcia arrived with a shout and fresh air and disorder’). David’s pleasure in domestic activities challenges traditional ideas of masculinity, and his passivity leaves him excluded from his own home. The title is ironic: the comforts of home ‘like mother used to make’ are made by a man and claimed by a woman who did not make them.

‘The Villager’

Summary : Miss Hilda Clarence, a woman in her thirties who came to New York hoping to be a dancer, now works in an office. She visits an apartment to look at furniture for sale. While waiting, she is mistaken for the owner by a visitor named Harris, and slips into the role, inventing a life as a ‘dancer’. After he leaves, she returns to her own reality. Analysis : Harris’s unexpected arrival is the catalyst that exposes Miss Clarence’s disappointment with her life. She tells herself she has ‘a good job’ and lives ‘better than she would have in her home town’, but her compulsive lie reveals her desire to be seen as someone more accomplished and interesting. The final line, ‘Her shoulders ached’, suggests the weight of carrying unfulfilled dreams.

‘The Witch’

Summary : A little boy travels by train with his mother and baby sister, looking out the window. A friendly stranger sits beside him and, encouraged by the boy’s questions, tells a gruesome story about killing his own little sister. The mother is horrified, but the boy is delighted. When the man leaves, the mother tries to explain it away, and the boy decides ‘Prob’ly he was a witch.’ Analysis : The story shows how children learn what behaviour is normal. The baby ‘strapped securely to the seat’ and the boy rewarded with a lollipop suggest a world of control and reward, yet the boy greets violence with breathless excitement (‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’). The cruelty arrives in a pleasant, everyday setting, which makes it more disturbing. The boy’s final explanation turns real violence into a fairy tale.

‘The Renegade’

Summary : Mrs Walpole, new to the country, learns that her family’s dog, Lady, has been killing a neighbour’s chickens. As she goes about the town, neighbours offer increasingly cruel advice for curing or punishing the dog, including tying a dead chicken around her neck or fitting her with a spiked collar. When her own children eagerly describe how the spikes would cut the dog’s head off, Mrs Walpole feels as though she is being attacked herself. Analysis : A renegade is someone who rejects conventional behaviour; the dog is one, but so in a sense is Mrs Walpole, an outsider in the community. The neighbours’ casual enthusiasm normalises violence, and her children’s delight shows how readily cruelty is passed on. The ending, ‘the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat’, transfers the punishment from the dog to the woman, revealing her fear of the community’s judgement.

‘Charles’

Summary : Laurie starts kindergarten and comes home each day with stories about a boy called Charles who misbehaves dreadfully. His parents are fascinated and amused, and ‘Charles’ becomes a family byword. At the parent-teacher meeting, Laurie’s mother looks for Charles’s mother, only to learn from the teacher that there is no Charles in the class. Analysis : Laurie invents an identity to conceal his own bad behaviour, and his mother’s unquestioning acceptance shows how parents see what they want to see. The twist exposes the gap between appearance and reality, and the comic tone hides a sharper point about how adults misread, and help create, children’s behaviour.

‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’

Summary : Johnny brings his friend Boyd, a Black boy, home for lunch. Johnny’s mother, Mrs Wilson, assumes Boyd’s family is poor, his father a labourer, and his mother overworked, and offers him charity he does not need. Boyd politely corrects each assumption. Mrs Wilson becomes offended when he declines her old clothes, while the boys, untroubled, carry on with their game. Analysis : The title comes from a comic strip about two absurdly polite Frenchmen who keep deferring to each other; the boys use it to play at manners. Jackson uses it ironically to expose the gap between outward politeness and genuine respect. Mrs Wilson believes she is being kind, but her ‘kindness’ is built on racist stereotypes, and her defensiveness shows how entrenched they are.

‘Flower Garden’

Summary : Young Mrs Winning, who has married into the town’s oldest family and lives under her mother-in-law’s control in the old Winning house, befriends Mrs MacLane, a widow who moves into the cottage she herself once dreamed of owning. The two women grow close, and their sons play together. When Mrs MacLane hires a Black man, Mr Jones, to help with her garden, the town turns against her, and Mrs Winning, fearing association, abandons her. Analysis : The townspeople act as gatekeepers, advising Mrs Winning to speak to Mrs MacLane ‘about that colored man’. Mrs MacLane recognises what is happening: ‘Pretty soon they... they’ll tell someone else to speak to me.’ Children reveal the town’s values: Howard’s racist name-calling towards Billy Jones shows how early prejudice is learned. The ending, as Mrs Winning walks ‘with great dignity’ back to the old house, shows conformity winning over friendship.

‘Elizabeth’

Summary : Elizabeth Style works for a struggling literary agency in New York, in a relationship with her business partner that has lost its warmth, and in a life far from the success she once imagined. When a new secretary arrives, Elizabeth feels her position slipping. The story ends with her making a dinner date with Jim Harris, who she hopes might help launch a new career. Analysis : Elizabeth’s story is one of insecurity and quiet despair beneath a professional surface. Her plans with Harris represent the possibility of another life, but the reader suspects they are another illusion. Like the protagonists of ‘The Daemon Lover’ and ‘The Villager’, she pins her hopes on a man who may never deliver.

‘The Tooth’

Summary : Clara Spencer takes an overnight bus to New York to see a dentist about a painful tooth. Dazed by codeine and lack of sleep, she meets a man in a blue suit, Jim, who speaks of a beautiful faraway place. In the city, her sense of self dissolves; she cannot remember details of her own life and must find her name written on her belongings. At the end she runs barefoot through hot sand, hand in hand with Jim. Analysis : Jim represents Clara’s desire to escape her ordinary, grey life. His poetic visions (‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, ‘the grass is so green and so soft’) evoke a mythical place far from bus stations and dentists’ rooms. Clara’s name means ‘clear’ or ‘bright’, ironic for a woman whose identity has faded. Unusually, the ending suggests escape from social expectations rather than defeat, though whether it is liberation or madness is left open.

‘Of Course’

Summary : Mrs Tylor watches new neighbours move in next door and decides from their furniture and clothes that they are ‘nice people, obviously’. When she speaks to Mrs Harris, however, she learns that the family does not allow movies, radio or newspapers. Mrs Tylor, unsettled, finds herself agreeing politely. Analysis : The story turns on the pressure of neighbourliness. Mrs Tylor’s first judgements are based on appearances; Mrs Harris then projects her own rigid views through friendly conversation (‘it means such a lot to have really thoughtful neighbors’). Social conformity and the appearance of politeness are maintained, despite the intolerance beneath them.

‘The Lottery’

Summary : On the morning of June 27th, the villagers of a small farming community gather in the square for their annual lottery. Children collect stones. Mr Summers runs the drawing from a battered black box. Tessie Hutchinson arrives late, joking that she forgot what day it was. Heads of families draw slips of paper; Bill Hutchinson draws the marked slip. Tessie protests that it ‘wasn’t fair’, and in a second drawing among the Hutchinson family she draws the paper with the black spot. The villagers, including her friends and children, stone her. Analysis : ‘The Lottery’ is the anchor story of the collection: it shows every key idea. The pleasant setting (‘clear and sunny’) makes the violence more shocking. The ritual is maintained by authority figures (Mr Summers, Mr Graves), defended by Old Man Warner (‘There’s always been a lottery’), and carried out by ordinary people. Mr Adams mentions villages that have given up the lottery, yet still joins in. Tessie is no rebel: she accepts the lottery until it threatens her. The detail that ‘although the villagers had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones’ suggests that the violence outlives any meaning the tradition once had.
