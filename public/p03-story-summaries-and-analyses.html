The collection contains twenty-five stories. The VCAA 2027 text list sets twelve of them for study, and they are the twelve below: learn them well enough to use them flexibly across many topics. Jackson originally subtitled the collection The Adventures of James Harris, and a man of that name (or a man in a blue suit) drifts through many of these stories. Watch for him: he usually arrives just as a character’s grip on her ordinary life starts to loosen.

‘The Daemon Lover’

Summary : On the morning she is to marry Jamie Harris, an unnamed woman of thirty-four prepares anxiously. She writes to her sister Anne, agonises over whether to wear a blue silk dress Jamie has already seen or a print dress she fears looks too young, tidies her apartment and stocks food for their new life. Jamie does not arrive. By late morning she leaves him a note and goes looking for him. At the building where he said he lived, the superintendent and his wife have never heard of him, and the couple who lent their apartment to a man in a blue suit know almost nothing about him. The delicatessen man brushes her off, the newsdealer and a customer turn her questions into a joke, and the florist, while trying to sell her flowers, ‘remembers’ a man buying chrysanthemums. An old shoeshine man and a twelve-year-old boy finally say they saw a man with flowers go into a run-down house. On the top floor she knocks at a door and seems to hear voices and laughter, but no one answers. The other door opens on an empty attic where a rat watches her with an ‘evil face’, and she flees, tearing her dress. For weeks afterwards she returns to knock, morning and evening, but ‘no one ever came to the door.’ Analysis : The protagonist is defined by deep-seated dissatisfaction: she is ‘not satisfied with her clothes, her face, her apartment’ and hopes marriage will transform her life. In the 1940s, a woman unmarried at thirty-four faced real social pressure, which explains her urgency. The recurring lack of food (‘There was nothing to eat in the apartment’) suggests a life that is empty and hollow. Jackson slowly undermines the reader’s trust in her: she cannot clearly picture Jamie’s face, nobody official will confirm he exists, and the only people who claim to have seen him (a vague old man and a cheeky boy) are as unreliable as she is. The reader is pushed to re-read everything she has said. The title comes from an old Scottish ballad, ‘The Daemon Lover’, also known as ‘James Harris’, in which a lover lures a woman away with promises of a new life and then destroys her. ‘Daemon’ can mean demon, but also a guiding or inspiring spirit; Jamie is both, offering hope and then vanishing cruelly. The casual cruelty of strangers (‘the boy grinned insolently at her’) shows how easily individual suffering is dismissed. The real conflict is internal, between hope and reality, and the door that never opens is one of Jackson’s starkest images of exclusion. Links to other stories : Jamie’s blue suit returns in ‘The Tooth’ and ‘The Witch’; the lonely single woman pinning her hopes on a Harris reappears in ‘Elizabeth’ and ‘The Villager’.

‘Like Mother Used to Make’

Summary : David Turner shops carefully for dinner and returns to his small, neat apartment, which he has painted and furnished himself with great pride. He lets himself into the untidy apartment of his neighbour Marcia (she has no key to his, and he likes it that way) to leave her a reminder about dinner. Marcia arrives late, loud and hungry, and admires his home. David serves a cherry pie, only his third attempt, and worries it is too sour. When Mr Harris, a large man from Marcia’s office, calls by, Marcia lets him assume the apartment is hers, calls David ‘Davie’, and claims the pie as her own. David, unable to assert himself, washes every dish, is gently edged out by Marcia’s hostess manner, thanks her for dinner, and accepts her key. He ends in Marcia’s cluttered apartment, hearing laughter from his own home across the hall, and miserably begins to clean. Analysis : Jackson contrasts David’s control (‘small quick movements’, ‘the neat small table and four careful chairs’) with Marcia’s disorder (‘Marcia arrived with a shout and fresh air and disorder’). David’s home is an extension of his identity, so losing it is a psychological as well as a physical displacement. His pleasure in domestic activities challenges traditional ideas of masculinity, and his passivity makes him complicit: he never tells Mr Harris the truth. Dramatic irony drives the second half, since the reader knows what Mr Harris does not. Cleaning Marcia’s apartment is David’s weary attempt to rebuild a sense of order and self. The title is ironic: the comforts of home ‘like mother used to make’ are made by a man and claimed by a woman who did not make them. Links to other stories : The swapped identity in a borrowed apartment mirrors ‘The Villager’; the home as a symbol of the self also drives ‘The Renegade’ and ‘Flower Garden’.

‘The Villager’

Summary : Miss Hilda Clarence, about thirty-five, came to Greenwich Village from a small town twelve years ago hoping to become a dancer, but now works as a stenographer. She carries a novel she read only ‘up to page fifty and only carried now for effect’. Answering an advertisement, she visits an apartment to look at furniture for sale. The owner, Nancy Roberts, has left her a note to come in and look around. Miss Clarence finds the apartment dirty and the furniture not to her taste, but she lingers over a book of modern-dance photographs and tries one of the poses. Artie Roberts phones and tells her they are selling because he has the chance to go to Paris. When another prospective buyer, Harris, arrives, he assumes she is the owner, and she slips into the role, telling him she is a dancer and that ‘Artie’s going to Paris’. After he leaves, she writes a note for Mrs Roberts and returns to her own life. Analysis : Harris’s unexpected arrival is the catalyst that exposes Miss Clarence’s disappointment with her life. She tells herself she has ‘a good job’ and lives ‘better than she would have in her home town’, but her props (the half-read novel, the occasional dance recital) show how much she cares about appearing sophisticated. The Robertses seem to live the artistic life Miss Clarence came to the city for (Arthur is an artist with a chance to go to Paris), so she simply borrows it. The dance pose symbolises her abandoned ambition, and the strain it causes shows how hollow the attempt is. The story has no climax and no change: the pretence brings only brief relief. The final line, ‘Her shoulders ached’, suggests the weight of carrying unfulfilled dreams. Links to other stories : Compare David’s lost apartment in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’ and Elizabeth’s thwarted ambitions in ‘Elizabeth’.

‘The Witch’

Summary : A four-year-old boy, Johnny, travels on a nearly empty train with his mother and baby sister, looking out the window and announcing what he sees. Bored, he tells his mother he saw ‘a big old ugly old bad old witch’ and told her to go away. An elderly, friendly stranger sits beside him. Johnny gives silly answers to the usual questions (he is ‘Twenty-six’, his name is ‘Mr. Jesus’, his baby sister is ‘twelve-and-a-half’), and his mother corrects him. The man asks whether he loves his sister, then calmly tells a gruesome story about killing his own little sister, cutting her into pieces and putting her head in a cage with a bear. The mother is horrified and orders the man away, but the boy is delighted. As he leaves, the man and the boy share a laugh. The mother insists he was only teasing, gives Johnny a lollipop, and the boy decides ‘Prob’ly he was a witch.’ Analysis : The story shows how children learn what behaviour is normal. The baby ‘strapped securely to the seat’ and the boy rewarded with a lollipop suggest a world of control and reward, yet the boy greets violence with breathless excitement (‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’). Children in Jackson’s fiction are often less bound by social convention than adults: the mother reacts with horror because that is what respectable adults must do, while the boy simply enjoys the story. The old man appears just after the boy imagines a witch, as if the fantasy has summoned him, and he is the only adult who takes the boy’s imagination seriously. Evil arrives in a pleasant, everyday setting behind a harmless exterior, which makes it more disturbing. The boy’s final explanation turns real violence into a fairy tale. Links to other stories : The stranger who unsettles an ordinary journey recalls Jim in ‘The Tooth’; children who absorb cruelty appear in ‘The Renegade’ and ‘The Lottery’.

‘The Renegade’

Summary : Mrs Walpole, new to a small country town, is rushing her twins, Jack and Judy, off to school when a neighbour phones to say that the family dog, Lady, has been killing Mr Harris’s chickens and must be dealt with. Shaken, she goes to see Mrs Nash, who has already heard. On her way through town, neighbours offer increasingly cruel advice: Mr White suggests tying a dead chicken around the dog’s neck, and the grocer and his customer suggest worse, in a tone of amusement. Lady comes home ‘quietly, harmlessly’, with flecks of blood on her legs. When the twins return, they gleefully describe a spiked collar that would cut the dog’s head off if she chased a chicken, while patting Lady affectionately. Mrs Walpole goes outside for air but feels as though she is the one being punished. Analysis : The title is deliberately ambiguous. A renegade is someone who rejects conventional behaviour; the dog is one, but so in a sense is Mrs Walpole, an outsider who cannot bring herself to have Lady killed. The neighbours’ casual enthusiasm normalises violence: Lady kills by instinct, but the townspeople plan cruelty calmly and with pleasure. Even Mrs Walpole’s own first impulse is to beat the dog, so no one is entirely innocent. She is alienated within her own family too: her husband barely notices her, and her children’s delight shows how readily cruelty is passed on. Jackson drew on her own struggle to fit into a small Vermont town. The ending, ‘the harsh hands pulling her down, the sharp points closing in on her throat’, transfers the punishment from the dog to the woman, revealing her fear of the community’s judgement. Links to other stories : The hostile small town returns in ‘Flower Garden’ and, at its most extreme, ‘The Lottery’.

‘Charles’

Summary : Told in the first person by Laurie’s mother, the story follows Laurie’s first weeks at kindergarten. He immediately becomes ruder at home, calling his father ‘y’old dust mop’, and comes home each day with stories about a boy called Charles who misbehaves dreadfully: hitting the teacher, saying bad words, being kept after school. His parents are fascinated and amused, and ‘Charles’ becomes a family byword for any mischief. At the parent-teacher meeting, Laurie’s mother looks for Charles’s mother, then corners the teacher, who describes Laurie’s difficult adjustment in terms that match Charles exactly. The mother still misses the point, until the teacher says there is no Charles in the class. Analysis : Laurie invents an alter ego to confess his own bad behaviour without being punished, while keeping his parents’ affection and attention. The clues are all there (his new rudeness, his stomping, his pause before naming Charles), and the reader usually guesses the truth before the narrator does, which creates dramatic irony. A second irony is that the mother is eager to meet Charles’s mother, not realising it is herself. Her unquestioning acceptance shows how parents see what they want to see. The comic tone hides a sharper point about how adults misread, and help create, children’s behaviour. Links to other stories : The gap between appearance and reality also drives ‘Of Course’ and ‘The Lottery’; invented identities appear in ‘The Villager’.

‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’

Summary : Mrs Wilson is baking gingerbread when her son Johnny brings home his friend Boyd, a Black boy, who is carrying wood for their game. She scolds Johnny for letting Boyd carry the load and invites Boyd to lunch. Over scrambled eggs and tomatoes, she assumes Boyd’s family is poor, his father a labourer, and his mother overworked. Each assumption is corrected: his father is ‘a foreman’, his mother does not need to work, and his sister plans to become a teacher. Mrs Wilson then offers Boyd some of Johnny’s old clothes. Boyd politely says his family does not need them. Offended, she withdraws the gingerbread and says she is not angry, only disappointed. The boys, puzzled, go back outside to play, Johnny remarking that his mother is ‘screwy sometimes’. Analysis : The title comes from Alphonse and Gaston, a comic strip about two absurdly polite Frenchmen who keep deferring to each other; the boys use the phrase to play at manners, and it frames their easy, equal friendship. Jackson uses it ironically to expose the gap between outward politeness and genuine respect. Mrs Wilson believes she is being kind, but her ‘kindness’ is built on racist stereotypes, and her withdrawal of the gingerbread exposes the ill-manners beneath her charity. The quiet, domestic setting shows that everyday prejudice can do harm without any open violence. The boys have not yet absorbed adult bias, which suggests prejudice is learned rather than natural. Links to other stories : ‘Flower Garden’ shows the same racism hardening into a whole town’s exclusion; the innocence of children contrasts with ‘The Witch’ and ‘The Lottery’, where children absorb cruelty readily.

‘Flower Garden’

Summary : Young Mrs Winning has married into the town’s oldest family and lives in the old Winning house under her mother-in-law’s control; after eleven years the two women have begun to look alike, and she is ‘officially a Winning’. She once dreamed of restoring a small cottage nearby. When Mrs MacLane, a widow from the city, moves into that cottage with her son Davey, Mrs Winning befriends her, and their sons play together. Mrs MacLane transforms the cottage and garden into exactly what Mrs Winning had imagined. When Mrs MacLane makes Davey apologise to Billy Jones, a boy of mixed race whom Howard Winning has insulted, and then hires Billy’s father, Mr Jones, to work her garden, the town turns against her. Mrs Winning’s mother-in-law, Mrs Harris and other neighbours pressure her to speak to Mrs MacLane, and Davey is left out of a birthday party. Mrs Winning withdraws and laughs at her friend in public. As the town’s coldness grows, the garden begins to fail. After a storm brings a neighbour’s tree down across it, Mrs MacLane wonders aloud whether she should give up and go back to the city, and Mrs Winning, watching, will not greet her. Analysis : The townspeople act as gatekeepers, advising Mrs Winning to speak to Mrs MacLane ‘about that colored man’. Mrs Winning’s own fear shows how the pressure works: ‘pretty soon they’ll stop coming to me first, they’ll tell someone else to speak to me.’ When Mrs MacLane asks whether it is ‘because of Mr. Jones working here’, Mrs Winning denies it, then privately thinks, ‘The nerve of her, trying to blame the colored folks.’ Racism here is not one person’s attitude, as in ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’, but a whole community’s, and it drives a family out. Mrs Winning is a disturbing protagonist: her own life is constrained and loveless, and her envy of Mrs MacLane’s freedom, warmth and garden turns into cruelty. The cottage symbolises the life she gave up; her own dutiful nasturtiums contrast with Mrs MacLane’s flourishing garden, which wilts as the town’s hostility grows. Children reveal the town’s values: Howard’s racist name-calling towards Billy Jones shows how early prejudice is learned. The ending shows conformity and tradition winning over friendship. Links to other stories : The pressure of tradition and the community against an outsider anticipate ‘The Lottery’; envy of another woman’s life echoes ‘The Villager’.

‘Elizabeth’

Summary : Elizabeth Style wakes from a dream of a warm, beautiful place to a grey, rainy New York morning. She has worked for nearly eleven years at a struggling literary agency with Robert Shax, who is both her business partner and her lover, though the relationship has lost its warmth. At the drugstore, Tommy, a young clerk, admits he has sent his play to another agency. At the office she finds that Robert has hired a pretty young assistant, Daphne Hill, without telling her. Over lunch she bitterly reminds Robert of Jim Harris, the writer whose signed photograph hangs on his wall and who left the agency after a quarrel. She turns down her visiting uncle, phones Jim Harris and talks him into coming to her apartment for a drink that evening, then dismisses Daphne, pretending it was Robert’s wish. At home she buys drinks and crackers and waits, imagining a new apartment and a new life. Analysis : Elizabeth’s story is one of insecurity and quiet despair beneath a professional surface. Her small cruelties (to Tommy, to Daphne, to Robert) are signs of a deep unhappiness she will not face. Daphne, young, attractive and untroubled, is a foil who reminds Elizabeth of everything she has lost, and Elizabeth’s sneer about whether Robert means to ‘beautify the office’ gives away her fear. Her name may allude to Elizabeth Style, a woman accused of witchcraft in seventeenth-century England. Harris is real, but his role in her future exists only in her imagination; Elizabeth is both a victim of the indifferent city and the antagonist of her own life, clinging to fantasy instead of changing anything. Like the protagonists of ‘The Daemon Lover’ and ‘The Villager’, she pins her hopes on a man who may never deliver. Links to other stories : Compare the women swallowed by the city in ‘The Tooth’ and ‘The Daemon Lover’.

‘The Tooth’

Summary : Clara Spencer’s husband sees her onto an overnight bus to New York, where she will see a dentist about a painful tooth. Dazed by codeine, whisky and lack of sleep, she meets a man who says his name is Jim. He sits with her, lets her rest her head on his shoulder, and speaks dreamily of a faraway place where ‘The sand is so white it looks like snow’. In the city he reappears, holds out a handful of pearls as he passes. The dentist sends her to a surgeon to have the tooth pulled, and under the anaesthetic her tooth feels like ‘the only part of her to have any identity’. Afterwards, in a washroom full of women, she cannot pick out her own reflection, thinks ‘you’d think my own face would know me’, and has to find her name on her belongings. She drops her barrette and a pin bearing the letter C into an ashstand. At the end she runs barefoot through what she imagines is hot sand, hand in hand with Jim, while passers-by stare. Analysis : Jim represents Clara’s desire to escape her ordinary, grey life. His poetic visions (‘the waves ringing on the shore like bells’, ‘the grass is so green and so soft’) evoke a mythical place far from bus stations and dentists’ rooms. He appears when she is drowsiest and vanishes while she is with the medical staff, so the reader can never be sure he is real; he may be a symptom of her breakdown rather than its cause. Her name, engraved on a barrette and a pin, is a symbol of identity, and abandoning it marks the loss of her self. Clara’s name means ‘clear’ or ‘bright’, ironic for a woman whose identity has faded. The whole collapse happens within a day, which makes it more dramatic. Unusually, the ending suggests escape from social expectations rather than defeat, though whether it is liberation or madness is left open. Links to other stories : Jim’s blue suit and promises echo Jamie in ‘The Daemon Lover’; the dream of a warm place echoes the opening and ending of ‘Elizabeth’.

‘Of Course’

Summary : Mrs Tylor, eager for a neighbour with a child her daughter Carol’s age, watches a new family move in next door and decides from their furniture and clothes that they are ‘nice people, obviously’. She invites Mrs Harris and her son James in to rest from the move. In the garden, Mrs Tylor’s suggestions keep failing: James does not go to movies, Mr Harris does not allow radio or newspapers, and he has gone to his mother’s to avoid the stress of moving. Mrs Harris explains that her husband believes movies are ‘intellectually retarding’, and even bridge is out. Mrs Tylor keeps answering ‘Of course’. As soon as the Harrises go home, Carol asks to go to the movies, and Mrs Tylor says, ‘I’ll go with you, dear.’ Analysis : The story turns on the pressure of neighbourliness. Mrs Tylor’s first judgements are based on appearances; Mrs Harris then projects her husband’s rigid views through friendly conversation (‘it means such a lot to have really thoughtful neighbors’). Mr Harris never appears, yet he controls his family completely. The title is ironic: Mrs Tylor’s repeated ‘Of course’ is politeness, not agreement, and her quiet trip to the movies at the end is her only act of resistance. Social conformity and the appearance of politeness are maintained, despite the intolerance beneath them. Links to other stories : Polite surfaces hiding judgement also shape ‘After You, My Dear Alphonse’ and ‘Flower Garden’.

‘The Lottery’

Summary : On the morning of June 27th, the villagers of a small farming community of about three hundred people gather in the square for their annual lottery. Children arrive first, and the boys fill their pockets with stones. Mr Summers runs the drawing from a battered black box, older than anyone can remember, assisted by Mr Graves. Slips of paper long ago replaced the original wood chips. Tessie Hutchinson arrives late, joking that she forgot what day it was. Heads of families draw slips in turn. Mr Adams mentions that a nearby village is talking of giving up the lottery, and Old Man Warner dismisses them as a ‘Pack of crazy fools’. Bill Hutchinson draws the marked slip. Tessie protests that Bill was not given enough time to choose and that it ‘wasn’t fair’. In a second drawing among the five Hutchinsons, Tessie draws the paper with the black spot. The villagers, including her friends and children (little Davy is handed pebbles), stone her as she screams, ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right.’ Analysis : ‘The Lottery’ is the anchor story of the collection: it shows every key idea. The pleasant setting (‘clear and sunny’) and friendly community (‘Take your time, son’) make the violence more shocking, and the title itself is ironic, since this lottery’s ‘winner’ dies. The ritual is maintained by authority figures (Mr Summers, Mr Graves, whose name hints at death), defended by Old Man Warner (‘There’s always been a lottery’), who warns that otherwise ‘they’ll be wanting to go back to living in caves’, and carried out by ordinary people. The old saying ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’ points to the ritual’s origin in sacrifice for a good harvest, a purpose no one now examines. Mr Adams mentions villages that have given up the lottery, yet still joins in. Tessie is no rebel: she accepts the lottery until it threatens her. The detail that ‘although the villagers had forgotten the ritual and lost the original black box, they still remembered to use stones’ suggests that the violence outlives any meaning the tradition once had. Read as an allegory, the story asks which of our own traditions and scapegoats we accept without question. Links to other stories : Every story above contributes a piece of ‘The Lottery’: the hostile town of ‘The Renegade’, the conformity of ‘Flower Garden’, the children who learn cruelty in ‘The Witch’.
