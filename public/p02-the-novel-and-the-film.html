The Novel and the Film

On this page
  • What Happens Differently in the Novel
  • The Missing Final Chapter
  • How to Stay on the Film

Your set text is Peter Weir’s 1975 film, not Joan Lindsay’s 1967 novel. The film follows the novel closely, but the novel contains events and explanations that never appear on screen. Anything that exists only in the novel cannot be used as evidence in your essay, and using it tells the assessor you are not writing about the film.

What Happens Differently in the Novel

  • Sara’s death. In the novel, Sara is found days later beneath the school’s tower, apparently a suicide. In the film, the head gardener finds her body in the greenhouse; how she died is never shown.

  • Mrs Appleyard’s death. In the novel, she jumps from a peak on the Rock. In the film, a closing voice-over reports that her body was found at the base of the Rock and that ‘it is believed she fell while attempting to climb the rock’.

  • Miss Lumley. In the novel, she leaves with her brother Reg, and both die in a hotel fire. In the film, she simply resigns.

  • Mademoiselle. In the novel, she announces she is getting married and leaves the college. In the film, she is still there at the end, dining with Mrs Appleyard.

  • Miss McCraw. In the novel, a frill of calico thought to be from her dress is found in 1903. The film gives no such clue.

The Missing Final Chapter

Lindsay cut the novel’s final chapter before publication. It was published after her death, in 1987, as The Secret of Hanging Rock. In it, the girls and a woman who seems to be Miss McCraw throw off their corsets and pass through a ‘hole in space’ in the Rock, while Irma is shut out when the hanging boulder tilts. This chapter has nothing to do with the film. Weir deliberately leaves the disappearance unexplained, and that refusal to explain is one of the most important things to analyse. Never use the missing chapter as an ‘answer’.

How to Stay on the Film

  • Name the director, not the author: ‘Weir suggests’, not ‘Lindsay suggests’.

  • Use film evidence: shots, framing, sound, music, costume, setting and editing, as well as dialogue.

  • If you have read the novel, check every detail you plan to use against the film. If you cannot point to the moment on screen, leave it out.

  • Use the novel only as context, and only in passing: for example, that Lindsay blurred fact and fiction, and that Weir keeps the mystery unresolved.
