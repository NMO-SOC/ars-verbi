Moving Up the Mark Bands

Use this as a checklist on your draft. Each band move is a few things you can actually change. The wording of the bands comes from the Section A descriptors on the Exam Assessment Criteria page.
Before you start, check every body paragraph has:

  • a topic sentence that states a theme, not a character;

  • at least one quotation from the text;

  • one technique (narration, setting, sound, a repeated image) and what it does;

  • a last sentence that links back to your contention.

From 6 to 7

  1. Use the text in every body paragraph. Count the quotations in each paragraph. If one has none, find a line that shows the idea you are making.

  2. Name one technique in each paragraph and say what it does. Replace “Jackson uses a calm voice” with the effect: “the calm voice makes the violence sound ordinary”.

  3. Keep the argument going to the end of the paragraph. If the last sentence only retells the story, rewrite it to say what the story shows.

From 7 to 8

  1. Explain how the technique produces the idea. Use this order: technique, then effect on the reader, then the idea about the topic.

  2. Link every paragraph back to the contention in its last sentence. Your essay should read as one argument, not three separate points.

  3. Cut repetition. Read the essay aloud. If two sentences say the same thing, keep the more precise one.

Worked example, using the Lottery low sample, body paragraph 2.

  • Before (6): “Tessie is the only one who complains. She says ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’. The other villagers tell her to be a good sport. This shows people are afraid of being different.”

  • After (7–8): “Tessie is the only one who complains, and her ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’ is answered not with an argument but with ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’. That reply turns a question of fairness into a question of manners, which is how the village keeps dissent quiet. The pressure is social, not violent, and that is why conformity lasts.”

From 8 to 9 or 10

  1. Show one complication. Find a piece of evidence that works against your view. Answer it in one sentence, then say what your view still is or where it needs limiting.

  2. Use precise verbs for what the text does. Replace “shows” and “reveals” with the action itself: “silences”, “exposes”, “normalises”. Check each verb against the text.

  3. Check every claim. Each sentence should point to something a marker can find in the text. If a sentence is a general comment about life, cut it or tie it to the text.

Practice

Take the Picnic low sample and apply the 6-to-7 checklist to one paragraph. Then take the Picnic medium sample and apply the 8-to-9 checklist to the same paragraph. The difference between the two is the band move.
