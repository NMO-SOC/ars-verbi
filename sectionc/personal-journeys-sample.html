Sample Personal Journeys Piece: ‘The Spare Key’

Practice stimuli (written for this site, not from an exam): ‘Home is where you leave something behind.’; ‘Keys are the only things we carry from one life into the next.’
For eleven years the spare key to our old house hung on a nail beside the back door of the new one. Nobody told me to keep it. It was simply there one morning, a brass thing with a loose tooth, after Mum had unpacked the last box and I was too tired to ask.

We moved in March. I was nine. The new street had a footpath on one side only, and a dog behind the fence that barked at anyone holding an envelope. Mum said we would be settled by winter. Dad did not say much about it. He drove the van up the hill with the windows down, so the dust from the gravel road came in, and he kept one hand on the dashboard as if the house might slide away behind us.

I did not make friends quickly. At school I learned which corners of the oval were mine and which were not. In the evenings I walked the new streets with the key in my pocket and tried to think of the old house as a place I could still reach. It had a lemon tree that dropped fruit onto the tin roof of the shed. It had a hallway that echoed when you whistled. None of that was in the new house, and I kept waiting for it to appear.

It never did, but the key kept working in my head. Every time someone asked where I was from, I gave the old address, and every time I gave it, I felt slightly less like a stranger in the place I lived now.

I went back on a Saturday when I was twenty. The new owners had painted the front door green. I stood on the path with the key in my hand, and I tried it in the lock out of habit. It did not turn. The lock had been changed years before, and the key was a copy of something that no longer existed.

I put it back in my pocket and walked to the end of the street. A boy was kicking a ball against the fence of the house that used to be ours, and a woman in a dressing gown was watching from the porch. Nobody knew me there. I was glad, in a way, because it meant I could leave without explaining.

The key is still in my drawer. It does not fit anything. I look at it sometimes when I am packing for another move, and I think about how much of a home is just a set of directions you keep carrying after the door has changed.
Why this piece works

  • It answers both stimuli without naming them. The key is the object that connects the two ideas, and the journey runs from the old house to the new one and back again.

  • The voice stays plain. The sentences are short where the feeling is strong, and the details (the lemon tree, the dog, the green door) carry the emotion without explaining it.

  • The ending returns to the object. Revisiting the key gives the piece a shape, which the reader can feel without being told what it means.
