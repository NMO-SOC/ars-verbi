Section A Sample Essays: The Lottery (Low, Medium and High)

Topic: In Jackson’s ‘The Lottery’, the villagers conform because they are afraid of being excluded. To what extent do you agree?
Sample Essay I (Low)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson shows a village that follows a ritual without question. I agree that the villagers conform because they are afraid of being excluded. Jackson uses the crowd, the black box and Tessie Hutchinson to show this.
Body Paragraph 1

The villagers all come to the square on time. Mrs Graves and Mrs Delacroix talk and laugh while they wait. This shows the villagers are friendly, but they still follow the lottery because it is what they have always done. Old Man Warner says ‘There’s always been a lottery’, which shows they will not change.
Body Paragraph 2

Tessie is the only one who complains. She says ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’. The other villagers tell her to be a good sport. This shows people are afraid of being different, so they make her quiet.
Body Paragraph 3

At the end, the villagers pick up stones. Old Man Warner tells everyone to come on. This shows the crowd is scared and wants to join in so they are not left out.
Conclusion

Jackson shows the villagers conform because they are afraid. So I agree with the statement.
Sample Essay II (Medium)

Introduction

In ‘The Lottery’, Shirley Jackson presents a village whose members gather each June to take part in a ritual none of them questions. I agree that the villagers conform out of fear of being excluded, though the story suggests the fear is learned rather than simply felt. Jackson builds the pressure through the crowd’s manners, the power of tradition and the way Tessie is isolated.
Body Paragraph 1

The villagers do not discuss the lottery; they simply attend it. Old Man Warner dismisses the talk of change as ‘Pack of crazy fools’, and insists ‘There’s always been a lottery.’ His certainty shows that the ritual survives because nobody is willing to be the one who breaks it. The narration confirms this, noting that no one liked to upset ‘even as much tradition as was represented by the black box’.
Body Paragraph 2

Tessie’s protest is what exposes the fear. When she cries ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’, her neighbours do not argue the point. Mrs Delacroix calls ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’, and Mrs Graves reminds her that ‘All of us took the same chance.’ The phrase is a warning: conformity is presented as fairness, and dissent is treated as poor manners.
Body Paragraph 3

The stoning shows what exclusion finally looks like. Once Tessie is singled out, the crowd moves towards her, and Old Man Warner’s ‘Come on, come on, everyone’ pushes the reluctant villagers into the action. The fear of being left out is greater than any sympathy for Tessie, and the villagers join in rather than stand apart.
Conclusion

Jackson shows that the villagers conform because exclusion is worse than the violence they must commit. The ritual is kept alive by people who would rather be part of the crowd than face being the outsider. The fear is real, so even if it is never spoken, it shapes every choice the villagers make.
Sample Essay III (High)

Introduction

Shirley Jackson opens ‘The Lottery’ with a morning that is ‘clear and sunny’, and the pleasantness is the first thing the story asks us to distrust. I agree that the villagers conform because they fear exclusion, but Jackson’s argument is more unsettling than that. The villagers are not forced. They have learned to read their own belonging as something they must keep up, and the fear of being separated from the crowd does the rest.
Body Paragraph 1

The story frames the ritual as ordinary. The children assemble first, ‘of course’, and the women talk among themselves while they wait. Mrs Hutchinson arrives late, having ‘clean forgot what day it was’, which suggests the lottery is so routine that it can be forgotten and still be attended. Conformity starts before anybody is asked to conform, because the day has already been made normal.
Body Paragraph 2

Old Man Warner shows how conformity is passed down. He calls the north village’s doubts ‘Pack of crazy fools’, then offers the proverb ‘Lottery in June, corn be heavy soon’. He is not defending the lottery on grounds of safety; he links it to survival, so anyone who questions it threatens the community’s shared sense of its own security. The narration’s aside that no one liked to upset ‘even as much tradition as was represented by the black box’ shows that the tradition has outlasted any reason for it.
Body Paragraph 3

Tessie’s protest is what exposes the mechanism. Her ‘It isn’t fair, it isn’t right’ is met with ‘Be a good sport, Tessie’, and then with Mrs Graves’s ‘All of us took the same chance.’ That reply turns fairness into a matter of who is willing to play along. The crowd’s first move against her is verbal before it is physical. By the time the stones fly, Tessie has already been reduced to someone who must be silenced so that everyone else can feel safe in their place.
Conclusion

Jackson’s village conforms because each person has been taught that the group’s approval is the only safety available. The black box, the proverbs and the good-sport rhetoric all teach that the outsider is the real danger. I agree with the statement, though the story suggests that fear of exclusion is not merely a motive. It is the mechanism that keeps the lottery alive.
