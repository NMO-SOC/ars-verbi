Children as a Motif

Children appear in many of Jackson’s stories, and they are rarely innocent observers. They copy the adults around them and show what those adults have taught them is normal. Read them as a motif: a figure that recurs across the collection, each time revealing something about the world it belongs to.

In ‘The Lottery’, the children assemble first, ‘of course’, as though the ritual were something they have always been expected to attend. Little Davy Hutchinson is handed pebbles by ‘someone’, so the violence is passed to the young by the adults around them. The children are not exceptions to the village’s behaviour; they are its clearest demonstration.

In ‘The Renegade’, Mrs Walpole’s children delight in imagining how a spiked collar would ‘cut her head off’. Their cruelty is not a sign of evil. It shows what they have absorbed from neighbours who offer increasingly violent advice, and it shows how violence becomes a game when it is part of ordinary conversation.

On the train in ‘Like Mother Used to Make’, a boy asks ‘Did you cut her all in pieces?’ with breathless excitement, while a baby is ‘busy with her rattle and her toast’. The contrast between innocent detail and a violent question makes the boy’s fascination more unsettling. The narrator does not tell the reader to be shocked; the juxtaposition does that work.

Taken together, the children show that cruelty in Jackson’s stories is learned and repeated. Their presence reminds the reader that the violence in these stories is not confined to one generation, and that it is passed on in the most ordinary settings.
