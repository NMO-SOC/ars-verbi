Section A Sample Essays: Picnic at Hanging Rock (Low, Medium and High)

Topic: In Picnic at Hanging Rock, social conventions restrict individual freedom. Discuss.
Sample Essay I (Low)

Introduction

In Picnic at Hanging Rock, Peter Weir shows that the rules of society stop people from being free. The girls at Appleyard College have to follow lots of rules, Sara is punished by the school and Albert and Michael are from different classes. Weir uses costume, characters and dialogue to show this.

Body Paragraph 1

At the start of the film the girls get dressed for the picnic in stockings and long dresses. Mrs Appleyard tells them ‘You may remove your gloves once the drag has passed through Woodend.’ This shows the school has a lot of rules about clothes. At the Rock some of the girls take off their shoes and stockings, but Edith keeps hers on. This shows that the rules are very strict. So the girls are not free while they follow the rules.

Body Paragraph 2

Sara is treated badly by the school. She is not allowed to go on the picnic and Miss Lumley straps her to the wall, saying it is ‘for her own good’. Mrs Appleyard says ‘You will be returned to the orphanage’ because her fees are not paid. This shows that Sara is not free. So the school’s rules hurt Sara the most.

Body Paragraph 3

Albert and Michael are from different classes. Albert says ‘I say the crude things. You just think ‘em.’ This shows Michael is posh and Albert is not. Michael cannot say what he thinks because of his class. So class also stops people being free.

Conclusion

Weir shows that social rules stop people from being free. The girls, Sara and Michael all show this. Therefore social conventions restrict individual freedom in the film.

Sample Essay II (Medium)

Introduction

In Picnic at Hanging Rock, Peter Weir presents a colonial society held together by rules: rules of dress, of conduct and of class. These conventions restrict the freedom of almost every character, but they fall hardest on those with the least power. Weir explores this through the costumes and rules of Appleyard College, the treatment of Sara Waybourne and the friendship between Michael and Albert.

Body Paragraph 1

Weir uses costume to show how convention confines the girls’ bodies. The film opens with the girls dressing for the picnic, pulling on stockings and tying ribbons, and Mrs Appleyard’s instruction that they ‘may remove [their] gloves once the drag has passed through Woodend’ shows that even the smallest freedom must be granted by authority. At the Rock, Miranda, Irma and Marion take off their shoes and stockings, while Edith keeps her hat, gloves, shoes and stockings on. The contrast suggests that freedom begins only where the college’s rules are left behind. Convention, then, is something the girls wear, and shedding it is the first step towards the Rock.

Body Paragraph 2

The cost of these conventions is clearest in Sara Waybourne. Sara is forbidden from the picnic, and Miss Lumley straps her to the gymnasium wall, insisting it is ‘for her own good’ to cure her ‘terrible stooping’. When the college faces financial pressure, Mrs Appleyard names ‘Sara Waybourne, for instance’ among the overdue fees and tells her ‘You will be returned to the orphanage.’ Because Sara is an orphan without money, the rules that merely inconvenience the other girls become a punishment for her. Weir shows that convention restricts freedom most severely when it is joined to class and wealth.

Body Paragraph 3

Class convention also limits the men of the film. Michael, a young English aristocrat, is expected to behave politely, while Albert, the Fitzhuberts’ groom, speaks freely. Albert’s remark, ‘I say the crude things. You just think ‘em,’ suggests that Michael’s manners do not remove his thoughts, only his ability to say them. Yet Michael does break away from expectation by searching for the girls himself. Weir suggests that convention restricts freedom of expression, even if it cannot entirely control action.

Conclusion

Weir presents social conventions as a constant limit on individual freedom in Picnic at Hanging Rock. The girls are confined by dress and rules, Sara is crushed by them, and Michael is silenced by the manners of his class. The film suggests that these restrictions are not equal: the less power a character has, the more tightly convention binds them.

Sample Essay III (High)

Introduction

Picnic at Hanging Rock begins inside the conventions it will question. Before the picnic, Weir shows the girls dressing one another, pulling on stockings and tying ribbons, an image of freedom given up willingly, even tenderly. Social conventions certainly restrict individual freedom in the film, but Weir is more interested in who they restrict and what happens when they fail. They confine the privileged girls lightly, crush the powerless Sara and silence even the gentlemen who benefit from them. When the Rock breaks those conventions, it offers not freedom but disappearance.

Body Paragraph 1

Weir presents the college’s conventions as a garment the girls are trained to wear. The stockings, ribbons and gloves of the opening are matched by Mrs Appleyard’s carefully rationed liberty: the girls ‘may remove [their] gloves once the drag has passed through Woodend’. Freedom here is a privilege granted at a fixed point on the map. At the Rock, Miranda, Irma and Marion remove their shoes and stockings, while Edith keeps her hat, gloves, shoes and stockings on and runs screaming. When Irma is found, the detail that her corset is missing becomes a scandal in itself, as if the loss of convention were as alarming as the loss of the girls. The contrast is not simply between restraint and release. The girls who abandon convention vanish, and the girl who clings to it survives. Weir allows convention to look like protection as much as confinement, and that ambivalence keeps the film from becoming a simple story of liberation.

Body Paragraph 2

If convention protects anyone, it is not Sara Waybourne. Weir uses Sara to expose the class and money beneath the college’s rules. She is forbidden from the picnic, strapped to the gymnasium wall ‘for her own good’ to cure her ‘terrible stooping’, and finally told ‘You will be returned to the orphanage.’ Mrs Appleyard’s list of ‘tuition fees impossibly overdue’ names ‘Sara Waybourne, for instance’, reducing her to an unpaid account. Sara’s own repetition of the phrase ‘This is not a charitable institution’ shows how fully she has absorbed the college’s judgement of her. For the wealthy girls, convention is a garment. For Sara, it is a sentence. Weir suggests that social rules restrict freedom in proportion to how little power a person has.

Body Paragraph 3

Even those who benefit from convention are diminished by it. Michael Fitzhubert, a young English aristocrat, can barely speak about the girls he has watched, while Albert, his uncle’s groom, is blunt: ‘I say the crude things. You just think ‘em.’ Albert’s line reverses the expected hierarchy, granting the working-class man a freedom of speech the gentleman lacks. Michael’s obsessive search for the girls is the one moment he acts against expectation, and it nearly destroys him. Weir implies that convention does not remove desire. It only drives it underground, where it becomes obsession.

Conclusion

Weir’s film agrees that social conventions restrict individual freedom, but it refuses to celebrate their collapse. The dress and rules of Appleyard College confine the girls, the college’s finances destroy Sara, and the manners of class silence Michael. When the Rock dissolves those conventions, what replaces them is not freedom but mystery and loss. Picnic at Hanging Rock leaves its audience with an uncomfortable recognition: the rules that restrict us may also be the only thing holding the world in place.
